# Make Help Findable in Learner Language

## Pattern Rule
**IF** learners must search for guidance before they know the field's expert terminology
**THEN** let their informal descriptions of symptoms, intentions, and stuck states retrieve the relevant trusted explanation and examples

## Do
- Test retrieval language against queries from actual novices rather than asking experts to guess how beginners describe a problem.
- Index everyday state words such as “flow” and “unstuck” alongside the formal concepts that answer those concerns.
- Send an informal query to clear guidance and concrete examples, not merely to a glossary entry that repeats unfamiliar terminology.
- Preserve the learner's original wording as a search route even after the response introduces more precise vocabulary.

## Don't
- Do not make knowledge of the answer's official name a prerequisite for finding the answer.
- Do not treat novice wording as noise just because it describes an effect rather than its technical cause.
- Do not improve query matching by routing learners to unreviewed or unreliable content.

## Checklist
- Can a learner search by the experienced problem rather than its formal label?
- Do common novice phrases return a relevant explanation and usable examples?
- Were the search terms checked against language learners actually use?
- Does the result connect informal wording with trustworthy craft guidance?

## Notes
Writer's Help 2.0 describes a search system developed from research with more than 1,600 student writers. Its revealing examples are not technical terms but “flow” and “unstuck”: words that report a learner's experience before the learner can diagnose it. The durable teaching decision is to make help retrieval bridge that vocabulary gap while keeping the destination authoritative.
