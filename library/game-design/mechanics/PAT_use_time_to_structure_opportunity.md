# Use Time to Structure Opportunity

## Pattern Rule
**IF** fictional time can change which actions, projects, opportunities, or consequences remain available
**THEN** choose a decision window appropriate to the activity, determine what normally fits inside that window, and make elapsed time explicit when competing activities, deadlines, recovery, training, order, or changing world state alter player choices
**ELSE** abstract the passage of time to the next meaningful decision or state change rather than tracking empty intervals for their own sake.

## Do
- Treat a round, several hours, several days, or a longer calendar interval as different scales of the same design question: what can participants accomplish before the current opportunity window changes or closes?
- Use time as capacity when activities compete for a limited window; make players choose what to finish, postpone, rush, delegate, combine, or abandon.
- Let task duration matter when surgery, repair, research, crafting, travel, training, healing, or another extended activity competes with other valuable uses of the same time.
- Use deadlines and schedules as incentives as well as constraints: acting sooner, waiting, or missing a window can change rewards, access, risk, or future opportunities.
- Let world processes create temporal windows when appropriate, such as departures, seasons, patrols, tides, lunar phases, recovery periods, or other events that proceed independently of the player characters.
- When a player tries to fit more work into less time, first use the game's existing difficulty, assistance, consequence, quality, or scope grammar before inventing a special rushed-action subsystem.
- Preserve recognizable real-world units when they communicate the cost cleanly; "four hours" or "three days" can be mechanically meaningful without requiring hour-by-hour procedure.

## Don't
- Treat time as meaningful merely because the fiction can be measured in seconds, hours, days, or months.
- Build detailed clocks for intervals in which nothing competes, changes, or creates a decision.
- Assume every time scale needs the same degree of procedure; combat may need exact ordering while travel or downtime can remain abstract.
- Make a deadline a disguised hard failure when the game could instead support rushing, reduced scope, assistance, alternative plans, or accepting a later consequence.
- Invent bespoke time penalties when the existing resolution grammar already expresses increased difficulty or reduced reliability.

## Checklist
- The current decision window and the activities competing inside it can be named.
- At least one player choice changes because available time is limited, scheduled, or valuable.
- Extended tasks state a recognizable duration or another clear way to compare their demand against available time when that comparison matters.
- Time pressure can be expressed through existing resolution grammar, reduced scope, added risk, or another deliberate consequence rather than an unexplained exception.
- At least one interval that carries no meaningful competition or consequence can be safely abstracted.
- Any scheduled world event changes access, value, risk, or state in a way the players can plan around rather than existing only as decorative calendar detail.

## Notes
Time is both a familiar fictional measure and a flexible game resource. Its smallest formal expression is often the combat round: participants have a bounded window and must decide what can fit inside it. The same structure scales upward. "What can I do this round?" and "What can I do in the next four hours?" ask the same underlying question at different resolutions. Time becomes especially useful as a pressure test because reducing the available window can expose whether repair, healing, crafting, movement, training, assistance, difficulty, and other mechanics integrate cleanly. The design goal is not to maximize timekeeping; it is to make opportunity legible when elapsed time changes what players can do.

**Temporal priority / initiative.** Inside a small decision window, initiative determines the temporal priority of competing intentions. Different games may use random rolls, fixed attributes, weapon or action speed, side order, declared actions, simultaneous resolution, delays, interrupts, or multiple passes. The universal design question is not "who rolls initiative?" but what priority grants: earlier resolution, later choice, information advantage, reaction rights, extra actions, or another timing benefit.

**Task duration.** An action may consume minutes, hours, days, or longer without requiring a separate time subsystem. Duration becomes mechanical when it competes with another activity, deadline, resource, recovery window, or opportunity.

**Training and advancement.** Learning or improving a capability can consume campaign time as well as experience, money, instructors, or facilities. This converts advancement from an instantaneous purchase into an opportunity-cost decision when other activities compete for the same interval.
