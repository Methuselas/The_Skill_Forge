# Match Practiced Behavior to the Intended Outcome

## Pattern Rule
**IF** a game is meant to teach, train, simulate, rehabilitate, assess, or otherwise produce an outcome beyond the play session
**THEN** define the target capability as behavior and make the game’s recurring player actions exercise that behavior
**ELSE** evaluate recurring actions against the intended play experience itself.

## Do
- State the target as something a player does or improves at, such as adapting to changing conditions, following a precise procedure, negotiating, coordinating, or making risk decisions.
- Inspect the actions players repeat most often rather than assuming the game’s subject matter proves what it teaches.
- Use uncertainty when adaptation is part of the target; reduce or remove it when exact procedural repetition is the capability being trained.
- When engagement and an external outcome both matter, test them separately and check whether the fun or reward loop reinforces the target behavior.
- Inspect what earns experience, development points, advancement checks, wealth, status, or other rewards; repeated rewards teach players which behaviors the game considers valuable even when the text claims broader priorities.

## Don't
- Confuse “a game about a topic” with “a game that practices a capability related to that topic.”
- Reward shortcuts or behaviors that contradict the skill the game is supposed to cultivate.
- Assume entertainment and instrumental purpose are mutually exclusive design categories.

## Checklist
- The intended outcome is phrased as an observable capability or behavior.
- The game makes players perform that behavior repeatedly enough to practice it.
- Randomness, cooperation, competition, and feedback are compatible with the target capability.
- Experiential success and instrumental success can be evaluated independently.
- Reward triggers have been compared against the actions the game actually intends players to repeat, including obvious ways those triggers could be farmed or optimized.

## Notes
Applied games succeed only when their procedures create the kind of practice the external goal requires. The same design can also pursue enjoyment. The strongest alignment occurs when the behaviors that make play engaging are the same behaviors the game intends to exercise. This also applies to ordinary advancement economies: what earns growth is a behavioral signal, while what players may purchase with that growth is a separate development decision.
