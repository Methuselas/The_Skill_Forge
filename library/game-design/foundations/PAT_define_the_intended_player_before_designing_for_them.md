# Define the Intended Player Before Designing for Them

## Pattern Rule
**IF** a game or major subsystem is being framed or revised
**THEN** identify the players the design expects to serve and use that audience to constrain complexity, assumed knowledge, communication, and the experience the game asks them to sustain
**ELSE** when the audience is intentionally broad or still unknown, minimize hidden assumptions and expose them explicitly for testing.

## Do
- Describe the audience in terms that affect design: age or experience range, familiarity with the rules tradition, tolerance for reference work, and what kind of play they are seeking.
- Let audience assumptions influence terminology, explanation depth, rules density, onboarding, and the amount of effort required before play becomes rewarding.
- Treat character-system burden as part of that contract: creation time, number of choices, persistent sheet information, resource tracking, and maintenance should match what the intended players are willing to manage.
- Treat character creation as mechanical onboarding: when practical, expose the vocabulary, resolution concepts, and kinds of choices that will recur during play so the first substantial rules experience truthfully previews the game that follows.
- Revisit the audience definition when the game changes direction; a design can outgrow the players it was originally framed for.
- Recruit playtesters who resemble the intended audience but vary enough in experience, play style, and familiarity to expose assumptions learned by a single regular group.
- Distinguish audience mismatch from design failure: feedback from someone outside the intended experience can still be informative, but it does not by itself show that the game failed its actual audience.
- Carry the intended-player definition into release communication: it should be specific enough to identify where those players are likely to encounter or seek this kind of game and what parts of the experience matter to them.
- Keep intended audience, reached audience, and actual players distinct; promotion can expose the game to people it was never primarily designed to serve.

## Don't
- Treat “everyone” as a useful audience definition when the rules assume specialist knowledge or a particular tolerance for complexity.
- Infer that a popular genre automatically supplies a single homogeneous audience.
- Optimize only for the designer’s own fluency and then assume unfamiliar players will fill in the same gaps.
- Teach an elaborate creation procedure or terminology that disappears once play begins unless that one-time complexity is itself part of the intended experience.
- Treat the designer's home group as sufficient representation of the intended audience merely because they already know, understand, or enjoy the game.
- Redesign toward every negative preference report without first asking whether the tester wanted the experience the game was actually built to provide.
- Treat a broad promotional reach as evidence that every reached person belongs to the game's intended audience.

## Checklist
- A specific intended player population can be described without naming only a genre.
- The rule complexity and explanation style are plausible for those players.
- Required prior knowledge is either taught, signposted, or deliberately excluded from the target audience.
- Playtests include people who resemble the intended audience rather than only the design team.
- Character creation and ongoing character administration have been timed or observed with people from the intended audience, not judged only by designers already fluent in the system.
- A new player can identify at least some recurring mechanical vocabulary or interactions during character creation that later appear in actual play.
- Completed-game playtests include intended-audience testers who did not learn the game only through the designer's regular table.
- Negative feedback can be classified as an audience-fit issue or a design-performance issue rather than treating the two as interchangeable.
- A concise description can explain who the game is for and what experience it offers without requiring the entire rulebook as context.

## Notes
Audience is a design constraint, not merely a marketing label. The same mechanical idea may need different presentation, onboarding, or complexity when aimed at children, veteran hobbyists, casual players, or specialists. Keeping the intended player visible throughout development prevents the designer’s own familiarity from becoming an invisible prerequisite. Character expressiveness and character administration are separate costs: a large menu of meaningful creation choices may be welcome even when the same audience does not want a large amount of persistent state to manage during play. Character creation is also often the player's first sustained encounter with the rules, so it should normally introduce the mechanical language and expectations the player is about to use rather than presenting a misleadingly separate experience. Playtesting should preserve the same audience discipline: the designer's regular group is valuable evidence but is not the audience in miniature. Testing with other people who still fit the intended player profile reveals assumptions one familiar table has learned to compensate for. Conversely, a tester disliking the promised experience is not automatically evidence that the game failed; audience mismatch and design failure must be diagnosed separately.
 The same audience definition should remain useful downstream: when the game is released publicly, it should help the creator identify likely players, choose where to communicate with them, and describe the promised experience in language meaningful to them. Intended audience, reached audience, and actual players remain different populations; promotion does not retroactively redefine who the design was built to serve.
