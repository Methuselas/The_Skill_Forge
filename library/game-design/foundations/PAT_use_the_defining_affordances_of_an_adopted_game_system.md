# Use the Defining Affordances of an Adopted Game System

## Pattern Rule
**IF** a design adopts an established game system, framework, genre tradition, or recognizable design family
**THEN** identify the features that create its distinctive play experience and give those features meaningful work in the new design
**ELSE** reconsider whether the adopted foundation is actually the right one.

## Do
- Play or closely study representative implementations to learn expectations that may live in play culture rather than in explicit rules text.
- Identify mechanics, pacing habits, resource pressures, character capabilities, or presentation conventions that users rely on when recognizing the system or tradition.
- Build situations that let the adopted system’s signature tools matter rather than merely preserving compatibility on paper.
- Preserve the expected experience while still changing content, setting, or emphasis where the design calls for it.
- Treat an established system as an installed play architecture: player knowledge, character data, published material, table procedures, house practices, and expectations may all depend on frequently used mechanics.
- Before replacing a high-frequency dependency, inventory the migration it imposes and test whether the benefit justifies converting or relearning existing material.
- Evaluate compatibility at multiple levels: whether old data remains readable, procedures remain executable, capabilities remain available, relative power remains comparable, and existing characters or content retain their play identity.
- Use the adopted system's established terminology when those terms carry rules meaning, workflow expectations, or recognition value for existing users.
- Follow relevant presentation and contributor conventions when they help users recognize and operate compatible material.
- Consult official style or contributor guidance when producing downstream material for an established system.

## Don't
- Claim compatibility while designing around or avoiding the foundation’s defining mechanics.
- Reduce a design tradition to surface aesthetics when its characteristic play comes from procedures and player expectations.
- Assume that reading a license or stat block is enough to understand how the system is experienced at the table or screen.
- Rename established terms casually when doing so adds interface friction without creating a meaningful design benefit.
- Treat play compatibility and publication or license compliance as the same question; both may matter, but they are distinct constraints.
- Assume that a newer, cleaner, more unified, or more fashionable mechanic is automatically a better fit for an established game.
- Treat the existence of a conversion formula as proof that compatibility has been preserved when converted characters, content, or table habits no longer behave comparably.

## Checklist
- The adopted system or tradition has a short list of defining play affordances.
- At least one recurring situation in the design activates each affordance that is supposed to remain important.
- Representative games have been observed in actual play or equivalent execution when feasible.
- Removing the adopted foundation would materially change the experience rather than only the terminology.
- Game-specific terminology and presentation conventions that carry user meaning are preserved or intentionally changed for a stated reason.
- Any replacement of a frequently used inherited mechanic names the demonstrated problem it solves and the migration cost it creates.
- At least one representative legacy character, rule interaction, or content unit has been tested through the proposed change for capability, power, and play-identity preservation rather than only numeric convertibility.

## Notes
Compatibility is not the same as fit. A design can obey every formal rule and still miss what users value about the framework. Studying representative play helps reveal conventions that are carried socially or procedurally, and using those affordances keeps an adaptation from becoming a generic design wearing borrowed labels. Established terminology can also function as part of the user interface: familiar terms let existing players transfer knowledge into new material. That design compatibility should be evaluated separately from legal, licensing, or publication compliance.

An established system also carries migration dependencies. Replacing a rare optional feature is not the same intervention as replacing the high-frequency procedure through which most actions are understood. The deeper and more frequently used the dependency, the more evidence a replacement needs because its cost can propagate into learned probabilities, character identity, published statistics, house practices, and accumulated table fluency. Conversion should therefore be judged by preserved behavior and identity, not only by whether old numbers can be translated into new numbers.
