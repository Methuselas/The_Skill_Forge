# Give Adversaries a Distinct Play Pattern

## Pattern Rule
**IF** an adversary is meant to feel meaningfully different from ordinary opposition
**THEN** give it a small number of traits, behaviors, or capabilities that change what players must notice, choose, or do while remaining coherent with what the adversary is and wants
**ELSE** reuse established adversary structures rather than adding novelty that never changes play.

## Do
- Start from what the adversary needs, wants, fears, hunts, protects, or avoids, then connect its unusual traits to those functions.
- Give the adversary at least one clear sensory, behavioral, or fictional tell that helps players form a memorable mental model of it.
- Prefer one or two consequential traits that change tactics, equipment choices, information needs, positioning, timing, or risk over a long list of low-impact abilities.
- Make unusual anatomy, powers, equipment, or behavior matter during actual interaction instead of existing only in description.
- Reuse, reskin, or recombine existing mechanical effects when they can create the intended play pattern without a new subsystem.
- Test whether players respond differently once they understand the adversary; learning how to deal with it can be part of the challenge.

## Don't
- Treat higher hit points, armor, damage, or bonuses as the only way to make one adversary different from another.
- Add a gimmick whose only result is extra arithmetic or another roll without changing a meaningful player decision.
- Give every monster a bespoke mini-game when the established resolution system can express the same threat.
- Write elaborate ecology or visual lore that never affects how the creature behaves, survives, attacks, escapes, or interacts with the party.

## Checklist
- The adversary has a recognizable need, motive, or behavioral logic that its capabilities support.
- At least one trait changes a meaningful player choice rather than merely increasing numerical output.
- The adversary's identity becomes visible during play within the amount of time it is likely to remain on stage.
- An informed party can respond differently from an uninformed or default approach.
- Any unique rule earns its complexity by producing a play pattern the existing rules cannot express cleanly.

## Notes
A distinctive adversary should change decisions more than it changes rules. A rusting touch, deceptive disguise, unusual defense, predatory behavior, or clever use of ordinary abilities can make a weak creature more memorable than a numerically stronger one. Distinctiveness and difficulty are separate: the goal is a recognizable pattern of interaction, not simply a larger stat block.
