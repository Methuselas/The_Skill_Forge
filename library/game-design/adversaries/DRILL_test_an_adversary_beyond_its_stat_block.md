# Test an Adversary Beyond Its Stat Block

## Practice Task
Take one adversary that is supposed to feel distinctive and test whether that identity appears through player decisions during actual or concretely simulated play.

## Target Skill
Verify that an adversary creates a recognizable decision pattern in actual play rather than relying on description, inflated statistics, or unused special rules.

## Setup
Choose one adversary with a defined role, need, or motive. Prepare a representative encounter in which the adversary can use its intended capabilities without requiring a special environment that exists only to make the test work.

## Instructions
1. State the adversary's basic need, motive, or behavioral logic and identify one sensory or behavioral tell that communicates what kind of threat it may be.
2. Identify the smallest trait, capability, or behavior that is supposed to make the adversary play differently from ordinary opposition.
3. Record the default approach you expect players to use before they understand the threat.
4. Run or concretely simulate the encounter using that default approach and record the decisions, costs, and outcome.
5. State at least one informed adaptation involving tactics, equipment, information, positioning, timing, or another meaningful choice.
6. Run or concretely simulate the encounter again using the adapted approach and record what changed.
7. Check whether the distinctive behavior can be resolved through the game's existing mechanical grammar; isolate any special rule that truly requires new logic.
8. Test a plausible near-miss such as simply increasing hit points, armor, damage, or bonuses and compare whether it creates the same decision pattern.
9. Keep, revise, simplify, or remove each distinctive trait based on the observed difference between the two executions.

## Success Check
- The encounter was actually run or concretely simulated twice, once with a default approach and once with an informed adaptation.
- The record names at least one player decision, cost, risk, information state, positioning choice, or other behavior that changed between the two executions.
- The adversary's motive or need is connected to at least one trait or behavior that appeared during play rather than only in descriptive text.
- A plausible near-miss based on raw numerical escalation was tested or directly compared, and the record explains why it did or did not reproduce the intended play pattern.
- Any special rule retained after the test includes a reason the established resolution grammar cannot express the same effect cleanly.
- The final keep, revise, simplify, or remove decision cites observed play evidence; “the monster seems cool” does not pass.

## Common Failures
- Evaluating the adversary only from its stat block or concept art.
- Raising hit points or damage until the encounter becomes difficult without asking whether player decisions changed.
- Adding several special abilities when only one can realistically appear during the adversary's time on stage.
- Assuming a clever tactic will matter without running the encounter against an adapted player response.
- Retaining a bespoke rule because it is flavorful even though the ordinary game engine produces the same result.

## Notes
A memorable adversary is more than a collection of numbers. This drill tests whether its identity survives contact with play: players should have something meaningful to notice, learn, or adapt to, and the adversary's distinctiveness should be visible through those decisions rather than through bookkeeping alone.
