# Attach Persistent Costs to Capability Gains

## Pattern Rule
**IF** a powerful capability is supposed to feel dangerous, compromising, dependent, or socially costly
**THEN** attach at least part of its cost to persistent state that continues to matter after acquisition—dependency, vulnerability, obligation, exposure, maintenance, time, lost capability elsewhere, or reduced autonomy
**ELSE** treat a one-time currency payment as sufficient when the intended experience does not require an ongoing tradeoff.

## Do
- Distinguish acquisition price from persistent cost. Ask what remains true because the character gained the capability.
- Make the cost legible in the fiction: a patron expects service, an implant creates a new failure mode, heavy protection impairs mobility, treatment creates downtime, or an enhancement erodes a valued social capability.
- Verify that the sacrificed domain is actually valuable to the build and campaign. A drawback that never activates is not balancing anything.
- Let scarcity sometimes be solved through costly relationships rather than simple denial; access obtained from a patron can create future obligations and scenarios.
- Use multiple constraint currencies when they create distinct decisions—money, access, time, social exposure, maintenance, bodily risk, or another independently meaningful bottleneck.
- When capability depends on connectivity, automation, remote access, or network integration, treat the resulting **attack surface and evidence trail** as possible persistent costs rather than pure convenience.
- Let superior capital sometimes buy **efficiency in a scarce capacity** (less bodily cost, less load, less maintenance, less exposure) rather than only a larger numerical effect; this creates long-term upgrade goals without requiring power inflation.
- Prefer **recurring, player-controlled escalation costs** when a capability is meant to stay dangerous in use: let the player push harder now in exchange for more fatigue, backlash, exposure, instability, or another consequence that rises with the chosen output.
- When advancement can reduce or externalize an ongoing cost, audit the whole progression path. If mastery removes sustaining burden, forensic exposure, recovery cost, or another original brake, either price the exemption by its full downstream leverage or introduce a replacement pressure so advancement does not simply erase the reason the cost existed.

## Don't
- Rename an optimization budget with thematic language and assume the theme now creates behavior.
- Price every drawback as if every build values the affected capability equally.
- Let a character keep the benefit of a refunded drawback after another purchase or campaign condition has made the drawback irrelevant.
- Make all costs one-time and then expect the upgrade to keep generating tension indefinitely.
- Add a strong recurring cost at low mastery, then let ordinary progression buy away every meaningful version of that cost without replacing the lost counterpressure.

## Checklist
- The capability has at least one cost that persists after the purchase when persistent compromise is part of the design goal.
- The affected domain changes real choices for the intended build and campaign.
- At least one cost can generate future play rather than only subtract currency.
- Dependencies and vulnerabilities are visible enough for players to plan around them.
- Removing the ongoing cost would materially change the behavior or identity produced by the option.
- If the player can voluntarily increase output, the recurring price has been tested at several output levels to confirm that greater leverage creates greater current risk rather than a flat surcharge.
- If later options can externalize or negate the persistent cost, the audit identifies what new constraint, scarcity, or exposure prevents mastery from turning the original tradeoff into a permanent free baseline.

## Notes
A tradeoff exists only while both sides remain valuable. Persistent costs are especially powerful when they leave something in the world: a creditor, employer, maintenance need, damaged relationship, surveillance exposure, supply dependency, attack surface, forensic trace, or new technological vulnerability. Capability can therefore entangle the character more deeply with the campaign instead of making advancement synonymous with independence. Recurring costs are especially effective when the player controls the escalation: a stronger immediate effect can be available without becoming free, because the character deliberately accepts more present danger to obtain it. Progression deserves a second audit whenever it buys exemptions from those pressures; mastery that removes every sustaining, exposure, or backlash cost can quietly convert an exceptional state into the new baseline.
