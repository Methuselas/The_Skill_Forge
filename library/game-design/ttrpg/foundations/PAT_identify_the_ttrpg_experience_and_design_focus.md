# Identify the TTRPG Experience and Design Focus

## Pattern Rule
**IF** beginning a tabletop role-playing game project before its scope is settled
**THEN** name the player experience you want to create and the design work you most want to carry.
**ELSE** make a deliberately small exploratory prototype, then revisit the choice after it reveals whether your energy goes to stories, worlds, characters, or rules.

## Do
- Describe the table experience in player-facing terms, such as collaborative mystery-solving, high-risk tactical combat, or character-driven drama.
- Notice which work repeatedly draws your attention when you play or read games: rule interactions, character options, world lore, or adventure situations.
- Let a rules-light route reduce mechanical authoring when the desired experience does not depend on detailed rules.
- Choose a genre whose emotional range, research burden, and recurring situations you can sustain through the project.

## Don't
- Treat a complete game as a requirement to master every design discipline at the same depth.
- Pick a genre or rules density just because it is common in other games.

## Checklist
- The intended player experience can be stated without naming a setting, franchise, or mechanic.
- The project names its primary design focus and the work it intentionally postpones.
- The proposed rules weight supports the stated experience rather than the designer's anxiety about completeness.

## Notes
TTRPG design combines several crafts, but a project becomes tractable when its scope follows the experience it promises. A designer can favor story, setting, characters, or mechanics without abandoning the rest of the game; the choice tells them where to spend the first serious effort.
