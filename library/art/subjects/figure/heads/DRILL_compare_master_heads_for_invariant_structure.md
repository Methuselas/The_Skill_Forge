# Compare Master Heads for Invariant Structure

## Practice Task
Choose four to six master head studies that differ strongly in medium or style. Analyze all of them through the **same structural questions** before you copy any surface treatment: great masses, orientation, feature supports, major planes, and where the artist spends or withholds accents. Then test transfer by redrawing one head with a different mark-making approach while preserving its structural read.

## Target Skill
Learning from master art without confusing a master's visible mannerisms with the deeper construction decisions that make the head work.

## Setup
Use works with enough variation that easy stylistic imitation cannot solve the exercise: for example sculpture, tight linear drawing, loose brushwork, tonal painting, caricature, and an atmospheric or highly simplified head. Keep the references visible side by side.

## Instructions
1. For each work, make a tiny **great-mass diagram** containing only cranium, facial mass/jaw, centerline, and the dominant turn or tilt. Ignore hair strands, brush texture, wrinkles, and decorative edges.
2. Add a second pass that marks the **support relationships** of the eye/socket, nose wedge, dental/mouth mass, cheek/jaw transition, and neck attachment only where the work actually reveals them.
3. Reduce the rendering to a **plane/value map**: identify which large planes or overlaps create depth and which details could disappear without collapsing the head.
4. Mark the artist's **accent economy**—the few edges, darks, highlights, or line pressures that carry identity, orientation, or a crucial form turn. Distinguish those from medium-specific texture.
5. Compare the studies. Write down three relationships that recur across unlike works and three choices that belong mainly to one artist's medium or temperament.
6. Pick one head and redraw it using a deliberately different surface method from the original—for example convert a loose brush head into a sparse line study, or a sculptural head into broad tone. Preserve the mass, attitude, support, and accent hierarchy rather than copying the original marks.
7. If the redraw loses the head when the style changes, return to the earliest structural layer that failed. Do not rescue it by imitating the source's finish.

## Success Check
- The same head remains spatially coherent after its original surface manner is removed.
- You can name structural relationships shared by works with very different appearances.
- Your transfer redraw changes medium or mark character without changing the head's major mass, turn, support, or identity.
- Style-specific effects are treated as choices layered over form knowledge, not as substitutes for it.

## Common Failures
- Copying brushwork, hatching, or line flavor before identifying the head's masses and support structure.
- Treating one historical proportion formula or one master's simplification as a universal rule.
- Making every study look alike because your analysis ignores the source's real differences in attitude, proportion, age, or expression.
- Assuming a rough or distorted work is structurally careless because its finish is unconventional.
- Assuming correct construction requires a polished or naturalistic finish.

## Notes
Hogarth's closing gallery is most useful as a **transfer test**. Its durable claim is not that every master literally used Hogarth's diagrams, but that disciplined form relationships can be recovered beneath radically different surface languages. The exercise therefore studies invariants across examples and keeps personal or historical manner separate from construction.
