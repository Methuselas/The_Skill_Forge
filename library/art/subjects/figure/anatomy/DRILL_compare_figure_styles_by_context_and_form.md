# Compare Figure Styles by Context and Form

## Practice Task
Choose six to eight figure works that differ clearly in period, medium, or purpose. Include at least one pair of artists from the same broad period so you can separate **individual manner** from a more widely shared figure convention. Analyze every work with the same form checklist before copying its surface style.

## Target Skill
Learning from historical and stylistic figure precedents without mistaking one artist's marks, one period's convention, or one historical explanation for universal anatomy.

## Setup
Use reliable reproductions with basic attribution and, when available, a known display or use context such as freestanding sculpture, relief, niche, panel, portrait, or large narrative painting. Keep the references side by side. Do not invent missing historical motives; mark context as unknown when the source does not establish it.

## Instructions
1. For each work, make a small **neutral figure thumbnail** that ignores brush texture, hatching flavor, decoration, and color. Preserve only the figure's dominant proportion, pose, mass arrangement, and relationship to its field.
2. Record the same observable attributes for every work: frontality or turn, symmetry or imbalance, scale hierarchy, proportion, movement, depth, contour/edge behavior, surface treatment, and relation to the enclosing frame or environment.
3. Separate three layers in your notes: **individual manner** (tool handling, line flavor, brushwork, local simplification), **shared convention** (recurring figure organization across more than one artist or work), and **structural/design choice** (the mass, proportion, space, or movement decision that still matters after surface manner is removed).
4. Compare two works from roughly the same period. Name at least two things they share and two things that belong mainly to the individual artist. If you cannot separate those levels, add another same-period example before drawing conclusions.
5. Compare an earlier and later work. Look specifically for **persistence, refinement, combination, or transition** rather than forcing a clean break between categories.
6. When a figure looks deliberately elongated, frontal, hierarchically scaled, compressed into a field, or otherwise unlike a naturalistic norm, test whether the documented viewing condition, symbolic role, or enclosing format helps explain the choice **before** calling it an anatomical error.
7. Pick one figure and redraw it in a neutral medium or mark language. Preserve its major proportion, movement, depth, and field relationship while removing the original artist's surface mannerisms. Then state which decisions survived the change of medium.
8. Finish with one sentence for each work: “The transferable figure decision is ___; the period- or artist-specific treatment is ___.”

## Success Check
- You can distinguish an artist's personal handling from conventions that recur across other works.
- The comparison uses the same observable form attributes rather than relying on style names alone.
- A stylized or distorted figure is not automatically normalized before its purpose, field, and viewing context are checked.
- Historical context is recorded as evidence or uncertainty, not inferred as fact from appearance alone.
- The neutral redraw preserves the figure's major design logic while visibly changing the original surface manner.

## Common Failures
- Copying brushwork, line flavor, or decorative motifs before identifying the figure's mass, proportion, movement, and spatial organization.
- Treating Hogarth's historical categories or twentieth-century terminology as exhaustive or scientifically fixed classifications.
- Assuming every unusual proportion is an anatomy mistake, or the opposite: excusing accidental construction errors as “style” without evidence.
- Explaining a work's meaning from appearance alone when its use or historical context is unknown.
- Comparing only one artist per period and calling that artist's personal manner the style of an entire era.
- Forcing a work into one category when it clearly combines several tendencies.

## Notes
Hogarth's Chapter III is most useful as a **comparison method**, not as a set of historical laws. Its durable move is to separate the artist's signature handling from broader conventions, then compare those conventions through recurring figure attributes and known context. His named historical types and modern psychological categories remain source-specific interpretive scaffolds; the Drill keeps the observable comparison and discards the requirement to accept the taxonomy.
