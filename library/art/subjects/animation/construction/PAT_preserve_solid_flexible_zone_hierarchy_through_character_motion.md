# Preserve Solid-Flexible Zone Hierarchy Through Character Motion

## Pattern Rule
**IF** a character must bend, twist, squash, stretch, or act broadly without becoming either rubbery everywhere or rigid everywhere
**THEN** divide the construction into comparatively stable masses and more flexible connecting or surface regions, then concentrate deformation where the design permits while using the stable masses as structural references
**ELSE** keep the ordinary mass construction when the action does not require meaningful deformation

## Do
- Identify the comparatively solid masses before exaggerating the action, then locate the flexible regions that connect, wrap, or articulate around them.
- Let flexible regions bend, compress, extend, or twist more freely while the stable masses continue to anchor scale, orientation, and character identity.
- Allow soft surface features over a stable support to shift substantially when the action requires it; a solid underlying mass is a reference, not a command to freeze every visible contour.
- Adjust the solid-to-flexible balance to the character design: a caricature may permit much more deformation than a realistic figure while still retaining recognizable structural anchors.
- Use the hierarchy across the whole body so the action can pass from one stable mass through a flexible connection into the next instead of deforming each part independently.

## Don't
- Do not make every body region equally elastic; uniform deformation erases the distinction between supporting structure and flexible material.
- Do not make every body region equally rigid; locking the connectors prevents the pose from expressing bend, twist, compression, or recoil.
- Do not preserve a stable mass by freezing the soft tissue or features that ride over it.
- Do not exaggerate a flexible region so far that attachments, scale relationships, or character identity stop reading coherently.

## Checklist
- The main stable masses remain identifiable before, during, and after the action.
- Flexible regions visibly carry most of the bend, twist, compression, or extension that connects those masses.
- Soft surface reactions can move over stable supports without making the underlying construction appear to dissolve.
- The degree of flexibility matches the character's design rather than defaulting to either a mannequin or a rubber hose.
- Attachments and mass relationships remain continuous through the deformation.

## Notes
The useful distinction is comparative rather than absolute. A character can be much more flexible than a realistic body and still benefit from stable structural anchors. The hierarchy keeps exaggerated motion legible by giving deformation somewhere to happen and something stable to happen against.
