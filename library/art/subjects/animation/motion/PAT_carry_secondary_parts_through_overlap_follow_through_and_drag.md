# Carry Secondary Parts Through Overlap, Follow-Through, and Drag

## Pattern Rule
**IF** flexible or loosely attached parts move too synchronously with the primary body and make the action feel mechanical
**THEN** let the driven root respond first, carry the change progressively through the attached structure, allow distal regions to lag or continue their prior motion, and settle them through follow-through after the primary action changes or stops
**ELSE** keep the part closely synchronized when it is rigid, tightly constrained, or too small to show meaningful inertial lag

## Do
- Identify the primary driver and the attachment root before offsetting motion; the secondary part must remain causally connected to the body that carries it. Determine which end is actually supplying the force, because a shoulder-driven hand propagates motion outward while an external pull through a hand or held object can propagate the causal sequence back toward the body.
- When direction changes, turn the root first and let middle and distal regions continue the old path long enough to create readable drag.
- After the main body stops, allow residual motion to travel through the secondary part, including overshoot or reversal when its flexibility and momentum support it.
- Reduce the motion toward the end of the settle so the terminal state eases into place instead of snapping to a stop.
- Vary the amount of lag along the structure; flexible tips such as hair ends, coat tails, ears, or tails can trail farther than regions close to the attachment. Scale that lag with speed and active control: fast acceleration or reversal exposes more inertial trailing, while slow deliberate motion can suppress it through muscular control.
- Treat a loosely held prop as an attached mass when its inertia is visible, allowing the grip to lead while the object trails, rotates, or catches up according to its weight and freedom.
- When a secondary part has enough independent inertia that its position cannot be predicted reliably from the body's key poses, solve the primary body first, then track the secondary part as its own continuous motion pass.
- In that secondary pass, derive the path from the driver's motion, the part's own weight and flexibility, and environmental resistance such as air drag; the secondary may be moving fastest while the driver itself is stopping.

## Don't
- Do not offset every part by the same amount and call it overlap; the timing difference should grow from attachment, flexibility, and momentum.
- Do not detach the secondary path from its root merely to create a prettier arc.
- Do not reverse the whole appendage simultaneously when the change should propagate from root toward tip.
- Do not stop a flexible secondary part on the same instant as the primary body when visible momentum remains.
- Do not apply maximal lag to every flexible joint by default; slow controlled motion may keep connected parts much closer to synchronization.
- Do not force an inertial extremity through the primary body's key poses when doing so interrupts the secondary part's own continuous path.

## Checklist
- The primary driver, attachment root, middle region, and distal region remain traceable through the action.
- A direction change propagates through the structure rather than occurring everywhere at once.
- Distal regions lag, overshoot, or continue only as much as their flexibility, momentum, speed, and active control justify.
- Follow-through continues after the main action when residual energy should remain.
- The final settle slows into a stable state without breaking attachment or creating arbitrary delay.
- Independently solved secondary motion remains causally tied to the driver while preserving its own momentum through changes of speed and direction.

## Notes
Overlapping action is not random stagger. It is a causal timing relationship in which connected regions answer the primary motion at different moments. Drag describes the temporary lag during a change, follow-through carries remaining motion after the driver slows or stops, and the final settle spends that residual energy without making the appendage feel mechanically synchronized.

- Generalize overlap beyond loose appendages: different masses of the whole figure can begin, stop, and reverse on related but offset schedules. Avoid making the body behave as one object with one clock.
- Successive joint reversals can pass through an articulated chain to create flexibility; the moving flow may justify controlled anatomical exaggeration in intermediate poses.
