# Transition Sprint Startup From Forward Drive Into Steady Running Posture

## Pattern Rule
**IF** a runner accelerates from a start into a sustained sprint
**THEN** animate a distinct startup transition in which forward drive and aggressive acceleration mechanics progressively reorganize into the more repeatable posture and rhythm of steady running

## Do
- Begin the launch with a strong forward-directed body attitude appropriate to acceleration rather than immediately using the posture of an established run cycle.
- Let the legs and arms work forcefully during the first strides to overcome inertia and build speed.
- Raise and reorganize the torso progressively as the runner approaches the repeating mechanics of steady running.
- Change stride geometry and rhythm across the acceleration phase instead of copying the same cycle from the first step onward.
- Preserve the sense that the forward lean solves an acceleration problem; reduce or reorganize it as that problem changes.

## Don't
- Do not paste a normal repeating run cycle directly after the start and call it acceleration.
- Do not hold the extreme startup lean unchanged after the runner has reached a steady stride.
- Do not treat the first strides as identical copies with only larger spacing.

## Checklist
- The startup reads as mechanically different from the steady run.
- Forward drive is strongest while acceleration is being built.
- Torso attitude and stride organization visibly transition toward the sustained gait.
- The final repeating run feels like the result of the startup rather than an unrelated cycle.

## Notes
Webster's sprint-start sequence is useful because it shows acceleration as a changing whole-body problem. The exact lean, stride count, and timing vary with athlete, style, and starting condition; preserve the transition principle rather than a fixed pose chart.
