# Identify the Causal Level of Animated Movement Before Adding Performance
## Pattern Rule
**IF** an animated element is being overcomplicated or its movement lacks a clear cause
**THEN** identify whether it currently requires activity, action, animation, or acting, and solve only the causal level the subject and scene actually demand before adding higher-level performance

## Do
- Treat **activity** as change through time without movement that must read as characteristic natural behavior.
- Treat **action** as recognizable movement caused by the object's material, structure, environment, and external forces.
- Treat **animation** as movement generated by an animate subject's own intention to move, while still respecting external forces.
- Treat **acting** as movement shaped by thought, emotion, mood, temperament, personality, or other internal psychological motivation.
- Let a shot contain different levels at once when different elements genuinely require them.

## Don't
- Do not add personality acting to an object whose task is only to exhibit physical action.
- Do not reduce intentional creature movement to passive material response.
- Do not confuse visual complexity with causal complexity; a large passive motion can still be action rather than acting.

## Checklist
- The movement's immediate cause is identifiable.
- Internal intention is added only when the subject can plausibly generate it.
- Psychological motivation is visible only when performance requires it.
- Lower-level mechanics remain coherent when higher-level performance is added.

## Notes
The four levels form a causal hierarchy, not a ranking of artistic quality. A moving title can succeed as activity, a flame as action, an animal locomoting by choice as animation, and a character revealing thought as acting. The diagnostic prevents unnecessary performance layers from obscuring the actual movement problem.
