# Scale Whole Body Effort To Apparent Load

## Pattern Rule
**IF** an action involves resistance or load and the amount of recruited body effort must make that load believable
**THEN** Scale the amount of body recruited into an action according to the apparent load or resistance

## Do
- Use local hand/arm action for light loads when the task does not require broader stabilization.
- As the load rises, bring the body closer to it, establish a broader or more deliberate support, and recruit shoulders, spine, hips, stance, legs, and balance rather than asking the arms or a bent back to carry the whole demand.
- For a heavy lift from low level, organize the stance and body around the load before the lift, then let the legs and whole-body extension contribute to raising it.
- After the load leaves its original support, reorganize the pose around the combined body-plus-load mass rather than preserving the unloaded body's balance.
- Reorganize feet, grip, torso, or body mass before a heavy lift when the load would otherwise pull the performer outside a believable support base.
- Let the body mass begin the power phase when necessary so the shoulders and limbs can spend that momentum into the tool or load instead of asking an isolated arm to generate the whole effort.
- Distinguish the loaded or power phase from an easier return or recovery phase when the task is mechanically asymmetric.
- Show expectation of effort before and during the move.
- For pushing or pulling, distinguish the initial effort needed to overcome static resistance from the effort needed to maintain motion once the object is moving; wheels, runners, surface friction, and available traction can change that relationship substantially.
- For sustained quadruped hauling, let heavier resistance shorten the forward reach, increase backward thrust, lower or reorganize the hindquarters, and bring the head-neck/body attitude farther into the pull when reference supports it.
- If an expected effort fails, let the performer reassess and reorganize before a stronger attempt rather than replaying the same action at a different speed.

## Don't
- Do not make heavy work look like the same motion played slower.
- Do not communicate greater load only by adding facial strain, squash, or longer duration while leaving the causal organization of the action unchanged.
- Do not force equal effort into both directions of a repeated task when only one direction bears the working load.

## Checklist
- The viewer can infer the load from the performer’s bodily commitment.
- The support and balance strategy remain believable while the load is being organized and moved, including after the body and object become one combined moving mass.
- The sequence of body mass, shoulders, limbs, and tool/load makes the apparent effort mechanically coherent.
- Failed and successful attempts differ in strategy when the character has learned that the first effort was insufficient.

## Notes
Heavy effort is not merely a larger version of light effort. As resistance increases, the performer may have to reorganize balance, bring the load closer to the support structure, recruit the legs and whole body, move body mass before the tool, and separate a force-producing stroke from an easier recovery. Once a lifted object is free of its original support, the balance problem becomes the combined body-plus-load system. Pushing and pulling also distinguish the effort required to break static resistance from the effort needed to maintain motion after inertia has been overcome. A failed attempt can become part of the performance: the character reads the resistance, changes strategy, and commits differently on the next try.

`VAR_webster_distinguish_carrying_from_sustained_quadruped_haul` preserves the quadruped hauling case: sustained resistance can reorganize head-neck attitude, stride, hindquarter position, and backward thrust, while a manageable already-moving load may require far less visible deviation from ordinary walking.
