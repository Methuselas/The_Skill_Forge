# Scale Whole Body Effort To Apparent Load

## Pattern Rule
**IF** an action involves resistance or load and the amount of recruited body effort must make that load believable
**THEN** Scale the amount of body recruited into an action according to the apparent load or resistance

## Do
- Use local hand/arm action for light loads.
- Recruit shoulders, spine, stance, legs, and balance as the load increases.
- Reorganize feet, grip, torso, or body mass before a heavy lift when the load would otherwise pull the performer outside a believable support base.
- Let the body mass begin the power phase when necessary so the shoulders and limbs can spend that momentum into the tool or load instead of asking an isolated arm to generate the whole effort.
- Distinguish the loaded or power phase from an easier return or recovery phase when the task is mechanically asymmetric.
- Show expectation of effort before and during the move.
- If an expected effort fails, let the performer reassess and reorganize before a stronger attempt rather than replaying the same action at a different speed.

## Don't
- Do not make heavy work look like the same motion played slower.
- Do not communicate greater load only by adding facial strain, squash, or longer duration while leaving the causal organization of the action unchanged.
- Do not force equal effort into both directions of a repeated task when only one direction bears the working load.

## Checklist
- The viewer can infer the load from the performer’s bodily commitment.
- The support and balance strategy remain believable while the load is being organized and moved.
- The sequence of body mass, shoulders, limbs, and tool/load makes the apparent effort mechanically coherent.
- Failed and successful attempts differ in strategy when the character has learned that the first effort was insufficient.

## Notes
Heavy effort is not merely a larger version of light effort. As resistance increases, the performer may have to reorganize balance, recruit more of the body, move body mass before the tool, and separate a force-producing stroke from an easier recovery. A failed attempt can also become part of the performance: the character reads the resistance, changes strategy, and commits differently on the next try.
