# Set Motion Fidelity by Simulation Representation or Interpretation
## Pattern Rule
**IF** an animated movement is being judged as too realistic, not realistic enough, or insufficiently believable
**THEN** first decide whether the motion is intended as simulation, representation, or interpretation, then evaluate it against that chosen standard instead of applying one realism target to every animation

## Do
- Use **simulation** when movement should closely reproduce behavior that can be checked against real events or subjects.
- Use **representation** when direct evidence is incomplete or unavailable; build a believable result from structural evidence and suitable analogues.
- Use **interpretation** when movement deliberately departs from natural behavior to serve expressive, graphic, comic, or stylistic aims.
- Keep internal consistency inside the chosen approach so the audience understands what kind of motion contract is operating.

## Don't
- Do not demand documentary accuracy from deliberately interpretive motion.
- Do not label unsupported invention "simulation" when it cannot be tested against the real behavior it claims to reproduce.
- Do not treat representation as permission to ignore anatomy, weight, environment, or other available evidence.

## Checklist
- The intended fidelity class is named before the motion is corrected.
- The reference strategy matches that class.
- Believability is judged against the intended result rather than a universal realism scale.
- Any departure from real behavior is deliberate and consistent.

## Notes
The three approaches describe different evidentiary obligations. Simulation aims at close replication, representation constructs a plausible account where evidence is incomplete, and interpretation uses animation more freely. The distinction is useful because the same movement can be successful or unsuccessful depending on which contract the work is trying to satisfy.
