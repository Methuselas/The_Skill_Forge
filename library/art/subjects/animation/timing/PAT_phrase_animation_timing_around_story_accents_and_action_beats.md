# Phrase Animation Timing Around Story Accents and Action Beats

## Pattern Rule
**IF** an animated action or line of acting feels mechanically even, over-described, or rhythmically unrelated to its story emphasis
**THEN** group the action into meaningful phrases, place expressive extremes and holds at the important accents, and vary the intervening tempo so the timing carries the intended beat, mood, and emphasis
**ELSE** retain simpler even timing when the action is intentionally mechanical, neutral, or requires no expressive phrasing

## Do
- Divide the action into readable phrases such as anticipate-move-pose, gesture-pose, decision-action, or another beat structure that matches what the character is doing.
- Choose a limited set of expressive extremes for the phrases instead of assigning equal descriptive importance to every instant.
- Use holds, faster transitions, slow-ins, slow-outs, and changes of tempo to distinguish accents from connective motion.
- Let dialogue emphasis inform body timing when speech is present; stressed words or phrase boundaries can suggest where anticipation, a pose, or the strongest extreme belongs.
- Allow head, torso, hands, eyes, and mouth to carry related but not necessarily identical phrasing when staggered emphasis makes the acting clearer.
- Match temporal density to the scene: sustained frantic activity, clipped accents, long quiet holds, and sparse movement should feel intentionally different.
- Calibrate preparation, action, and reaction time to the intended audience and context: give an unfamiliar or easily missed idea enough time to register, but compress any phase that lingers after the audience has understood it.
- When a beat is unclear in playback, diagnose which phase needs adjustment—preparation, the action itself, or the reaction—rather than slowing the whole passage indiscriminately.
- Use anticipatory movement to steer attention toward the place where a fast event will occur; a clearly prepared action can be much shorter without becoming unreadable.
- When surprise depends on withholding anticipation, preserve the abrupt accent and return readability after it with a freeze, overshoot, recoil, or settle long enough for the audience to catch up.

## Don't
- Do not spread emphasis uniformly across every drawing; equal stress makes important beats disappear.
- Do not add extra extremes merely to illustrate every spoken word or every small motion when a stronger phrase can carry several of them.
- Do not make independent body parts follow unrelated rhythms unless the acting or physical cause requires that separation.
- Do not treat timing as cleanup after posing; the placement and strength of poses should be chosen with the intended rhythm already in mind.
- Do not use a single rhythmic formula for scenes with different moods, species, personalities, or dramatic functions.
- Do not slow a surprise merely because its accent is too quick to parse; when telegraphing would spoil the effect, let the aftermath carry the missing read time.

## Checklist
- The action can be divided into a small number of meaningful phrases or beats.
- Important accents receive clearer poses, holds, or timing contrast than connective motion.
- Tempo changes support the scene's mood and story rather than occurring accidentally.
- Dialogue and body phrasing cooperate when speech is present without forcing one pose per word.
- Different body regions remain coordinated even when their phrase boundaries or accents are offset.
- The sequence has an intentional rhythmic texture instead of uniform spacing and emphasis.
- A fresh viewer can understand each important beat on one pass without missing the action or waiting after its meaning is already clear.
- Fast anticipated actions remain readable because attention is prepared before the accent, while intentionally unanticipated accents remain readable through their aftermath.

## Notes
Phrasing treats animation as authored rhythm. A sequence can move-pose, anticipate-move-pose, hold, accelerate, or relax in patterns that make the story easier to read. The goal is not to prescribe one timing chart; it is to decide where the meaningful accents live and then organize the surrounding motion so those accents have contrast and authority.
`VAR_stanchfield_stage_action_through_preparation_anticipation_action_and_residuals` gives one useful phrase for actions that need setup and payoff: signal what is coming, gather the force, perform the action, then let physically appropriate residual motion finish the event.

- Map important action beats onto the timeline before filling every frame: contacts, changes of attention, prop contacts, dialogue accents, and other story events become temporal targets.
- For readable action, allocate enough time for the audience to perceive the event; do not cram more physical action into fewer frames merely because the poses can technically be drawn.
- Use anticipation → action → reaction/settle as a general causal phrase when the event benefits from preparation and consequence. The preparation may be a visible opposite/compressing move or, for sudden action, a micro-anticipation too brief to telegraph consciously.
- Use temporal contrast rather than one uniform “normal” speed: slightly faster and slower passages can make each other feel more expressive.
- Budget readable time around accents; the exact frame count is contextual rather than a universal recipe.
- Readability is an audience-time problem as well as a pose problem. Preparation directs attention, the action delivers the event, and the reaction confirms its consequence; each phase should last only as long as the intended audience needs to assimilate it.

`VAR_whitaker_phrase_explosion_as_fast_burst_and_slow_dispersal` treats an explosion as an asymmetric phrase rather than a uniform effect: the main burst arrives very quickly, a sufficiently large event may stay alive through irregular secondary activity, and the residual smoke, dust, or debris takes longer to dissipate. The relationship is more durable than any fixed frame count.

`VAR_whitaker_modulate_acting_tempo_hold_density_and_energy_by_mood` treats tempo, hold density, rebound, and motion energy as part of the acting. Low-energy states may settle into slower transitions and longer holds, high-energy states may become quicker and springier, and deliberative states may need extra readable time for thought to register. These are context-sensitive tendencies, not emotional frame-count formulas.

`VAR_whitaker_map_animation_accents_to_musical_beats_bars_and_mood` maps selected musical beats and bars onto the animation timeline while preserving the larger phrasing and mood of the music. Strong accents may recruit the whole body, and very fast music may call for skipping visible hits on some beats so the action remains readable rather than mechanically busy.
