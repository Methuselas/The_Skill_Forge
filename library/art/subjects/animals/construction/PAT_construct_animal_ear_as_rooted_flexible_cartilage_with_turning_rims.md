# Construct an Animal Ear as Rooted Flexible Cartilage With Turning Rims

## Pattern Rule
**IF** an animal ear is being attached to the head as a flat leaf or triangle
**THEN** build it as a flexible bag-like cartilage form rooted near the skull through a stabilized base, define the auricle opening, and track the inner and outer rims as separate turning paths that can twist, curve, and change relationship toward the tip
**ELSE** simplify the ear silhouette only after its root, opening, and rim turn are already understood.

## Do
- Establish the root and the direction of the ear opening before designing the outer silhouette.
- Give the cartilage a shallow bag or shell volume with an inside and outside rather than a single plane.
- Follow inner and outer rim paths independently; one edge can twist or bend differently from the other as it approaches the tip.
- Let the species determine whether the opening is pointed, blunt, rounded, vertical, lateral, or downward-facing.
- Preserve the rooted volume when the ear rotates for attention, threat, or other behavior cues.

## Don't
- Do not float the ear on the skull without a readable rooted base.
- Do not draw inner and outer edges as perfectly parallel outlines of one flat shape.
- Do not copy one horse, cow, dog, or big-cat ear silhouette as a universal template.
- Do not use expressive ear rotation while allowing the cartilage volume to collapse or detach from the head.

## Checklist
- The ear has a clear rooted base and opening direction.
- Inner and outer rims describe a three-dimensional shell rather than a flat cutout.
- Edge twists remain plausible as the ear turns.
- Species-specific silhouette differences arise from the shared construction rather than replacing it.
- Behavioral rotation preserves the same cartilage/root logic.

## Notes
Bammes describes the outer ear as highly flexible cartilage shaped like a bag, with a stabilizing muscular/root region near the skull and species-dependent openings. His horse and cow examples emphasize that the inner and outer edges do not simply run parallel: rim paths twist and curve differently as they approach the tip. That three-dimensional rim logic is the transferable construction; the exact pointed, blunt, or rounded species silhouettes remain variants.
