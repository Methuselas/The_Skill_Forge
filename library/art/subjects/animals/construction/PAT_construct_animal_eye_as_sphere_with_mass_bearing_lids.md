# Construct an Animal Eye as a Sphere With Mass-Bearing Lids

## Pattern Rule
**IF** an animal eye reads as a flat almond or painted symbol when the head turns
**THEN** construct the eyeball as a sphere seated in the skull, wrap upper and lower lids over that sphere as forms with their own thickness, and continue the spherical bulge into the surrounding flesh so overlap and foreshortening survive every view
**ELSE** simplify the visible eye opening only after the spherical support is already understood.

## Do
- Establish the eyeball's spherical volume before drawing the eyelid opening.
- Wrap the lids around the ball and show their overlap/intersection changing from front, side, three-quarter, and rearward views.
- Give the lids actual mass; let their edges sit on the sphere rather than cutting a paper-thin hole in a face mask.
- Continue the eyeball's roundness into the immediately surrounding flesh instead of flattening the orbital area at the lid boundary.
- Adjust the relative recession of the inner and outer corners to the species and viewpoint rather than copying one horse-eye diagram universally.

## Don't
- Do not draw the eye opening first and fit an eyeball behind it afterward.
- Do not keep the same almond shape as the head rotates.
- Do not separate the lid edge from the spherical form it wraps.
- Do not promote species-specific pupil shape, iris color, eyelashes, or corner placement into universal construction rules.

## Checklist
- The eyeball reads as a sphere even when most of it is covered.
- Upper and lower lids have thickness and overlap the ball plausibly.
- The surrounding flesh continues the spherical volume.
- The eye opening changes with viewpoint instead of remaining a flat symbol.
- Species-specific detail is added only after the construction is secure.

## Notes
Across a rotation of the same eye, keep the eyeball spherical, make the lids follow and overlap that sphere with their own mass, and continue the spherical volume into the surrounding area. Species-specific traits such as pupil shape and corner recession remain bounded observations rather than universal rules.
