# Select One Dominant Quality for an Animal Study

## Pattern Rule
**IF** an animal offers several attractive qualities and the study is becoming an equal-weight inventory of proportion, action, structure, surface, and detail
**THEN** name the single strongest impressional quality first, make that quality the study's governing statement, and subordinate or omit observations that do not strengthen it
**ELSE** when the assignment explicitly requires several dimensions, establish a clear order of emphasis and study them in sequence rather than trying to develop all of them equally at once

## Do
- Decide what first makes this animal worth drawing before committing to detail. Bammes's examples include proportion, function or action, surface texture, solidity, structure, delicacy, massiveness, and squatness.
- Phrase the governing impression concretely enough to test, such as unusually massive, extremely slender, strongly compressed, dominated by a coat texture, or defined by a particular action.
- Let large choices serve the selected idea first: choose the pose, proportion emphasis, degree of simplification, and amount of surface description according to what the study is trying to say.
- Allow interesting secondary traits to recede when they compete with the selected quality, even when they would be enjoyable to draw.
- Stop when the chosen impression is sufficiently stated. Economy means using only the contour, gesture, hatching, tone, or surface information needed to complete that objective—not drawing quickly, vaguely, or to an arbitrary low line count.
- Use shorthand only after the governing impression is clear enough that abbreviated marks still point back to a coherent animal rather than to disconnected symbols.

## Don't
- Do not give every observed feature equal visual importance merely because it is present in the reference.
- Do not keep adding attractive details after the main idea has become harder to read; return to the selected quality and remove or quiet what competes with it.
- Do not confuse economy with haste or incompleteness; a concise study is finished when its selected objective is complete in itself.
- Do not confuse selection with stereotype. The dominant quality should come from the actual animal, pose, or intended statement, not from a generic claim about the species.
- Do not let a focus on coat, color, or expression erase the structural relationships needed to keep the animal convincing.

## Checklist
- The study's governing quality can be stated in one short phrase before refinement begins.
- The largest proportion, pose, mass, or surface decisions visibly support that phrase.
- Secondary observations are either omitted or clearly quieter than the main statement.
- The animal remains structurally coherent when descriptive detail is reduced.
- Adding one more detail would strengthen the chosen quality rather than merely increase information density.

## Notes
Bammes calls the qualities that first seize the artist's attention **impressional qualities**. His opening exercise is selective: survey the animal, decide which impression is strongest, and concentrate on that one instead of trying to develop every appealing aspect in the same drawing. The purpose is not simplification for its own sake; selection creates a stable graphic concept and trains the artist to distinguish essential information from merely available information.

Section 1.2 immediately places a structural limit on this freedom. A selective study still benefits from enough anatomy to perceive the animal as one articulated entity. Choosing a dominant quality changes emphasis, not the requirement that the parts belong to the same constructed body.
