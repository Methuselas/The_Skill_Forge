# Orient an Animal Body in Depth With Backline and Transverse Axes

## Pattern Rule
**IF** an animal turns, reclines, or foreshortens enough that a profile backline and left-right body landmarks no longer explain one coherent volume
**THEN** carry the body's longitudinal back/center route through visible and hidden passages, intersect it at a few major structural stations with transverse axes, and use those crossings as the spatial reference system before adding body masses
**ELSE** keep the simpler profile or vertebral-direction framework when depth and attachment are already unambiguous.

## Do
- Continue the longitudinal route from the muzzle through the trunk toward the tail even where the body hides it; the concealed passage still determines how the visible masses belong to one animal.
- Place transverse axes only at structurally useful stations. Bammes names the line between the scapular tips and the line between the hip tuberosities as especially useful cross-axes because their tilt exposes the body's turn in space.
- Let the ends of those transverse axes rise, fall, or stagger according to the actual view. In repose or a turned pose, the paired landmarks are often not horizontally aligned on the page.
- Use the resulting crossings to order the major masses along one three-dimensional trail: head, neck, thorax, lumbar connection, pelvis, and tail. Choose each mass from the animal's function and spatial situation rather than from a stock primitive recipe.
- Add limb directions after the trunk reference system is coherent, so shoulder and pelvic attachments inherit the same depth solution instead of being patched onto a flat silhouette.
- With practice, reduce or omit the drawn guide only after the same longitudinal and transverse relationships can be held reliably in the inner eye.

## Don't
- Do not stop the body's organizing route simply because fur, overlap, or a near mass conceals it; infer the hidden continuation from the visible structure around it.
- Do not keep shoulder and hip cross-axes mechanically horizontal in a three-quarter, reclining, or twisted view.
- Do not solve foreshortening by stacking generic discs, circles, ellipses, or ovoids whose orientation is unrelated to the animal's actual structural landmarks.
- Do not copy the tiger demonstration's exact primitive shapes onto every species. The useful rule is the ordered spatial reference system, not a fixed catalog of solids.

## Checklist
- One continuous longitudinal route can be traced mentally through the whole trunk, including hidden portions.
- At least two transverse axes clarify how the shoulder and pelvic regions turn in depth.
- The transverse axes agree with paired anatomical landmarks instead of floating independently of the body.
- Major masses can be placed in sequence along the route without contradicting the axis tilts.
- Limbs attach to a spatially oriented trunk rather than to a flat outer contour.
- Removing the guide lines would not force a redesign of the established foreshortening.

## Notes
Bammes shifts from the profile backline exercise to a more demanding spatial problem. He argues that the back route and a few intersecting cross-axes form a three-dimensional reference system, with the scapular tips and hip tuberosities providing concrete transverse landmarks. A reclining-tiger study makes the method explicit: the longitudinal route is continued through concealment, cross-axes are placed at important turns, basic masses are ordered along that trail, and only afterward are the limbs and more organic intersections developed.

The source also warns against replacing this reasoning with attractive but mindless schematic formulas. That warning is central to the Pattern: a primitive is useful only when its orientation is earned by the animal's specific spatial structure.
