# Control Tail-Driven Fish Speed With Amplitude, Then Frequency

## Pattern Rule
**IF** a tail-driven fish must accelerate from rest into faster sustained swimming
**THEN** increase tail-beat amplitude first, then raise beat frequency once the useful sweep is established, keeping the strongest oscillation concentrated where the species actually generates thrust
**ELSE** use the species' alternate low-speed propulsive system when the tail is reduced or temporarily inactive

## Do
- Start slow movement with relatively small tail excursions rather than immediately using a maximum sweep.
- Enlarge the side-to-side caudal excursion as the fish accelerates toward a normal cruising action.
- Increase beat frequency for further speed after the useful amplitude range has been reached instead of continuing to widen the sweep indefinitely.
- Keep much of the trunk comparatively stable in rigid-bodied high-speed swimmers and concentrate the strongest oscillation through the caudal peduncle and tail.
- Allow flexible species to recruit more of the body into the propulsive wave when their construction supports it.
- At very low speed, let species with capable paired fins reduce or suspend tail beating and maneuver with those fins instead.

## Don't
- Do not equate faster swimming only with a larger tail arc.
- Do not animate a high-speed rigid-bodied swimmer as though the entire trunk were an eel-like ribbon.
- Do not keep the tail beating merely because the animal is translating if reference shows low-speed fin propulsion instead.
- Do not force one amplitude or frequency relationship onto every species.

## Checklist
- The tail sweep grows before high-frequency beating dominates the acceleration.
- Faster sustained motion does not require implausibly ever-larger side-to-side excursions.
- The amount of trunk participation matches the fish's body plan.
- Low-speed propulsion can transfer to paired fins when the species supports that behavior.

## Notes
Webster separates two visible controls that are often collapsed into a single idea of "move the tail faster." Sweep size can grow as a fish leaves rest, but beyond a useful range additional speed is achieved more efficiently by increasing repetition. Body plan changes where that oscillation lives: stiff, fast swimmers localize much of it near the tail, while flexible fish can carry a larger traveling wave through the trunk.
