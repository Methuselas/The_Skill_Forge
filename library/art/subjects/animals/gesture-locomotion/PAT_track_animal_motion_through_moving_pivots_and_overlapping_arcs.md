# Track Animal Motion Through Moving Pivots and Overlapping Arcs

## Pattern Rule
**IF** an animal action sequence feels hinged to fixed points, mechanically synchronized, or as though every part is following one path
**THEN** map the major motions as arcs around joints whose own positions travel with the body, and let connected parts follow distinct overlapping paths while preserving their attachment to the primary action
**ELSE** keep a simpler common trajectory when the subject is genuinely moving as one rigid or nearly rigid unit

## Do
- Establish the main body travel first, then place the shoulder, hip, and other carrying pivots where that travel moves them from phase to phase.
- Swing distal members from those transported pivots rather than around points pinned to the page; the joint can advance while the leg continues its own arc.
- Let forelegs, hindlegs, head, or other connected parts overlap the main action by taking different paths or arriving at different phases instead of matching one another exactly.
- Use a reversal when the motion actually changes direction or a part counteracts the preceding sweep; the reverse should answer the action rather than merely decorate it.
- Keep every secondary path traceable back to its parent structure so overlapping motion remains articulated rather than becoming detached flourish.

## Don't
- Freeze a pivot in screen space while the torso that carries it travels past.
- Give every limb the same arc, phase, or direction merely to make the sequence orderly.
- Confuse overlapping action with arbitrary delay; the different paths still belong to one connected animal and one larger event.
- Read the stylized horse examples as exact gait timing for every species or speed.

## Checklist
- Each important limb or secondary part can be traced to a parent pivot that also moves through the sequence.
- At least one secondary path differs intentionally from the primary body path without breaking attachment.
- Direction changes correspond to a real reversal, counteraction, or transition in the event.
- Reducing the drawings to pivots, arcs, and a body trajectory still preserves the action's order and direction.

## Notes
Hultgren's animation sheet treats an arc as a relationship to a pivot, but explicitly warns that the pivot itself can move. His successive horse drawings then separate the leg paths from the body's overall travel and label this difference as overlapping action. The useful decision is therefore not to draw prettier curves, but to analyze a moving articulated system in which the carrier and the carried member can have different trajectories at the same time.
