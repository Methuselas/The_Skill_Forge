# Match Bird Foot and Leg Placement to Locomotion, Support, and Capture Role

## Pattern Rule
**IF** a bird's distal leg, foot, or leg placement must be designed for a specific way of moving, supporting weight, or handling prey
**THEN** shape the toes, claws, webbing, contact spread, leg length, and fore-aft leg placement around that functional role while preserving the bird leg's underlying joint chain
**ELSE** follow reliable species reference without inventing a more specialized foot than the action requires

## Do
- Use gripping toes and claws when the bird must perch or cling to narrow supports.
- Spread the support area with broad or splayed toes when the bird must distribute weight over soft or wet ground.
- Use webbing when the feet provide aquatic propulsion.
- Strengthen toes and claws toward talons when the feet must seize, hold, or kill prey.
- Reduce and strengthen the distal foot when the bird's primary terrestrial job is rapid running.
- Move the legs farther rearward when reference shows a swimming specialization, and carry the resulting terrestrial balance and walking consequences into the pose or animation.
- Match terrestrial gait to size and leg placement: small light birds often use symmetrical hops, some intermediate birds use asymmetrical hops or mixed walk/hop strategies, and larger birds more often walk or run.
- Increase side-to-side waddling when short legs or rearward leg placement force stronger lateral balance shifts; reduce it when long legs support a more upright running strategy.

## Don't
- Do not treat all bird feet as interchangeable three-toed symbols.
- Do not add webbing, talons, or extreme toe spread without a locomotor, support, or capture function.
- Do not move the legs rearward for swimming efficiency and then animate the bird on land as though its balance were unchanged.

## Checklist
- Toe spread, claw form, and webbing match the bird's contact or propulsion job.
- Leg length and placement support the intended environment and movement.
- The foot specialization still connects correctly to the hidden-knee/visible-ankle bird-leg chain.
- Any functional gain is accompanied by the visible tradeoff Webster describes when the body plan demands one.
- Ground gait, body angle, and lateral sway agree with the bird's apparent size and leg placement.

## Notes
Webster links bird feet directly to locomotion and behavior. Perching, wading, running, swimming, gripping prey, and supporting weight on soft ground produce different distal solutions. His aquatic examples add an especially useful whole-body consequence: rearward leg placement improves propulsion in water but makes terrestrial walking less effective. His ground-locomotion examples extend the same principle: small birds can afford repeated hopping, larger birds shift toward walking or running, and rearward or short-legged construction increases waddling while long-legged flightless birds run more upright. The card therefore owns both the specialized foot and the placement/scale tradeoffs that change how the bird carries itself on land.
