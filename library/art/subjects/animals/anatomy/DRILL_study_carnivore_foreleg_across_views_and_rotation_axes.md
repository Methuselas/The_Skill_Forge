# Study a Carnivore Foreleg Across Views and Rotation Axes

## Practice Task
Make one dog foreleg study that progresses from measured front-view directions to several spatial views and paw details, then make one lion forearm study in which the radius-ulna pivot axis is explicit and the forms are condensed into simple three-dimensional masses.

## Target Skill
Constructing a carnivore shoulder and foreleg consistently across views while tracking segment directions, radius-ulna rotation, paw rays, and the forearm pivot axis.

## Setup
Use clear skeletal references of a dog and a cat-family carnivore from at least front and three-quarter views. Reserve one area for the dog sequence and one for the lion rotation study. Work in line and light hatching; omit musculature until the skeletal relationships are stable.

## Instructions
1. In front view, mark the relative lengths and main directions of scapula, humerus, radius-ulna region, carpus, metacarpus, and digits. Add the major joint levels before drawing bone contours.
2. Rebuild the same front view with the actual widths, tapers, and joint ends. Keep radius and ulna independently visible and preserve the metacarpal fan rather than collapsing the paw into one block.
3. Isolate the paw in a three-quarter external view. Draw the metacarpal rays first, then the phalangeal directions, and finish one claw as a continuation of a digit rather than as a separate symbol.
4. Construct a three-quarter front view of the whole foreleg, then an internal view. Compare the two against the front-view measurements so segment identity survives the change of camera.
5. On the lion study, draw a longitudinal pivot axis through the forearm before adding the two bones. Place the ulna and let the radius change relation around that axis as the paw turns.
6. Reduce the lion's forearm and paw to a few simple solids that make the torsion readable. Use cross-sections or short contour bands where they clarify how the forms penetrate space.
7. Draw a tiny door-hinge analogy beside the study: one long axis with a plate swiveling around it. Then return to the anatomical drawing and verify that the analogy clarifies the radius movement without replacing the real two-bone structure.
8. Finish by comparing dog and lion studies. Name one place where the construction was distorted by view and one place where the forearm rotation was accidentally shifted into the wrist; redraw those two passages only.

## Success Check
- Front-view segment lengths and joint levels remain consistent in the three-quarter and internal reconstructions.
- Radius and ulna are separate, spatially coherent forms rather than parallel decorative lines.
- The lion paw turn can be traced back through the radius-ulna relationship to the forearm pivot axis.
- The paw fan and digit directions remain three-dimensional in both front and three-quarter views.
- The hinge analogy helps explain rotation but the final anatomy still contains two distinct bones and species-specific joints.
- Condensed masses make the forearm torsion easier to read without erasing the skeletal landmarks.

## Common Failures
- Starting with the finished silhouette before measuring the relative directions and lengths of the skeletal sections.
- Copying one profile repeatedly instead of proving the same structure from different views.
- Drawing radius and ulna as two fixed parallel rails and rotating the paw below them.
- Treating the metacarpal fan as a flat comb whose digit directions cannot turn in depth.
- Letting the door-hinge analogy become a literal single-bone mechanism.
- Adding muscles or fur to disguise a rotation or proportion error that is still unresolved in the skeleton.

## Notes
Bammes's Figure 87 presents the dog study as an ordered investigation rather than a finished plate copied whole: first the relative lengths and directions in front view, then the basic skeletal study, then isolated paw and claw details, followed by three-quarter and internal views. That sequence makes view consistency part of the exercise instead of an optional redraw.

Figure 88 adds a different learning problem with the lion. The source asks the student to emphasize three-dimensionality and condensed form while paying special attention to the lower-limb bones changing position. The red pivotal axis and the door-hinge sketch make that rotation inspectable, so this drill keeps both the comparative multi-view study and the explicit axis test rather than reducing the unit to anatomy labeling.


`VAR_bammes_cycle_carnivore_foreleg_from_life_observation_to_home_reconstruction` adds the §6.6 life-study loop: observe recurring muscle/landmark behavior on the moving animal, reconstruct the skeleton afterward, and reduce the recurrent attitudes to simple blocks.
