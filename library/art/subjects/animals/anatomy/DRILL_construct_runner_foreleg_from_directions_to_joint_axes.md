# Construct a Runner Foreleg From Directions to Joint Axes

## Practice Task
Make a three-pass skeletal study of a horse or another clearly documented ungulate runner foreleg, then add one flexed-state comparison so the shoulder, carpus, and distal joints are understood as moving constructions rather than fixed landmarks.

## Target Skill
Constructing the shoulder and foreleg of a specialized runner from segment directions, joint axes, and state-dependent skeletal forms before surface anatomy.

## Setup
Use a clear skeletal plate or museum reference with at least a profile or three-quarter view. Give the study enough page space to separate the directional pass, the joint-form pass, and the flexed-state comparison. Keep muscle rendering and coat detail out of the exercise.

## Instructions
1. Visually assess the lengths of the scapula, humerus, forearm, metacarpal region, and distal foot. Mark only their main directions and the shoulder, elbow, carpal, and digital pivots.
2. Add the slanted scapula as a real triangular plate on the side of the thorax rather than a shoulder point. Show its major spine/edge direction so the shoulder girdle has a readable spatial orientation.
3. Rebuild the chain in a three-quarter view. Mark the cross-axes of the shoulder, elbow, carpal, and distal joints and add any through-axis needed to explain the shoulder orientation in depth.
4. Construct the joint ends and shafts around those axes. Preserve the observed taper and curvature of the bones; do not turn the foreleg into a stack of equal straight rods.
5. Add only the large skeletal landmarks that materially affect the living form: point of shoulder, ulna/elbow projection, carpal/pisiform mass, and the relevant fetlock/digital projections in the animal being studied.
6. Make a separate flexed-state comparison. Shift the scapula if the pose requires it, close the shoulder/carpal/digital chain, and redraw the joint shapes for that state rather than rotating the extended study intact.
7. Finish by reducing both versions back to the segment directions and axes. If the detailed construction cannot be explained by the same underlying chain, correct the skeleton instead of adding surface detail.

## Success Check
- The study can be read from scapula to distal contact as one ordered foreleg chain.
- Segment lengths and directions were established before bone contour.
- Joint cross-axes agree with the three-dimensional view.
- The scapula is mobile and spatial rather than a fixed decorative landmark.
- The flexed carpal/digital regions visibly change form instead of keeping the extended-state silhouette.
- Bone shafts keep observed taper and curvature.
- The study remains skeletal enough that musculature can be added without rebuilding the basic foreleg.

## Common Failures
- Starting from the horse's outer chest/leg silhouette and discovering the scapula only afterward.
- Treating the scapula as fixed while the rest of the foreleg swings.
- Drawing a perfectly straight lower limb because the runner support chain is comparatively columnar.
- Copying one joint symbol down the whole leg.
- Adding tendon and muscle detail before the joint axes and segment directions agree.
- Using horse-specific fusion or hoof construction as the template for a carnivore or primate comparison.

## Notes
Bammes presents a **step-by-step working study** whose first phase establishes the directions and lengths of the limb sections; the next moves into a three-quarter front view with the joint axes and constructional shapes; and a local elbow study clarifies the hinge-dominant runner anatomy. The artist assesses lengths and directions, draws the joint and three-dimensional axes, preserves mechanical feasibility, and remembers that skeletal forms curve rather than behaving like rigid sticks.

A flexed-state comparison supplies the transfer check: the mobile scapula can shift while the limb swings, and closed joints produce visibly different local forms. The exercise therefore stops before musculature, which owns the next layer.
