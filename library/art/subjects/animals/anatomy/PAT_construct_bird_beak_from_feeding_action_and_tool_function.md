# Construct a Bird Beak From Feeding Action and Tool Function

## Pattern Rule
**IF** a bird's beak must be designed or reconstructed and a generic wedge would erase species-specific function
**THEN** derive its length, thickness, curvature, tip shape, strength, and any specialized movement from the feeding or tool action the beak must perform, then fit that solution to the bird's head
**ELSE** retain the observed beak proportions when reliable species reference already establishes the functional shape

## Do
- Start from the action the beak performs: tearing, cracking, probing, spearing, chiseling, filtering, sweeping through water, or another evidenced feeding/tool behavior.
- Match robustness to the task: strong blunt forms suit forceful crushing or chiseling, while long slender forms suit reaching or probing.
- Let curvature and orientation follow the way the bird meets the material it feeds on rather than assuming every similar diet produces the same silhouette.
- Preserve unusual functional articulation when reference establishes it, such as a beak whose upper and lower parts can shift laterally to reach food.
- Compare several birds that solve a similar feeding problem before treating one beak shape as the only valid solution.

## Don't
- Do not design every bird beak as the same tapered cone with only length changed.
- Do not infer exact beak shape from diet alone when the feeding action differs.
- Do not force different filter-feeding or probing birds into one silhouette merely because their food source is similar.

## Checklist
- The beak shape implies a specific feeding or tool action.
- Length, thickness, curvature, and tip design agree with that action.
- The beak remains integrated with the underlying head instead of reading as an attached prop.
- Similar-function comparisons have not been flattened into one universal solution.

## Notes
Treat the beak as a working tool whose form reflects how a bird obtains food. Raptors tear, seed eaters crush, some fish hunters use spear-like bills, woodpeckers chisel, curlews probe, hummingbirds reach into flowers, and filter-feeding species arrive at visibly different solutions because they gather food in different ways. The transferable decision is functional rather than taxonomic: first identify the action, then construct the beak that can plausibly perform it.
