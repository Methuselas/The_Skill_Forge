# Model and Redraw an Animal Thorax Across Views and Sections

## Practice Task
Choose one animal thorax type and study it as a simple three-dimensional core rather than as a finished torso. If an actual skeleton is unavailable, make a small model from clay, Plasticine, foam, paper, or another simple material. Draw the same core in front view, profile, cross-section, and several three-quarter/high/low angles, then reconstruct one view without looking at the model.

## Target Skill
Internalizing an animal thorax as a manipulable three-dimensional core whose changing sections can be reconstructed consistently from unfamiliar viewpoints.

## Setup
Use a readable thorax reference, skeleton image, or Bammes-style comparative section study. Keep the first model simple: one enclosed thorax mass with an anterior opening, posterior expansion, dorsal/ventral routes, and only the section changes needed to distinguish its type. Avoid fur and musculature during the drill.

## Instructions
1. In profile, draw the thorax as one closed core and mark its broad dorsal and ventral routes. Use the profile outline only as a guide to the mass, not as a finished silhouette to copy.
2. Add a front or end view and one or two cross-sections. Record the actual width/depth relation at those stations instead of defaulting to an ellipse.
3. If possible, build a small physical version of the thorax. Turn it in the hand and compare the model's curve apexes, front/rear expansion, and plane transitions with the drawings.
4. Draw the core from at least three unfamiliar angles, including one foreshortened view. Keep the same structural proportions while letting the visible contour change with the camera.
5. In each view, place only enough internal section lines to prove the volume. The lines describe the core's changing sections; they are not a diagram of the ribs.
6. Compare the drawings side by side. Correct any view in which the front opening, rear expansion, width/depth ratio, or dorsal/ventral route contradicts the same object seen elsewhere.
7. Remove the model/reference and redraw one three-quarter view from memory. Reconstruct from the longitudinal routes and section sequence rather than from a memorized silhouette.
8. Finish with a small living-animal study in which fur or soft form is present, but keep the thorax core mentally and structurally readable beneath it.

## Success Check
- The same thorax can be recognized across front, profile, cross-section, and three-quarter views without changing its underlying proportions arbitrarily.
- Cross-sections reflect actual changing width/depth rather than repeated generic ovals.
- A foreshortened view still preserves the relation between the anterior opening, posterior mass, back route, and belly/sternum route.
- Internal guide lines clarify the core and are not mistaken for literal ribs.
- The memory redraw can be rebuilt from structural relations after the model is removed.
- In the final living-animal study, coat or soft tissue wraps a specific thorax rather than hiding a generic barrel.

## Common Failures
- Copying the side silhouette repeatedly while never proving the core from an end or oblique view.
- Building a model that is symmetrical and tubular even when the chosen animal's thorax is compressed, broad, asymmetric, or otherwise distinctive.
- Treating every drawn section line as a rib and becoming trapped in anatomical detail.
- Adding many equally spaced contour bands instead of a few sections at meaningful changes of form.
- Changing the thorax proportions from view to view because each drawing was solved independently.
- Memorizing one finished outline for the imagination pass rather than reconstructing the mass from its routes and sections.

## Notes
Bammes explicitly recommends making differently shaped thorax models when an animal skeleton is unavailable. The point is not model-making for its own sake: printed page 102 asks the student to view the body from every conceivable angle so there are no gaps in three-dimensional imagination, and states that the preparatory work deepens observation and lasting comprehension of form. Figures 106-108 then supply the visual progression from comparative section diagrams to simplified cow and horse thorax studies.

This Drill keeps the source's tactile/multiview method and adds one memory reconstruction as the test that the plastic core has actually been internalized rather than merely copied.
