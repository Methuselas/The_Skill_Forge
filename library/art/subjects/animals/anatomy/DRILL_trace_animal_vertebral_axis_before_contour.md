# Trace the Animal Vertebral Axis Before Contour

## Practice Task
Make a sheet of rapid animal studies in which the first and most important mark is one continuous axial line from the tip of the nose to the tip of the tail. Delay contour and anatomy until the line alone records the viewing angle, the main rises and falls, and the relative lengths of the body sections.

## Target Skill
Reading the animal's vertebral course as both a movement/expression line and a spatial body axis before contour or internal detail is allowed to distract from it.

## Setup
Choose several clear animal references or live poses with visibly different back courses: at least one relatively level-backed quadruped, one high-withers or high-sacrum type, and one strongly flexed, extended, sitting, or crouching pose. Work small with pen or another tool that discourages repeated correction.

## Instructions
1. Before drawing, judge the overall direction of the animal in space and identify where the vertebral course rises, falls, flattens, or changes angle.
2. Put down one continuous line from nose tip through the head/neck direction and trunk to the tail tip. Do not lift the tool to inventory small contour changes.
3. Compare the relative length of the cervical, thoracic/lumbar, sacral, and tail portions only as broad sections. Correct the next repetition rather than patching the current line with several competing strokes.
4. Repeat the same pose two or three times quickly. Alter only one decision at a time - viewing angle, a rise/fall, or a section length - and notice how much the animal's expression changes.
5. On one repetition, add only the skull, thorax, and pelvis as light masses around the accepted axis. Check that the line still reads as the organizing route through them rather than as a decorative stripe added afterward.
6. Only after the axis is convincing, add a minimal contour or two. Stop if the contour starts replacing the structural decision you were practicing.
7. Repeat with a second animal type or a very different pose so the exercise does not become memorization of one species' static back profile.

## Success Check
- One uninterrupted line communicates the animal's main orientation before any contour is added.
- The rises, falls, and section lengths change appropriately between species and poses.
- Adding the three core masses does not require relocating the accepted axial route.
- A flexed, extended, crouched, or sitting pose changes the vertebral course rather than merely changing the silhouette around a stock line.
- The study remains fast and spare enough that the back/axis decision is obvious at a glance.

## Common Failures
- Tracing the outer back contour instead of locating the body's organizing vertebral route in space.
- Drawing many short searching strokes until no single axial decision remains readable.
- Copying one memorized horse, dog, or cat backline into every pose.
- Adding limbs and surface anatomy before the viewing angle and rises/falls are secure.
- Treating the line only as gesture and ignoring its job as a three-dimensional body axis.
- Treating the line only as anatomy and losing the expressive change created by its overall course.

## Notes
Bammes states that the course of the back is simultaneously a vehicle of movement/expression and an indicator of the body's central axis in space. His procedure is unusually explicit: judge the overall course and viewing angle first, draw the central axis in one line from nose tip to tail tip, practice the rises, falls, and section lengths until confident, and delay contour or internal form until that decision is secure. The intended destination is an increasingly economical animal study in which the vertebral disposition carries much of the action.
`VAR_bammes_internalize_profile_backline_with_unbroken_contour_and_memory` adds a deliberately two-dimensional observation exercise: hold the pen on the page from muzzle to rear foot, repeat the same animal until its profile rhythm is familiar, and alternate concentrated observation with a memory-only transcription. It complements rather than replaces the host Drill's internal nose-to-tail vertebral axis.
