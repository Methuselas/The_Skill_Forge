# Construct a Bird Wing Surface From Functional Feather Bands

## Pattern Rule
**IF** a bird wing must read as an articulated aerodynamic surface rather than a single fan of repeated feathers
**THEN** organize the visible wing into primary, secondary, and tertiary flight-feather bands, bridge them with overlapping coverts, and preserve the thumb-feather group as a distinct controllable element when slow-flight reference exposes it
**ELSE** simplify the feather detail while keeping the same functional band hierarchy beneath the silhouette

## Do
- Place the longest primary feathers along the outer wing where Webster assigns the strongest propulsive role.
- Build the secondary feathers along the forearm as the broad lifting region of the wing.
- Use tertiary feathers to continue the surface from the upper arm toward the body.
- Layer coverts over the flight feathers so neighboring rows can slide while the wing keeps a comparatively smooth continuous surface.
- Preserve the small thumb-feather group, the alula, as a separate structure that can open during slow flight instead of merging it into the primary fan.

## Don't
- Do not distribute identical feathers evenly from shoulder to wingtip.
- Do not draw the entire wing as one rigid fan whose rows cannot overlap or slide.
- Do not place the primaries, secondaries, and tertiaries without regard to the wing segments that support them.
- Do not erase the alula when the pose or slow-flight action makes its separation relevant.

## Checklist
- The outer, forearm, and upper-arm feather regions are structurally distinct.
- Coverts bridge the bands without destroying their underlying organization.
- The feather rows form one continuous wing surface rather than disconnected decorative strips.
- Slow-flight construction can expose the alula without inventing a new attachment.

## Notes
Webster divides the large flight feathers into primary, secondary, and tertiary groups and gives each a different location and aerodynamic job. Coverts overlap those feathers and slide over one another so the changing wing keeps a smooth surface. He also identifies the thumb-feather group as a controllable slow-flight element used to help resist stalling. The durable drawing move is to construct the wing as layered functional bands before rendering individual feather texture.
