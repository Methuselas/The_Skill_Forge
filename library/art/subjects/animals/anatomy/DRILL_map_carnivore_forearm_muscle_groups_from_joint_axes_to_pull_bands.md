# Map Carnivore Forearm Muscle Groups From Joint Axes to Pull Bands

## Practice Task
Make a three-state lion or comparable carnivore forearm study: first map the functional groups over a front-view skeleton, then repeat them over a back-view skeleton, and finally remove the forearm bones and redraw only the essential origin-to-insertion pull bands around the elbow and carpal/digital axes.

## Target Skill
Reducing a carnivore forearm from a skeletal front/back study to a functional flexor-extensor band map and then to an origin-to-insertion abstraction without losing the joint-axis logic.

## Setup
Use one clear carnivore foreleg skeletal reference plus a reliable muscle reference for the same species. Draw the elbow and carpal joint levels large enough that both cross-axes and the forearm's longitudinal orientation can be marked. Use two distinguishable hatching directions, values, or annotation conventions for the flexor and extensor groups.

## Instructions
1. Draw the front-view humerus, radius, ulna, carpus, metacarpus, and major digits in simplified form. Mark the elbow and carpal cross-axes before adding muscle.
2. Lay in only the major carpal/digital extensor and flexor groups as broad directional bands. Keep each band continuous from its proximal origin zone through the forearm toward its distal tendon route.
3. Repeat the same study from the back. Do not mirror the first drawing mechanically; use the bone relationships to determine which group is actually visible and how its apparent width changes.
4. Compare the two views and deliberately mark the flexor group as the larger mass when the chosen carnivore anatomy supports that relationship. Check that the difference appears as three-dimensional volume, not just a darker label.
5. Make a third drawing with the forearm bones omitted. Keep only the proximal humeral origin regions, the elbow axis, the broad flexor/extensor pull bands, the carpal axis, and the distal metacarpal/digital insertion directions.
6. Put the skeletal drawing beside the abstraction and trace each band's route verbally or with a fingertip: origin zone, joint axis crossed, forearm path, distal tendon, insertion region. If a band cannot be reconstructed, restore only the missing skeletal landmark and try again.
7. Add a small runner comparison using a horse or cow foreleg: indicate where the equivalent distal route becomes long tendon and where the skeletal form begins to dominate. Keep this comparison schematic; it is a contrast test, not a second anatomy plate.
8. Finish by redrawing one forearm from memory as two or three functional masses over the radius-ulna construction. Then check it against the reference and correct only the wrong axis, side, or mass ratio.

## Success Check
- Front and back studies preserve the same joint levels and radius-ulna construction.
- Flexor and extensor groups cross the intended axes on coherent, continuous routes.
- The flexor-heavy mass relationship is visible in form when appropriate, not merely stated in text.
- The bone-free abstraction can still be expanded back into a plausible skeletal forearm because its origin, axis crossings, and insertion directions are explicit.
- Distal tendon paths continue toward the paw rather than ending at the carpus.
- The runner comparison makes the carnivore's retained forearm musculature easier to see without importing ungulate anatomy into the carnivore study.

## Common Failures
- Copying the labeled anatomy plate muscle by muscle without first marking the joint axes.
- Treating front and back views as flat mirrored color maps instead of the same three-dimensional forearm seen from opposite sides.
- Making the flexor and extensor bands equal merely for graphic neatness.
- Abstracting too early and losing which side of the elbow or carpal axis each group occupies.
- Letting the pull bands stop at the wrist instead of continuing through tendon routes toward the metacarpal and digital mechanism.
- Turning the runner comparison into a claim that all carnivores and ungulates share identical muscle attachments.

## Notes
Bammes's Figure 92 is valuable because it does not stop at a labeled anatomy plate. It moves from front and back skeletal views with functional color bands to a third diagram in which the forearm skeleton is removed and only the essential origin/insertion arrangement remains. That sequence trains a useful compression test: anatomy has been understood when the artist can remove detail without losing the mechanical route.

The dog and cow plates on printed p. 85 provide the comparative check used near the end of the exercise. The dog retains a more muscular distal forearm, while the cow sends the equivalent control through long tendons so the skeleton dominates. The contrast is used to sharpen the carnivore study, not to create a generic cross-species muscle map.
