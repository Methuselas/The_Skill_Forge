# Practice Wet and Dry Brush Animal Texture Swatches

## Practice Task
Build paired brush-and-ink swatches for a smooth glossy coat and a shaggy broken coat, then apply each treatment to a simple curved animal-form patch.

## Target Skill
Change coat character and value deliberately through stroke spacing, open paper, brush load, and pressure instead of using one generic fur mark everywhere.

## Setup
Use a brush, black ink, scratch paper, and drawing paper. Draw two or three simple curved patches or oval body masses before beginning the texture pass.

## Instructions
1. Load the brush and make a row of thin, close, even strokes. Keep some clean paper between or beside the strokes so the lightest areas remain open rather than being painted back in later.
2. Repeat the row on a curved patch, bending the stroke direction around the form instead of keeping every mark flat to the page.
3. Reload the brush, then work it on scratch paper until the tip is flatter and only lightly wet at the outer hairs.
4. Make a second row of dry-brush swatches. Change only one control at a time: first pressure, then remaining moisture, so you can see which change alters value and which change alters the broken edge.
5. Apply the dry-brush treatment to another curved patch, keeping the broken marks directional rather than scattering them as noise.
6. Alternate the two treatments several times until you can predict whether the next stroke will read smooth and glossy or shaggy and broken before it touches the final paper.

## Success Check
- Smooth swatches use close controlled strokes while deliberately leaving paper for lights rather than filling every gap until the surface loses its sheen.
- Dry-brush swatches break at the edge and across the stroke because of controlled moisture and pressure, not because marks were scattered as random noise.
- Pressure and remaining moisture are changed separately enough that their different effects on value and broken edge can be identified from the swatches.
- On the curved patches, both treatments turn with the form instead of keeping one flat page-direction that makes texture detach from volume.
- Before the final applications, the runner can state which prepared stroke should read glossy or shaggy and then check the actual mark against that prediction rather than accepting any texture after it lands.

## Common Failures
- Filling every light gap in the glossy treatment until the surface loses its sheen.
- Starting the dry-brush row with too much ink and getting solid black strokes instead of broken texture.
- Changing moisture, pressure, spacing, and direction all at once so the cause of each result cannot be learned.
- Making texture swatches that ignore the turn of the form when transferred onto the animal patch.

## Notes
Controlled swatches followed by small animal-surface applications isolate what wet and dry brush handling each contribute. Keeping brush load, pressure, spacing, and open paper explicit makes the exercise diagnostic rather than a collection of texture tricks.
