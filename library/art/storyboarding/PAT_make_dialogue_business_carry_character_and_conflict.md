# Make Dialogue Business Carry Character and Conflict

## Pattern Rule
**IF** characters are speaking through a storyboard scene
**THEN** give them physical business that grows from the scene objective, character, and relationship so the action performed during the lines also advances the dramatic beat
**ELSE** allow stillness when stillness is the clearest and most truthful behavior.

## Do
- Choose prop handling, seating, covering/uncovering, touching, withdrawing, looking away, preparing to leave, making oneself comfortable, creating a barrier, taking over space, or other business because it reveals objective, attitude, relationship, conflict, setup/payoff, or character.
- Let the same piece of business change meaning as the relationship changes.
- Use physical action to expose contradictions between what a character says and what the character is actually doing.
- Let business create opportunities for reaction, interruption, escalation, and payoff rather than serving as continuous filler motion.
- Keep the business readable enough at storyboard scale that its dramatic function survives rough drawing.
- Prefer actions that belong naturally to the location, props, and immediate task of the scene.

## Don't
- Do not add random fidgeting merely because a talking character seems too still.
- Do not make prop activity so elaborate that it upstages the dialogue or the intended focal character.
- Do not use business that can be removed without changing character, conflict, relationship, information, or comic/dramatic timing unless atmosphere alone is the explicit goal.
- Do not force constant movement when a pause, stare, hold, or refusal to act carries the stronger beat.

## Checklist
- The action has a clear reason grounded in the scene rather than a generic need for motion.
- The business reveals or changes something about objective, character, relationship, or conflict.
- The action remains understandable at storyboard-reading scale.
- Removing the business would measurably weaken the beat or relationship progression.
- The business supports rather than competes with the scene's focal priority.

## Notes
Bluth's *All Dogs Go to Heaven* blocking example repeatedly lets physical business carry story during dialogue: Itchy grabs the sideways book because the story offends his own values; Charlie pitches the book as his patience fails; a blanket becomes a literal barrier between Anne Marie and the adults; the kiss generates character-specific comic business; and the eventual seat exchange makes Charlie's defeat visible. Bluth's closing observation is that what the characters do while speaking can be more entertaining and revealing than the lines themselves. The durable rule is not to keep characters moving, but to make any movement earn dramatic function.
