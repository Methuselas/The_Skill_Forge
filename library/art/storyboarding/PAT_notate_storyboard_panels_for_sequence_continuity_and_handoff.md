# Notate Storyboard Panels for Sequence Continuity and Handoff

## Pattern Rule
**IF** storyboard drawings must be reviewed in sequence, reordered, timed, discussed, or handed to another person
**THEN** give every panel a consistent identity and only the production notes needed to interpret its place, action, camera, dialogue, and duration
**ELSE** keep private exploratory thumbnails free of unnecessary notation until they become part of the working board.

## Do
- Use a stable numbering/identity scheme that lets collaborators refer to one panel unambiguously even after discussion and revision.
- Record the dialogue, action, or descriptive cue needed to understand what the panel represents when the drawing alone would be ambiguous.
- Add camera instructions only when the shot behavior cannot be inferred reliably from the images in continuity.
- Add duration or timing information when it becomes a real sequence decision rather than inventing precision before the board is timed.
- Keep the notation format consistent enough that another person can interpret the board without learning a new convention from panel to panel.
- Inspect the annotated boards in continuity; use the sequence view to detect missing, redundant, or confusing images.

## Don't
- Do not preserve obsolete stationery, label positions, field sizes, or studio-specific paperwork as if they were the craft principle.
- Do not bury the drawing under notes that repeat information already clear in the image.
- Do not let numbering become so fragile that inserting or revising a panel makes existing discussion references unusable.
- Do not treat notation as a substitute for a board that visually communicates the action.

## Checklist
- Every working panel can be referred to unambiguously.
- Dialogue/action notes clarify rather than duplicate the image.
- Camera and timing notes appear only where they materially aid interpretation or handoff.
- A collaborator can read the panel order and identify what belongs before and after each image.
- Reviewing the sequence as a whole exposes gaps or redundancies that isolated panels would hide.

## Notes
A paper-board workflow may use fixed labels for sketch number, scene or sequence information, camera instructions, dialogue, and footage, but the portable lesson is not the physical template. Storyboards are collaborative sequence documents. Their notation has to preserve identity, order, and interpretable intent while the drawings are still being revised. The exact fields and interface can change with the production; consistent handoff logic should not.
