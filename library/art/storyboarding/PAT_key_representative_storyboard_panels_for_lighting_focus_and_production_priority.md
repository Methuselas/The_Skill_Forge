# Key Representative Storyboard Panels for Lighting Focus and Production Priority

## Pattern Rule
**IF** a storyboard sequence has survived rough staging and timing strongly enough that its lighting direction must be communicated for production
**THEN** select a small set of representative panels and key them strongly enough to establish focal priority, major light relationships, mood, silhouette/value organization, and where downstream production effort should concentrate instead of fully rendering every storyboard panel
**ELSE** keep early boards rough and use only enough value/light indication to make the intended audience emphasis understandable.

## Do
- Choose representative panels that cover the important lighting situations, focal changes, and major visual states of the sequence rather than selecting only the prettiest images.
- Establish the primary focal subject and enough primary/secondary light relationship that downstream artists can tell what must read first and what should remain subordinate.
- Organize the large light and dark masses so important figures, props, or story information separate clearly from the environment.
- Let focal priority migrate across the selected keys when the sequence's emotional or informational center changes.
- Use the lighting keys to communicate production emphasis: where acting, drawing, effects, color, or finish deserves the greatest care because that area carries the story.
- Keep the representative keys coherent with established geography, time of day, motivated sources, and the broader sequence tone.
- Use whatever focal arrangement best serves the shot: light-over-dark, dark-over-light, silhouette, rim light, reflection, local key, or another coherent contrast structure.

## Don't
- Do not polish every storyboard panel to final-render quality merely to communicate the lighting plan.
- Do not assume the brightest object must always be the focal subject when another contrast arrangement communicates the hierarchy more clearly.
- Do not give each representative key an unrelated lighting scheme simply because it makes an attractive standalone picture.
- Do not let a lighting key contradict established source direction, geography, time, or sequence continuity without a deliberate story reason.
- Do not let the lighting key invent story importance that the sequence itself does not support; use it to express the dramatic hierarchy already established.

## Checklist
- The chosen panels collectively represent the major lighting/focal situations of the sequence.
- Each key has a clear primary subject or story-information priority.
- Large light/dark relationships and silhouettes keep the intended action readable.
- Secondary material is visibly subordinated without becoming unintelligible.
- The keys communicate enough mood, source logic, and focal intent for downstream production decisions.
- Production emphasis follows story importance rather than rendering everything with equal care.
- The selected keys remain coherent with the approved board and animatic.

## Notes
Treat keyed storyboard panels as a bridge between an approved timed board and downstream cinematography or production, not simply as prettier boards. A few selected panels can establish where the viewer should look, how the scene's mood and light hierarchy behave, and which characters or effects merit the greatest production attention. Focal subjects can read through several different contrast structures rather than one fixed brightness formula. Key representative states after the sequence structure is working, while leaving rough boards inexpensive earlier in the process.
