# Use Small Color Keys as Shared Final-Look Targets

## Pattern Rule
**IF** a storyboard sequence, timing, and broad lighting strategy are stable enough that downstream artists need a shared picture of the intended finished look
**THEN** create a small set of representative color keys that establish the sequence's dominant palette, large value/light atmosphere, environmental mood, focal color emphasis, and any important effects color so production can work toward one coherent visual destination
**ELSE** keep the board and lighting exploration inexpensive until the sequence is ready to justify a final-look target.

## Do
- Choose representative moments that collectively cover the sequence's important palette, atmosphere, focal, and effects states rather than painting every storyboard panel.
- Keep the keys small and broad enough that large color/value relationships dominate over incidental rendering detail.
- Establish the dominant palette and enough light/color structure that backgrounds, effects, character color, and other downstream work can aim at the same visual world.
- Include story-critical effects behavior when glow, fire, rain, sparkle, shadow color, atmospheric color, or another effect materially defines the intended scene.
- Preserve focal color emphasis so the production target supports the same story hierarchy already established in the approved board.
- Compare neighboring keys as a sequence so the final-look targets remain coherent while still allowing intentional mood or gamut changes.
- Treat the keys as visual targets rather than literal finished frames; let final production solve animation, detail, rendering, and effects while preserving the approved large relationships.

## Don't
- Do not polish every storyboard panel merely to communicate the color plan.
- Do not let each department independently reinvent the same sequence's palette or atmosphere after a shared look has been approved.
- Do not optimize one attractive key in isolation while allowing adjacent moments to drift into an unrelated visual world.
- Do not confuse a color key with a literal final frame or demand exact local detail from a broad visual-development target.
- Do not let a color key contradict the approved staging, focal hierarchy, lighting direction, or story purpose without a deliberate revision to those earlier decisions.
- Do not treat one physical key size or one painting medium as mandatory; the durable requirement is compact, quickly readable final-look communication.

## Checklist
- The selected keys represent the important visual states of the sequence.
- Dominant palette and large value/light relationships are immediately legible.
- Mood and environmental atmosphere match the intended story effect.
- Focal color emphasis supports the approved narrative hierarchy.
- Important effects color or atmospheric behavior is represented where it materially affects the look.
- Neighboring keys feel like parts of one designed sequence unless an intentional shift is being staged.
- Downstream artists could use the keys to identify the intended visual destination without mistaking them for literal final frames.

## Notes
Bluth presents color keys as a compact bridge from approved storyboard/lighting decisions into production. The small paintings communicate what the finished movie should broadly feel and look like so background, effects, and other departments are not inventing separate answers from the rough boards. His examples show that the useful target is larger than palette alone: environment, illumination, weather, shadow, atmosphere, focal color, and production identity can all be carried by a small representative key. The durable storyboard operation is therefore to establish shared final-look targets after the sequence is stable enough to justify them, while keeping earlier boards inexpensive and revisable.
