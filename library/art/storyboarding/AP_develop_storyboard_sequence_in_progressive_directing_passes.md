# Develop a Storyboard Sequence in Progressive Directing Passes

## Objective
Author a storyboard sequence without trying to solve cutting, camera, information, lighting, and timing all at once, so each directing problem can be made clear before the next layer depends on it.

## Steps / Flow
1. **Read for scene purpose and cast, and reread before locking the first image.** Re-read the material until the characters, their core motives, the sequence purpose, and the intended audience response are understood well enough to state. Generate several possible visual responses rather than committing the first image that occurs. Capture fleeting possibilities immediately as tiny expendable thumbnails, including directly beside the script when useful. Use `PAT_define_image_story_job_before_visualizing` when the governing dramatic focus is still vague.
2. **Listen to recorded performance before deciding acting intensity when recorded dialogue exists.** Treat the actual delivery as evidence of how the line is being performed, not merely as later lip-sync or timing material. If the written direction suggests broad emotion but the actor delivers a quieter beat, board the performed intensity unless directing authority explicitly asks for another interpretation. Choose a framing scale that lets the important performance information actually read.
3. **Resolve only the setting information that affects continuity.** Establish the location, useful spatial relationships, entrances/exits, important props or furniture, and environmental character far enough that later shots are not being invented inside an undefined set. Do not finish-design the environment before the sequence needs it.
4. **Generate cheap board roughs.** Work small and fast. Treat the drawings as disposable visual notes whose job is to expose the sequence, not as presentation art. Keep characters recognizable enough for identity, attitude, and acting to read without requiring final on-model finish, and distinguish visual translation/staging from a substantive story rewrite that requires approval.
5. **Resolve complex choreography before fragmenting it into shots.** If a fight, dance, stunt exchange, or similarly interdependent movement sequence is not yet physically trustworthy, pause camera invention and use `PAT_build_complex_storyboard_choreography_on_performed_action_foundation` to establish a coherent performed/reference action first. Ordinary actions whose mechanics are already clear do not need this extra gate.
6. **First pass — cutting and camera angles.** Decide how the action is divided into shots and from where each beat is seen. Favor the simplest shot pattern that communicates the intended action and emotion; use `PAT_choose_viewpoint_to_strengthen_story_effect` when the camera choice itself carries the dramatic read.
7. **Second pass — information and visual focus.** Re-read the sequence for what the audience learns in each shot, what should remain withheld, and what visual emphasis makes the intended information readable. Add or adjust lighting/focus cues only as needed to support that information hierarchy.
8. **For action or chase material, map the causal beats before timing individual sketches.** Write a plain ordered list of the important story events before polishing shot solutions. Treat each beat as an event or change in the problem rather than a finished camera setup. Use `PAT_build_action_board_suspense_through_rising_jeopardy_and_reversal` when the action needs escalating jeopardy rather than repeated exchanges.
9. **Third pass — timing and pace.** After the shot order, viewpoints, information flow, and major beats are coherent, estimate how long each sketch or beat needs to register. Derive the estimate from what actually drives the moment: visualized action, recorded dialogue, or musical/rhythmic structure. Record the duration once it becomes a real board decision, but treat it as an initial value until assembled playback or animatic testing proves it. If a scene has made its statement, prefer cutting forward to padding the tail because unused drawings or frames happen to exist.
10. **Run an early scrutiny/story-meeting gate while the board is still disposable.** Pitch the rough sequence to informed fresh eyes before treating the cutting, camera, information, or acting choices as precious. Use `AP_review_and_revise_rough_storyboards_in_story_meeting` to test the actual conflict, character motivation and arc, audience information, subtext, and whether proposed fixes address the right problem. Revise, then rerun the directing passes as needed.
11. **Run a post-first-pass panel-value edit.** Once the sequence exists intuitively, use `PAT_earn_each_storyboard_panel_with_information_character_plot_or_tension` to challenge redundant sketches. Preserve panels that carry necessary information, character, plot/action, tension, or another explicit sequence job; combine, revise, or cut panels that add nothing. Keep this analytical pass separate from initial ideation so logic does not choke discovery.
12. **Externalize difficult spatial problems before overworking the boards.** When choreography, camera geography, or access inside the set is difficult to reason about in flat drawings, make a cheap spatial proxy first: a ground plan, camera-position diagram, simple miniature, or equivalent blockout. Audition useful camera positions against that proxy, then return to the rough sequence. Use the proxy to solve blocking and geography, not to finish-engineer the production before the story works.
13. **Inspect shot hook-ups and the sequence in continuity.** Read the boards in order rather than as isolated drawings. For cuts made during continuing action or camera motion, use `PAT_hook_storyboard_shots_through_continuing_action_and_camera_state` so direction, phase, velocity, spatial state, and camera state advance coherently through the boundary. Add, remove, reorder, or revise panels when a transition, action, or information handoff is missing or confusing.
14. **Expose production constraints after the story solution is strong enough to justify them.** Do not let downstream compositing, level breakdown, departmental mechanics, or cost anxiety strangle the exploratory board before the dramatic sequence exists. Before production lock, however, hand the selected solution forward for feasibility, registration, effects, lighting, asset, and other production planning so the board does not remain willfully impossible.
15. **Separate rough lighting intent from final-board lighting keys.** During early information/focus passes, indicate only enough value or light to make the intended emphasis readable. After the board has survived timed previsualization strongly enough that cinematography and downstream production need a clearer target, select representative panels and use `PAT_key_representative_storyboard_panels_for_lighting_focus_and_production_priority` to establish the sequence's focal lighting, mood, major light relationships, and production emphasis without fully rendering every board panel.
16. **Establish representative color/final-look targets when color and atmosphere materially define the sequence.** After the board has survived timed previsualization and the broad lighting/focus strategy is stable, use `PAT_use_small_color_keys_as_shared_final_look_targets` to create a compact set of representative color keys. Let them communicate the intended palette, atmosphere, focal color, and story-critical effects across the sequence without turning every storyboard panel into a finished painting. If the sequence does not depend materially on color or atmospheric development, this pass can remain minimal.
17. **Run a downstream-execution notation pass once the visual sequence is readable.** Give panels/scenes stable identities so insertions, deletions, continuations, and revisions remain traceable; identify speakers and distinguish visible dialogue from off-screen or voice-over speech when execution depends on it; record only necessary action/SFX information; represent camera movement across enough board states to make direction and progression legible; mark entrances, exits, continuation, transitions, and hookups when they would otherwise be ambiguous; and record reused artwork by reference to its authoritative source. Preserve the information relationship rather than any fixed studio panel template, color code, physical paper size, film-footage arithmetic, or numbering interval. Run an ambiguity check: another department should not have to reconstruct what the board artist meant.
18. **Hand the authored sequence forward to production with previsualization as the timing authority.** Use `AP_validate_sequence_in_previsualization_before_final_animation` to test the assembled timing, continuity, camera behavior, and performance before expensive production and whenever structural changes are made. If timing or shot structure changes materially after the representative lighting or color-key passes, revisit those keys before production lock so the shared visual targets still match the approved sequence.

## Notes
Bluth treats storyboarding as directing through successive decisions rather than a single drawing problem. Byrne adds two production-facing controls without changing that owner: listen to the recorded performance before committing acting intensity and shot scale, and after the sequence reads, run a concise notation pass so downstream production can identify shot state, camera movement, dialogue/action ownership, transitions, entries/exits, continuation, and reuse without reverse-engineering the board. His timing chapter also adds a useful upstream distinction: action beats should first exist as a causal event list, then receive drawings and initial durations from action, dialogue, or music before the animatic is asked to validate them. His practical solution is to reduce cognitive load: understand the cast and scene, rough the sequence, solve cutting and camera first, then examine the information and lighting, and only afterward concentrate on timing and pace. The pass order is not a prohibition against noticing later problems early; it is a way to prevent every board from becoming a simultaneous contest among too many unresolved decisions. The board stays inexpensive and revisable throughout. Bluth's story-meeting material adds an explicit scrutiny gate before production: outside reaction is useful when it helps identify the real dramatic problem, but the meeting should revise the diagnosis as readily as the drawings. His mechanics chapter adds two useful boundaries. First, when complex blocking or camera geography is the real obstacle, externalize the set with a cheap spatial proxy rather than guessing indefinitely in perspective. Second, keep early story exploration free enough that downstream engineering does not pre-censor the sequence, but expose the chosen solution to production feasibility before lock. Shot hook-ups are then checked at the board stage so continuing action and camera state survive cuts before downstream animation inherits them. Bluth's final-board lighting material adds a later distinction: rough boards need only enough light/value information to communicate emphasis, while representative keyed panels are most useful after timing and sequence structure have been tested, when they can communicate cinematography and production priority without forcing the entire board into finish rendering. His choreography chapter adds one conditional exception to the ordinary camera-first progression: when the underlying multi-body action is itself unresolved, establish a coherent performed/reference action before fragmenting it into storyboard shots, then return to the normal directing passes. His final color-key chapter completes the handoff by separating broad final-look communication from both rough boards and focal-lighting keys: once the sequence and lighting direction are stable, a small set of representative color keys can establish palette, atmosphere, effects color, and the shared visual destination for downstream production without repainting the entire board.
