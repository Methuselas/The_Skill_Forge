# Hunt Linear Compositions with Viewfinder Thumbnails

## Practice Task
Use a small rectangular viewfinder to scan an ordinary environment and make at least twelve tiny thumbnails that record only the strongest contour-and-space arrangements you discover.

## Target Skill
Finding potential compositions through framing and abstract spatial relationships instead of waiting for an obviously "picture-worthy" subject.

## Setup
Cut or improvise a small rectangular viewing aperture. Use a pocket sketchbook or one sheet divided into many thumbnail boxes. Work in a familiar place such as a room, yard, street corner, studio, or workshop so subject novelty does not do the compositional work for you.

## Instructions
1. Look through the viewfinder and move it until the frame creates an interesting division of positive and negative space.
2. Ignore object names and incidental detail. Record the dominant contours, overlaps, and large spaces in a thumbnail small enough that detail is inconvenient.
3. Shift position, height, crop, or orientation and repeat. Do not keep polishing one view.
4. Make at least twelve thumbnails; twenty is better when time permits.
5. Lay the set out together and mark the three arrangements that hold together most clearly at a glance.
6. For each selected thumbnail, state what makes it work: an overlap, dominant directional line, unusual spacing, unequal mass balance, strong negative shape, or another concrete relationship.

## Success Check
- The thumbnails are readable as arrangements even when the depicted objects are not obvious.
- The set contains genuinely different framings rather than small crop variations of one view.
- The strongest selections can be explained in terms of contour, space, overlap, or mass relationship.
- At least one useful composition came from a subject that initially seemed ordinary or unpromising.

## Common Failures
- Drawing objects carefully instead of studying the arrangement made by the frame.
- Choosing only conventionally attractive subjects and therefore never training composition discovery.
- Making every thumbnail from the same standing eye level and distance.
- Stopping after two or three attempts before comparison begins to expose preferences and habits.

## Notes
Use a small viewfinder to scan ordinary surroundings and record a dozen or more miniature linear arrangements. The target is not accurate object drawing but active discovery of contour-and-space relationships through framing. This differs from the same-scene camera-angle drill, which fixes one narrative beat and searches viewpoint for story effect; here the environment itself supplies the raw compositional material.

`VAR_guptill_compare_multiple_crops_of_one_reference_with_quick_value_trials` turns one fixed reference into a controlled crop-and-value comparison instead of scanning many different views.
