# Stage Interacting Characters as One Action-Reaction System

## Pattern Rule
**IF** two or more characters are meant to interact but read as separately posed figures that merely share the frame
**THEN** define the shared event, assign who initiates and who reacts, and coordinate each figure's gaze, weight, spacing, reach, withdrawal, and directional action around the same point of interaction so the group reads as one causal system
**ELSE** preserve independent actions when the story intentionally shows unrelated simultaneous behavior

## Do
- State the shared event before refining either figure: offering and refusing, greeting and hesitating, pulling and resisting, accusing and recoiling, or another specific action-reaction pair.
- Identify the physical or visual bridge that joins the characters, such as a handoff point, clasped hands, a shared prop, converging gaze, a contact line, or a clearly directed approach and retreat.
- Give the initiating figure an action that projects toward the event and give the responding figure a complementary reaction that answers it rather than duplicating the same pose.
- Coordinate body weight with the relationship. A character who advances, offers, presses, or reaches should commit differently from one who accepts, withdraws, doubts, braces, or resists.
- Use spacing and negative shape between the figures as part of the interaction. Open or compress the gap according to attraction, avoidance, pressure, uncertainty, or contact.
- Let hands, head turns, torso directions, and eye lines reinforce the same exchange so the viewer does not have to infer the relationship from dialogue or labels alone.
- Choose direct, intermittent, or deliberately avoided eye contact from the relationship and context rather than assuming constant mutual gaze is always correct. When the characters are meant to read as engaged with one another, make the gaze behavior responsive enough that the exchange does not become accidentally disconnected.
- Use gaze avoidance only as an intentional part of the relationship or performance and support it with other behavioral evidence; the meaning of eye contact varies with context and cultural convention.
- Keep each character's individual personality visible inside the shared event; two characters can participate in one action while reacting with very different intensity, confidence, or temperament.
- Identify whether the interaction is collaborative, shared, choreographed, momentary, or opposing/reactive before timing it; that relationship determines how tightly one participant must accommodate the other.
- In cooperative actions, moderate each participant's force, reach, and timing so the partner can complete the shared task; allow the share of effort to migrate when the task requires it.
- In opposing actions, make each participant respond continuously to the other's movement, resistance, pause, or withdrawal instead of letting both perform independently predetermined phrases.
- For exchanges such as throwing and catching, stage the initiating action so the receiving action remains physically possible at the intended moment and location.
- For multi-person lifting or carrying, treat the participants and shared object as one temporary support system: coordinate grip acquisition, weight acceptance, and the moment the object leaves or changes support; allow the load share to migrate between participants when geometry or timing requires it.
- Preserve small preparatory pauses or communication beats when coordinated participants need them to establish a shared grip or commit to the same lift rather than forcing simultaneous motion from the first frame.

## Don't
- Do not pose each figure independently and rely on proximity alone to imply that they are interacting.
- Do not make both characters perform the same directional action when the scene needs a readable initiator-response relationship.
- Do not let a shared prop float between figures without clear ownership, transfer, pressure, or attention from the bodies handling it.
- Do not over-coordinate the group into symmetry or mirrored gestures when unequal reactions would better express the relationship.
- Do not force a causal relationship into a scene whose point is that the characters are deliberately disengaged, unaware of one another, or occupied by separate actions.
- Do not force unbroken eye contact through every exchange or treat gaze avoidance as a universal code for one emotion, motive, or social meaning.

## Checklist
- The interaction can be named as one shared event rather than two unrelated pose descriptions.
- It is clear which character initiates, which reacts, or how the causal roles exchange during the moment.
- Gaze, hands, torso direction, weight, and spacing agree about where the interaction is happening.
- The negative space between the characters supports contact, approach, resistance, withdrawal, or another intentional relationship.
- The figures remain individually characterized while still reading as parts of one action-reaction system.
- Cooperative participants leave one another enough physical and temporal opportunity to complete the shared action.
- In a shared lift or carry, the object has a believable support path through the participants' grips and bodies before, during, and after the transfer of weight.
- Opposing participants visibly alter their actions in response to the other body rather than following isolated motion tracks.
- Removing dialogue or captions would not make the physical relationship between the characters disappear.
- Gaze behavior supports the intended relationship without becoming mechanically constant or accidentally severing an interaction that should feel engaged.

## Notes
A multi-character story moment is strongest when the figures behave as participants in one event rather than as separate drawings placed near each other. Stanchfield's interacting-character examples pair an initiating action with a distinct response: one figure offers or advances while the other reaches, hesitates, recoils, or otherwise answers. Webster extends that causal staging into time: collaborative actions constrain one participant so the other can succeed, while opposing actions require continuing response to the partner's force, motion, or lack of motion. In shared lifting and carrying, the participants plus the object become one temporary support system, so grip acquisition, weight acceptance, and changes in load share have to remain coordinated rather than being animated as independent actions. Webster also makes gaze a relational control rather than a fixed rule. Characters intended to engage one another need responsive eye behavior, but direct gaze, intermittent gaze, and avoidance can all be appropriate depending on the situation, and cultural context can change how those choices are read. The bodies, the space between them, gaze behavior, and any shared prop should all describe the same exchange. This differs from `PAT_route_group_composition_through_directional_paths_and_accents`, which organizes picture-wide directional flow across several subjects; this Pattern specifically governs the readable behavioral relationship between characters who are acting on one another.
