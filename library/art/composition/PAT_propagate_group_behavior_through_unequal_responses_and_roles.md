# Propagate Group Behavior Through Unequal Responses and Roles

## Pattern Rule
**IF** an acting group needs to respond to one event but reads like duplicated simultaneous motion or a collection of unrelated individuals
**THEN** establish the initiating member or subgroup, let the response pass through the group with unequal participation and timing, and preserve distinct roles, hierarchy, or skills so the collective action can spread, stall, amplify, diminish, or become collaborative
**ELSE** keep independent behavior when the story does not require one action to influence the group

## Do
- Identify what starts the group event: one person's action, an external incident, a shared cue, or a subgroup already in motion.
- Decide who responds first, who follows, who resists or ignores the event, and whether the response eventually becomes collaborative.
- Let different members participate to different degrees. A readable group does not require every individual to make the same gesture or exert the same effort.
- Preserve role differences when the group contains specialists, leaders, followers, antagonists, or members with unequal responsibility for the shared goal.
- Use small response delays when an action is meant to pass from one member to another rather than occurring as perfectly synchronized duplication.
- Allow the group to amplify or suppress individual behavior when the relationship supports that result; the presence of the group can change what one member is willing or able to do.
- When collaboration emerges, coordinate the complementary contributions instead of forcing everyone into one identical action.
- Keep enough individual characterization that the viewer can tell who is driving, following, hesitating, resisting, or contributing a specialized role.

## Don't
- Do not animate every member as an equally strong copy of the initiating action unless rigid synchronization is itself the intended group behavior.
- Do not assume that membership in a group erases personality, hierarchy, role, or unequal participation.
- Do not make a group reaction instantaneous everywhere when the event is supposed to spread through observation and response.
- Do not invent a leader-response chain when the event is genuinely simultaneous or when members are intentionally acting independently.
- Do not use one stereotyped gesture to stand in for a social, cultural, or demographic group.

## Checklist
- The event that initiates the group behavior is identifiable.
- Participation differs where the story, hierarchy, or role structure calls for it.
- A viewer can tell who initiates, follows, resists, ignores, or contributes a specialized action.
- Any spreading response has readable temporal progression rather than arbitrary delay.
- Collaborative action emerges from complementary contributions, not duplicated poses.
- The group still contains individual behavioral evidence instead of becoming one homogeneous symbol.
- The collective behavior supports the scene without relying on a fixed stereotype about the group itself.

## Notes
Webster observes that action can flow through a group: one individual acts, others respond in turn, and the result may eventually become collaborative. He also stresses that not every member participates to the same degree or at all, that groups can suppress or amplify behavior, and that team activity may depend on members with different roles and skills contributing toward one goal. The durable staging decision is therefore behavioral propagation with unequal response, not blanket synchronization. This Pattern extends `PAT_stage_interacting_characters_as_one_action_reaction_system` from direct participant exchange to a larger group whose internal response can travel through time. It differs from `PAT_route_group_composition_through_directional_paths_and_accents`, which organizes picture-wide visual flow rather than the causal spread of behavior.
