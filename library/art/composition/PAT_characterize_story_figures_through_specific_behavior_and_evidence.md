# Characterize Story Figures Through Specific Behavior and Evidence

## Pattern Rule
**IF** a story figure reads as generic, interchangeable, or like a posed model rather than a particular person in a particular situation
**THEN** add a small set of role-specific observable facts and natural behaviors that reveal age, occupation, condition, personality, relationships, or lived context while preserving coherent figure structure
**ELSE** keep characterization restrained when anonymity, archetype, uniformity, or neutral documentation is the intended effect.

## Do
- Choose models or references whose age, build, proportion, and physical type already support the role instead of asking surface styling to do all the work.
- Research specific visible facts that matter: age effects in hands and face, garment construction and wear, hairstyle, tools, accessories, posture, or environmental traces of the character's life.
- Give supporting figures plausible incidental actions so they appear occupied by the event rather than waiting for the illustrator.
- Let characters react to one another through gaze, gesture, spacing, weight, and task behavior.
- Use a few specific cues with high explanatory value rather than piling on decorative stereotypes.
- Preserve structural credibility while adding narrative evidence; characterization should grow from the figure, not sit on top of it.
- Separate stable character signature from temporary scene state: derive the baseline from relevant physical type, proportions, habitual posture, temperament, established mannerisms, and known behavioral evidence, then let the current mood, motive, and circumstance modify that baseline.
- When several established characters perform the same verb, compare whether their gestures still differ in ways that belong to those characters rather than collapsing into one generic performance.
- Check culturally learned gestures, signs, nods, postures, and body-language conventions against the character, situation, and intended audience before relying on them as semantic cues.
- When a prop matters to the scene, derive how the figure approaches, holds, uses, protects, avoids, leans on, displays, or otherwise handles it from the character's task, attitude, and story relationship to that object.
- Let prop handling reveal character evidence through contact, orientation, grip, spacing, posture, and use rather than treating the object as a neutral accessory.

## Don't
- Do not solve character merely by changing hair color, adding wrinkles, or attaching generic costume symbols to an otherwise interchangeable figure.
- Do not pose every secondary figure directly toward the viewer or freeze them into equal presentation poses.
- Do not use stereotype as a substitute for observed or researched evidence.
- Do not exaggerate distinguishing traits so far that anatomy, balance, or intended identity collapses unless deliberate caricature is the task.
- Do not assume a learned gesture or conventional body-language sign carries the same meaning across cultures or audiences.
- Do not place an important prop correctly in the scene while leaving the character's bodily relationship to it generic, passive, or contradictory to the task and story.
- Do not force a signature mannerism into every action when the story calls for restraint, neutrality, or a temporary state that should suppress it.

## Checklist
- At least one physical fact and one behavior or relationship cue support the character's role when the scene allows both.
- Costume, props, and environment provide evidence rather than decoration alone.
- Supporting figures have believable reasons for their poses.
- Character-specific cues do not depend on a single stereotype.
- Structural anatomy and action remain coherent after characterization is added.
- Any culture-dependent gesture used as characterization has an intentional, context-supported meaning for the intended audience.
- Any important handled prop has a readable character-action relationship, not merely correct presence or attractive placement.
- The figure preserves a recognizable stable character signature beneath temporary mood or circumstance when the character is already established.
- If two established characters could exchange the same acting pose without anything feeling wrong, test whether the performance is still too generic.

## Notes
Loomis treats characterization as a problem of specific facts and behavior rather than superficial differentiation. Stanchfield adds that a meaningful prop can function as behavioral evidence: how a character handles an object should reveal both the immediate task and the character's relationship to that task, rather than leaving the prop as generic set dressing. In Volume Two he further distinguishes the character's stable behavioral/physical signature from a temporary scene condition: physical type, proportions, habitual posture, temperament, and established mannerisms should bias how the same action is performed, while current mood and motive modify that baseline. His advice spans model choice, age-specific anatomy, clothing and accessories, incidental actions, and reactions between figures. This card differs from `PAT_intensify_distinguishing_characteristics_without_losing_structure`: that owner governs selective structural exaggeration, while this Pattern governs narrative specificity through observable evidence and behavior.

`VAR_loomis_choose_costume_for_character_and_value_mass_role` adds a compositional check to costume selection: clothing should remain believable for the person and story while also being judged as value, color, pattern, and mass inside the whole image.

`VAR_loomis_carry_emotion_through_whole_pose_and_hands` adds body-wide acting: emotional state should alter head attitude, spine, shoulders, hips, weight distribution, limbs, hands, gaze, and interaction as one behavioral system rather than being pasted onto a neutral pose as facial expression alone.

`VAR_dahlig_increase_emotional_intensity_through_muscular_tension` refines whole-body acting without requiring a new pose. Compare relaxed and tensed versions of the same basic stance and let visible muscular relationships change the intensity while the structural body remains coherent.

`VAR_stanchfield_preserve_character_mood_through_pose_to_pose_motion` carries characterization across time. Once a mood or movement character is established, the transitions between poses should continue to belong to that state until a story event motivates a definite change. Stanchfield also warns that learned body-language conventions are not universally semantic; use culture-dependent gestures only when their intended meaning is supported by the character, situation, and audience context.
`VAR_stanchfield_derive_physical_action_from_mood_personality_and_attitude` works in the opposite direction: identify the character state that should drive the event, then let the visible acting emerge from that cause instead of treating motion as generic mechanics. When outside-in assembly stalls, Stanchfield's play-acting idea becomes a bounded perspective-taking search: adopt what the character believes is happening and wants from the moment, generate the gesture from that situation, then return to structural and story checks.

`VAR_stanchfield_define_character_action_with_verb_and_adverb` adds a compact acting prompt. Name the action as a verb, specify its manner with one character-relevant qualifier, then convert that phrase into one or two visible consequences such as a cleared gaze path, projected head/neck, organized squash-versus-stretch, shifted support, or a matching timing choice. The verbal cue is only a design aid; the finished acting must communicate without requiring the explanation beside it.

`VAR_mattesi_infer_character_from_multiple_observed_moments` adds an observational route for discovering stable character signature. Compare several different moments of the same subject, preserve enough identity continuity to make the comparison meaningful, and look for recurring behavior in posture, gesture, movement, task handling, silhouette, and object use. Treat one-off deviations as temporary scene state unless repetition shows they are characteristic; carry the recurrent evidence into designed poses without averaging the subject into neutrality.

- Sequential character design benefits from stable silhouette and recognition anchors across changing poses and costumes; the audience should not have to stop and re-identify a recurring character.
- Coordinate head shape, body contour, costume, feature proportions, and accessories around a coherent shape tendency when that tendency supports the character’s personality.
