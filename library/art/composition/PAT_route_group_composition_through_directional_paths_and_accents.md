# Route Group Composition Through Directional Paths and Accents

## Pattern Rule
**IF** several active subjects are individually readable but the picture feels scattered, monotonous, or has no clear route for the viewer's eye
**THEN** organize the whole picture around a dominant line of action and a few supporting vertical, horizontal, diagonal, or curved directions; repeat or oppose those directions across subjects and setting, and use a deliberate accent or redirected path to break monotony and pull attention where it belongs

## Do
- Sketch the picture-level action paths before polishing individual animals. Let at least one major path continue from one subject into another instead of restarting the gesture at every body.
- Use direction families selectively. A strong vertical can hold a grouping together, a diagonal can lead from a limb toward another head or body, curves can carry movement, repetition can bind separated parts, and opposition can keep repeated motion from becoming parallel and flat.
- Redirect attention inside the group with a head turn or a secondary line of motion when one subject needs emphasis. Hultgren's deer grouping turns the mother's head toward the fawn while placing the fawn on another motion path.
- Let the setting participate in the same design. Ground grooves, banks, hair, reins, or other visible marks can reinforce the dominant movement or deliberately oppose it to strengthen the read.
- Reserve an accent for a useful interruption in the larger flow. Hultgren uses an isolated head or a small directional break to keep a long action path from becoming monotonous and to mark a focal stop.

## Don't
- Give every animal a convincing individual gesture while leaving the group as a pile of unrelated poses.
- Make every directional passage parallel or uniformly curved; repetition without opposition can flatten the picture's movement.
- Treat verticals, horizontals, and diagonals as a quota that must appear whether or not the staging supports them.
- Scatter equal accents everywhere. An accent should interrupt or redirect an established path, not create another competing path with no hierarchy.

## Checklist
- The eye can trace a dominant movement through the picture without having to restart at every subject.
- At least one repeated or opposing direction links separate subjects or links the subjects to the setting.
- Strong verticals, diagonals, curves, and ground directions have specific compositional jobs rather than existing as a checklist.
- A focal head, subject, or small accent interrupts the larger flow deliberately and is not lost among equal accents.
- The grouping still reads as one organized picture when reduced to a rough layout with action arrows and large shapes.

## Notes
Line-of-action thinking can extend from an individual animal to the entire picture. A foreground limb may act as a stabilizing vertical and lead toward grouped heads; another limb may form a diagonal toward a rear accent; gaze can carry action between separate animals; and terrain marks can oppose a subject's dominant angle. These are examples, not a fixed inventory of vertical, horizontal, and diagonal lines. Coordinate direction, repetition, opposition, and accent at picture scale so separate subjects behave as one composition.

`VAR_olofsson_balance_product_sheet_with_active_face_directions` treats each product's active face as an arrow in the page composition. Avoid accidental exits at the edge and avoid making every object aim at one rigid center; vary facing, size, and perspective so the group circulates attention while each view still serves its information job.

`VAR_mattesi_mass_crowd_as_one_directional_entity_before_individuals` makes crowd staging explicitly whole-to-part. First establish the crowd's overall mass, directional bias, and focal event, then use heads, backs, props, tables, spacing, and other broad vectors to carry attention through that mass. Only after the crowd reads as one organized entity should it be broken into individuals; vary their posture, pace, spacing, and actions enough to keep the group alive without losing the larger directional hierarchy.

- When several independent directional cues can support one focal point, align them deliberately so group orientation, gaze, body direction, and environmental vectors reinforce rather than compete.

`VAR_whitaker_prioritize_animated_group_motion_by_story_focus_and_depth` adds temporal hierarchy to group design. Let the focal performer carry the clearest motion; keep supporting figures responsive but quieter, reduce distant/background movement, and vary crowd phase, speed, cycle, or selected actions enough to avoid accidental synchronized motion. Foreground figures that are easy to inspect deserve more individualized performance than distant crowd members.
