# Physically Rehearse Action to Feel Weight Balance and Effort
## Pattern Rule
**IF** visual reference or memory does not reveal how an action organizes weight, balance, support, or effort
**THEN** safely act the movement out yourself at an appropriate scale, paying attention to where the body is supported, where its weight shifts, how the center of gravity travels, and where muscular tension or release is felt
**ELSE** use direct or recorded observation when the mechanics are already clear enough to analyze.

## Do
- Rehearse the action as the intended character or mechanical situation rather than merely imitating its outer silhouette.
- When useful, direct another performer or trade roles with a partner so the intended action can be tried, altered, and compared before animation begins.
- Use a mirror to combine kinesthetic evidence with visual inspection: a full-length mirror for whole-body mechanics or a smaller mirror for head, face, and mouth studies.
- When a prop affects the mechanics, use the real object or a safe surrogate close enough in size, weight, grip, and handling demand to expose the required body response.
- Notice when support changes from one foot, hand, or contact to another.
- Feel where the body must brace, lean, compress, extend, or redirect itself to remain balanced.
- Use the rehearsal to generate questions that can be checked against visual reference, not as a substitute for reference when accurate evidence is available.

## Don't
- Do not reproduce dangerous, painful, extreme, or load-bearing actions merely to obtain reference; substitute safe stand-ins when literal equipment or force would be risky.
- Do not assume your own body proportions, strength, age, or range of motion exactly match the subject being animated.
- Do not rehearse only as yourself when the intended subject's attitude, purpose, or mechanics materially change the action.
- Do not copy only the external pose while ignoring what the action feels like mechanically.

## Checklist
- The main support and weight-transfer points are identifiable.
- The action's center-of-gravity shift can be described in sequence.
- Major regions of tension, compression, or effort have a mechanical reason.
- Any performer, mirror, or surrogate prop used in rehearsal exposes the same constraint the target action must solve.
- The rehearsal has clarified at least one movement relationship that visual inspection alone did not resolve.

## Notes
Embodied rehearsal adds kinesthetic evidence to action analysis. It is most useful when the animator can see a movement but still cannot explain where the weight is, how balance is maintained, or why one part of the body leads another. The result should be translated back into the target character rather than copied literally from the performer.
