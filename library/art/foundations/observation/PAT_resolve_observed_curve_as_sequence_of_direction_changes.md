# Resolve an Observed Curve as a Sequence of Direction Changes

## Pattern Rule
**IF** an observed contour is drifting into a generic smooth arc that no longer matches the particular projection
**THEN** identify the contour's real directional changes, flats, turns, and inflections first, construct those relations explicitly, and smooth only as much as the observation warrants
**ELSE** keep the curve continuous when the source genuinely presents a simple continuous turn.

## Do
- Look for where the contour changes direction rather than naming the body part or object that the curve belongs to.
- Reduce a difficult curve temporarily to a sequence of straighter or less-curved intervals whose angles and lengths can be compared.
- Preserve distinctive flats, kinks, reversals, and asymmetries that prevent the contour from becoming a symbol.
- Reintegrate the segments into a continuous contour only after the directional structure agrees with the observation.

## Don't
- Do not round a cheek, skull, hip, hill, vessel, or other familiar form more continuously merely because the object is conceptually rounded.
- Do not replace many small observed changes with one elegant arc when those changes carry identity or structure.
- Do not keep the segmented construction visible in the finish when the intended contour should read continuously.

## Checklist
- The major direction changes correspond to the observed projection.
- Distinctive flats or inflections survive instead of being averaged away.
- Smoothing has not reintroduced the generic arc the construction was meant to correct.

## Notes
Curves are easy to symbolize because the mind recognizes the object before the eye has measured the particular contour. Treating a difficult curve as a chain of direction changes makes it comparable and correctable, then allows continuity to be restored without sacrificing the specific projection.
