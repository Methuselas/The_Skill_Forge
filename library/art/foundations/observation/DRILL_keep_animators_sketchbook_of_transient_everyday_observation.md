# Keep an Animator's Sketchbook of Transient Everyday Observation

## Practice Task
Keep a low-stakes sketchbook or equivalent capture surface readily available and use it to make frequent, concise records of ordinary transient subjects. Treat each entry as a rapid selection problem: preserve what made the observed moment worth noticing before detail or finish can replace it.

## Target Skill
Rapidly selecting and recording the dominant action, character, spatial relation, or compositional impression from ordinary transient experience before it disappears.

## Setup
Use a sketchbook or capture surface inexpensive and convenient enough that protecting pages does not discourage use. Work from passing everyday subjects rather than only formal model sessions: people, animals, vehicles, interiors, landscapes, sports, performances, or other situations that may change before a finished drawing is possible.

## Instructions
1. Before marking, identify the main reason the subject caught your attention: an action, attitude, personality cue, spatial arrangement, shape relationship, or other dominant impression.
2. Record that whole impression first with economical marks. Do not begin by finishing an isolated head, limb, vehicle part, or decorative detail while the event is still changing.
3. When composition matters, establish a simple rectangular frame and relate the important masses to one another and to the borders rather than letting the subject float without context.
4. When depth is part of the observation, preserve enough ground plane, overlap, scale change, or environmental relationship to retain the spatial situation.
5. Deliberately broaden the practice. Alternate subject classes, viewpoints, distances, and situations instead of repeatedly choosing only familiar three-quarter figures or comfortable poses.
6. Add a short written note only when it preserves something the drawing alone cannot retain, such as a behavior, expression, idea, question, or later-use cue. Keep the note subordinate to the visual record.
7. Leave earlier entries intact. If a later observation changes your interpretation, make a new entry rather than repairing the old one into a record of what you wish you had noticed.
8. Periodically run a controlled comparison: choose one ordinary verb such as walking, opening a door, using a key or pen, picking something up, or taking a drink, then observe several different people performing it under different circumstances. Record differences in tempo, confidence, posture, body language, tool handling, and context rather than searching only for spectacular actions.
9. Periodically review a group of entries and name one recurring blind spot and one useful observation. Let the blind spot determine a targeted study or the next kind of subject you seek out.

## Success Check
- Each entry preserves the dominant action, attitude, personality cue, spatial relation, or other reason the moment was worth noticing even when the sketch is materially incomplete.
- When ground plane, depth, or surrounding context matters, enough of it survives to keep the event spatially legible rather than leaving a polished isolated fragment floating without situation.
- Across an accumulated group, subjects, viewpoints, distances, and situations vary materially instead of remaining inside one repeated comfort zone. A first sitting may leave this condition open; planned variety is not the same as an accumulated record.
- Written notes preserve behavior, expression, questions, or later-use information the drawing cannot retain rather than narrating contours already visible on the page.
- A controlled ordinary-verb comparison actually records several performers or circumstances and exposes meaningful variation instead of collapsing the action into one generic symbol from memory.
- Periodic review of accumulated entries names one recurring blind spot and one useful observation to carry forward. This condition cannot close until a real group exists; intending to review future pages is not evidence from a review.

## Common Failures
- Turning every entry into a miniature finished drawing and missing the transient event.
- Drawing only convenient or familiar subjects and viewpoints.
- Recording isolated anatomy or object detail while losing the action, character, or situation that made the subject interesting.
- Writing extensively instead of making the visual observation.
- Chasing only spectacular or athletic actions while neglecting ordinary actions that may be more directly useful in animation.
- Treating page count as proof of improvement without reviewing what the sketches repeatedly miss.
- Retrospectively correcting old entries until they no longer record the original observation.

## Notes
Stanchfield treats the animator's sketchbook as continuing observational practice rather than a collection of polished drawings. The useful pressure comes from ordinary life changing faster than a formal study can be completed: the artist has to select the action, personality, spatial relation, or compositional impression that matters and record it economically. Webster adds a useful comparison mode: ordinary repeated actions can be more revealing than spectacular ones because differences in tempo, confidence, posture, tool handling, and circumstance become easier to compare. Analytical entries remain low-stakes; their primary value is what the observation teaches, not whether each page becomes a polished drawing. Stanchfield's note-taking advice extends the same practice to fleeting ideas and behavioral observations, while preserving earlier impressions as evidence rather than continually revising them into cleaner records.
