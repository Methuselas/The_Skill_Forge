# Design the Pose Against Its Center of Gravity

## Pattern Rule
**IF** a figure's gesture needs believable support, imbalance, or impending movement
**THEN** establish the center-of-gravity relationship and the actual support/contact base, then place the head, rib cage, pelvis, and supporting limbs so their offsets either balance the figure or create intentional recoverable tension
**ELSE** when the figure is suspended, seated, braced on an object, or supported through the arms, define the real support system instead of forcing a standing-leg model

## Do
- Treat balance and imbalance as design variables that serve the pose's story.
- Identify the primary weight-bearing contact before judging whether the torso and pelvis can lean as drawn.
- Let a real support contact change the pose that bears on it: compression, settling, countershift, joint angle, and adjacent mass relationships should propagate from the contact instead of leaving an unchanged mannequin balanced on a point.
- Use counter-balancing among head, rib cage, pelvis, and limbs rather than stacking all major masses on one vertical.
- For a mid-step or impending-action pose, let the mass arrangement approach or cross a stable relationship while another contact is positioned to catch, redirect, or complete the action.
- Check the pose at gesture level before anatomy hides the support logic.
- Mentally remove an external support such as a rope, wall, pole, hand brace, or suspension point and predict the direction the body would fall or rotate; verify that the real support plausibly resists, redirects, or arrests that tendency.
- When the figure carries a significant external mass, evaluate the combined body-plus-load center rather than the unloaded body's ordinary plumb; a rear load may require forward compensation, an asymmetric side load may require lateral compensation or a free-arm counterbalance, and a centered front load may permit a more symmetrical response.

## Don't
- Do not center every major mass over one vertical merely because the figure must ultimately be supportable.
- Do not make a pose dynamically off-balance without a believable next support, brace, suspension, or momentum state.
- Do not assume a balanced pose is inferior; stability may be exactly what the intended story requires.
- Do not repair unclear weight distribution with contour, muscle detail, clothing, or shadow.

## Checklist
- The actual support/contact base is identifiable.
- The major masses have a deliberate relationship to the gravity line rather than an accidental one.
- A stable pose reads as supported; an unstable pose reads as intentionally moving toward another support condition.
- Removing anatomy and contour does not destroy the sense of weight.
- Weight-bearing contacts produce believable structural consequences in the masses and joints they support.
- If an external support is removed mentally, the predicted fall or rotation agrees with the load that support is actually carrying.
- When a carried load is significant, the combined figure-and-load mass remains believable over or against the current supports.

## Notes
Hampton frames human movement as repeated controlled loss and recovery of balance. The useful runtime decision is not “make every pose unstable,” but to know whether the current arrangement is stable, tensioned, or transitioning and to design the support accordingly.
Mattesi adds a useful counterfactual support test: temporarily remove an external brace or suspension in the analysis, predict the body’s fall or rotation, and use that result to verify that the stated support is mechanically doing the job the pose claims.
