# Practice Deliberate Brush-Line Control

## Practice Task
Make controlled straight-direction strokes, curves, and irregular lines with continuous whole-arm movement, then apply the same control in a tiny design.

## Target Skill
Guide a long expressive line continuously instead of assembling it from small local corrections.

## Setup
Use a brush, pen, or other tool that makes the stroke clearly visible. Work large enough that finger motion alone is insufficient and keep enough room to start fresh lines beside failures.

## Instructions
1. Charge or prepare the tool so one stroke can travel the intended distance without repeated patching.
2. Draw several long straight-direction lines slowly enough to watch the mark grow and steer its direction.
3. Repeat with broad curves, then with intentional irregular turns while preserving continuous motion.
4. Vary pressure or tool angle only when it serves a chosen width or character rather than as accidental wobble.
5. Copy a small passage from a strong line drawing for control and quality of touch, not for subject accuracy.
6. Finish by making a tiny abstract or representational design that uses the same deliberate line control in context.

## Success Check
- Long straight-direction strokes hold a chosen course as single continuous marks rather than reaching it through many finger-sized corrections or repeated patching.
- Broad curves and intentional irregular turns remain continuous; wobble caused by hesitant steering does not count as designed irregularity.
- Changes of pressure or tool angle correspond to a chosen width or character instead of appearing as uncontrolled fluctuations along the stroke.
- The copied passage is judged by quality of touch and mark behavior rather than by how accurately its subject was reproduced.
- Failed strokes remain visible as separate attempts or are replaced beside the first mark rather than repaired into fuzzy bands.
- The final tiny design uses the same control in context, so success is not limited to isolated warm-up lines.

## Common Failures
- Steering the mark through many finger-sized corrections that break its continuity.
- Drawing so quickly that momentum replaces observation and control.
- Repairing one failed stroke repeatedly instead of making a fresh attempt beside it.
- Spending so long on isolated penmanship that the controlled line never returns to design.

## Notes
The exercise treats slow movement as active control rather than hesitation. Whole-arm travel gives a long line room to develop; slight organic variation is acceptable when direction, width, and expressive purpose remain deliberate.
