# Project Form Curves From the Camera View

## Pattern Rule
**IF** a familiar torso or limb connection changes appearance in a high, low, turned, or deeply foreshortened view
**THEN** derive its visible overcurves, undercurves, compressions, and dominant masses from the unchanged three-dimensional form as seen from that camera position
**ELSE** use the simpler familiar projection only when the view genuinely supports it

## Do
- Decide which mass is nearer to the eye before choosing the visible curve sequence.
- Let low views expose upward vaults and overcurve progressions where the form warrants them, and let high views reveal compressed undercurves where the same structure turns away.
- Track the natural route around the volume rather than forcing a remembered front-view anatomy map onto the new angle.
- Make every later contour, cross-contour, anatomical edge, attachment, and visible surface graphic inherit the same surface turn and camera logic; a line added for anatomy or decoration is not exempt from the parent form.
- Project garment stripes, seams, bands, grids, and other wrapping surface patterns across the solved volume: let their curvature, compression, spacing, and apparent width change with the form and camera instead of pasting them flat onto the silhouette.
- Compare several viewpoints of the same form so the underlying structure remains stable while its projection changes.
- Treat the visible outer contour as a camera-dependent silhouette event: when perimeter copying starts to flatten the figure, re-establish interior centers, cross-surface paths, overlaps, and volume turns before returning to the silhouette.

## Don't
- Treat overcurve and undercurve as permanent labels attached to a body part.
- Define the direction by the top or bottom of the canvas; the drawing surface records the view but does not cause the form.
- Reverse a curve automatically because the pose has been rotated on the page.
- Infer a permanent anatomical ridge, boundary, or surface path merely because it currently coincides with the silhouette; a viewpoint change can move the contour without changing the underlying volume.

## Checklist
- The selected curve agrees with which surfaces face, turn from, or disappear from the camera.
- A high and low view look like projections of the same underlying body rather than two different constructions.
- Rotating the canvas alone would not change the structural explanation.
- Added contour, anatomy, and surface-pattern lines reinforce the established surface orientation instead of flattening or contradicting it.
- In strong foreshortening, wrapping graphics support the near/far size sequence and turning of the volume without being distorted merely to manufacture depth or conceal a faulty underlying mass.
- If the viewpoint changes, the silhouette can change substantially while the constructed volume remains recognizably continuous.

## Notes
Hogarth contrasts high- and low-angle torso views to show the same anatomical masses producing different visible curve families. The guided review clarified the governing model: the eye behaves as a camera in three-dimensional space; the body remains the body, while perspective changes what its natural forms reveal.

`VAR_loomis_square_organic_masses_then_round_to_projection` adds a temporary diagnostic squaring pass for difficult figure views: solve the camera-facing planes first, then round the anatomy back out without losing the perspective decision. Visible stripes, seams, bands, and similar graphics should then be projected as surface paths on that same solved form; they can reinforce foreshortening, but they must not be used to fake depth or hide incorrect construction.

`VAR_mattesi_sculpt_forceful_form_with_surface_lines` turns those camera-correct surface paths into selective sculpting marks: anchor them to the solved volume, let them wrap rather than cut inward, and choose their direction so they can simultaneously show roundness, plane change, pressure, stretch, or the active force through that region. Keep the line economy high and never let expressive force contradict the parent form's perspective. A visible silhouette is likewise a projection of the volume from the current camera, not an intrinsic structural line; when copying the perimeter hides weak construction, recover the form from interior routes and surface turns first.
