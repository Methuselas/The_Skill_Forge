# Build a Visual Idea Around Audience Motive or Friction

## Pattern Rule
**IF** an image is meant to provoke a response from a defined audience rather than merely identify a subject
**THEN** build the concept around a relevant audience goal, desire, concern, frustration, aspiration, or problem that the subject can visibly affect
**ELSE** let the subject or story carry the concept without inventing an audience motive that the assignment does not need.

## Do
- Ask what the subject enables somebody to do, avoid, gain, protect, become, understand, or feel rather than stopping at its literal features.
- Ground the motive in the actual brief, audience research, observation, or defensible context.
- Convert the motive into a visible human situation, consequence, contrast, or behavior that can be understood pictorially.
- Prefer specific friction and consequence over vague claims such as "people want success" when the assignment supports something more concrete.
- Check that the emotional or motivational appeal arises credibly from the subject and situation; if it feels dragged in merely to intensify response, remove it or reframe the concept.

## Don't
- Do not treat period stereotypes, demographic clichés, or personal acquaintances as universal audience psychology.
- Do not assume one motive applies equally to every viewer in a broad category.
- Do not add sentiment that the pictured situation has not earned.
- Do not substitute emotional pressure for a clear visual relationship between the audience concern and the subject.

## Checklist
- The intended audience or viewing context is identifiable.
- The selected motive is supported by the assignment rather than guessed as a universal instinct.
- The concept shows a consequence, use, friction, or aspiration rather than only naming a feature.
- The emotional appeal feels credible inside the pictured situation.
- Removing the audience motive would materially change the idea, not just its caption.

## Notes
Loomis's "basic appeals" material is most useful when stripped of its period claims about universal psychology. The portable craft move is audience-centered ideation: find a relevant human motive or friction and make that relationship visible. His later warning about forced sentiment sharpens the rule—an appeal must ring true inside the actual subject and situation rather than being pasted onto it for effect.
