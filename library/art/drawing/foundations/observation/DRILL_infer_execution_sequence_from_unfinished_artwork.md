# Infer Execution Sequence From Unfinished Artwork

## Practice Task
Analyze unfinished or intermediate artworks as process evidence, infer the likely order of operations, and test those inferences against later states when available.

## Target Skill
Recognize what an intermediate image can and cannot reveal about workflow: preparatory versus resolved passages, overlap and coverage order, likely next operations, and competing process hypotheses.

## Setup
Choose several works for which at least one unfinished or intermediate state is visible. Prefer examples that expose different amounts of underdrawing, block-in, resolved finish, scraping, repainting, or local completion. A later state is useful but not required.

## Instructions
1. Separate visibly preparatory marks from passages that already appear intended to survive into the finish.
2. Inspect overlaps, coverage, edge intersections, changes in opacity, and differences in resolution to infer which operations probably preceded others.
3. Mark the areas whose evidence is ambiguous and write at least one alternate sequence when the image does not prove a unique process.
4. Predict the next logical operation needed to advance the work without assuming that every unfinished area must be completed uniformly.
5. When a later state exists, compare it with the prediction. Record which process clues were reliable and which were misleading.
6. Repeat across different artists, media, and starting systems so the exercise trains evidence reading rather than one habitual workflow.

## Success Check
- The inferred sequence is tied to visible evidence such as overlap, coverage, edge state, or resolution.
- Ambiguous passages retain alternate explanations instead of being converted into false certainty.
- Predictions distinguish preparatory information from passages that were already functioning as finish.
- Later-state comparison improves future workflow inference rather than merely rewarding guesses.

## Common Failures
- Reading a polished final state backward and inventing a precise historical procedure unsupported by intermediate evidence.
- Assuming unfinished means uniformly early; some passages may already be complete while others remain only blocked in.
- Treating one artist's visible sequence as a universal workflow.
- Confusing subject construction with execution order; this drill studies process evidence, not merely the objects represented.

## Notes
Unfinished work reveals decisions that a polished surface often conceals. The useful skill is not historical certainty but disciplined process inference: distinguish what the visible state proves, what it merely suggests, and what remains unknowable, then use that distinction to understand how a finished result could be staged efficiently.
