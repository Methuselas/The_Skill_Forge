# Attach Joint Overlap to the Advancing Member

## Pattern Rule
**IF** two bent cylindrical members are seen frontally and their joint projection must declare which member advances in depth
**THEN** interlock the joint's decisive contour and overlap with the advancing member while preserving one continuous anatomical chain
**ELSE** use the ordinary joint transition when no front-to-back ambiguity exists

## Do
- Decide which shank is nearer before drawing the knee, elbow, or knuckle boundary.
- Let the near member receive the joint projection so the rear member recedes cleanly behind it.
- Apply the same spatial test to fingers, where each knuckle mediates between two bent phalanges.
- Read the result as a contour and overlap solution rather than a transfer of anatomical ownership.

## Don't
- Lock the joint visually to the receding member when the nearer shank clearly advances.
- Draw the joint as an independent bead between two disconnected cylinders.
- Treat the phrase "gets the joint" as though the knee, elbow, or knuckle ceased to belong to the whole limb.
- Accept a swollen, cracked, or spatially contradictory bend because each local mass looks plausible alone.

## Checklist
- The nearer member and joint read as one advancing unit.
- The receding member passes behind without overriding the near form.
- The limb or finger remains one continuous structure through the bend.
- Reversing which member advances produces a corresponding and intentional change in the joint contour.

## Notes
Hogarth's joint interlock is a visual depth rule. The guided correction protects the Chapter 3 continuity lesson: a joint does not become a detachable connector or the property of one segment. The overlap merely tells the eye which side of the continuous chain is advancing.
