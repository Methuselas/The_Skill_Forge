# Resolve Unintended Tangencies With Overlap or Separation

## Pattern Rule
**IF** independent shapes merely kiss, align, or terminate together in a way that creates a false attachment, false depth relationship, or accidental focal event
**THEN** decide the intended relationship and make it explicit through overlap, separation, a changed contour/viewpoint, or reframing
**ELSE** preserve the tangent when the coincidence itself is intentionally being used for ambiguity, humor, flattening, or pattern

## Do
- Inspect internal contacts and frame-edge contacts at thumbnail scale as well as close up.
- Ask whether two touching edges are meant to connect, overlap in depth, or remain separate.
- Create a decisive overlap when one form should pass in front of another.
- Create visible separation when the forms should remain independent.
- Shift viewpoint, placement, contour, or crop when a local nudge cannot solve the false relationship cleanly.

## Don't
- Leave accidental edge kisses because each object looks correct in isolation.
- Correct a tangent by adding arbitrary detail that does not clarify ownership or depth.
- Treat all tangencies as forbidden; deliberate visual coincidences can be powerful design devices.
- Ignore the page edge as a possible tangent partner.

## Checklist
- Accidental contacts do not imply false anatomy, support, attachment, or depth.
- Important silhouettes remain readable.
- Frame-edge terminations look intentional.
- Any surviving tangent serves a deliberate compositional purpose.

## Notes
Dodson's examples show elbows, heads, windows, mountains, vehicles, and page edges creating accidental shared boundaries. The durable decision is not "avoid all tangents" but "notice the coincidence and decide whether it should read as connection, overlap, separation, or intentional ambiguity."
