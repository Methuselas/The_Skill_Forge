# Concentrate Contrast and Accents at the Focal Area

## Pattern Rule
**IF** a picture has a chosen focal area but the viewer's attention is distributed too evenly
**THEN** concentrate the strongest local value or color contrast and supporting directional or repeated accents near that focal area while subordinating competing emphasis elsewhere
**ELSE** maintain a more even field when the design intentionally has no dominant focal hierarchy

## Do
- Decide what should be seen first before intensifying local contrast.
- Use surrounding directions, repeated accent colors, or light-dark framing to reinforce the same priority rather than creating unrelated competing spots.
- Let secondary accents echo the focal area at lower strength when balance needs them.
- When only one product component or adaptation is being judged, keep enough of the whole product visible to assess fit, but suppress surrounding line, color, contrast, or detail so the active part carries the strongest resolution.
- Keep the chosen focal area as the comparison standard while inspecting the rest of a large subject; later-inspected regions should not inherit full local sharpness merely because attention temporarily moved there.
- Build focal dominance across the few axes that matter—value/color contrast, edge sharpness, drawing specificity, detail, or visual interest—while reducing those strengths in competitors; all axes do not need to peak together when one or two already establish the hierarchy.

## Don't
- Do not distribute every strong contrast evenly across the picture.
- Do not use a symbolic color as a universal emotional rule; context controls what an accent means.
- Do not let sequential observation create several accidental focal centers by rendering every newly inspected region at its momentary maximum clarity, detail, and contrast.

## Checklist
- The intended focal area wins at a glance.
- Secondary accents support or echo the hierarchy without stealing first attention.
- Removing local detail does not destroy the large emphasis pattern.

## Notes
Mattesi's animal-design examples coordinate value, color, orientation, and surrounding marks so the chosen head, face, or forward form receives the strongest attention. The same decision is portable to character and creature concept work. `VAR_dodson_spend_observation_and_detail_by_focal_priority` adds attention time and detail density as another way to subordinate secondary areas while the focal area receives the sharpest observation. `VAR_loomis_reserve_peak_chroma_against_greyed_support_colors` adds a color-hierarchy route: support a limited area of clean or strong chroma by muting competing color elsewhere rather than saturating the entire picture. It also treats scale as part of chroma strength: a broad area can dominate at lower saturation than a small accent needs.

A design-study focal hierarchy can be functional rather than dramatic. Resolve the part being judged and keep the rest of the product quieter but still present; the viewer should see both the part and whether it belongs to the whole.

`VAR_guptill_build_focal_contrast_with_graded_surround` builds a local contrast peak at the focal area and grades surrounding competition downward rather than scattering equally strong accents.

Focal dominance can be distributed across several visual axes rather than maximized mechanically on all of them. A focal passage may lead through contrast and edge while drawing specificity stays restrained, or through specificity while value contrast remains modest, as long as competitors are subordinated accordingly.
