# Route Viewer Attention Through Planned Visual Paths

## Pattern Rule
**IF** the important content is present but the viewer's attention enters, wanders, exits, or competes in ways that weaken the intended read
**THEN** design a deliberate visual route through the picture—entry, focal stop or stops, continuation, and optional exit—using directional lines, curves, diagonals, perspective convergence, object orientation, repeated accents, value/shape junctions, or other visible cues; then redirect accidental exits and subordinate unintended competing routes
**ELSE** preserve a simpler or more open attention structure when ambiguity, diffusion, or multiple equivalent readings are intentional.

## Do
- Decide what should be encountered first, what should be discovered next, and where attention may safely leave or loop back.
- Let visible structures do the routing: perspective lines, limbs, gazes, architecture, curves, repeated accents, overlaps, value boundaries, and directional shapes can all hand the eye from one region to another.
- Treat a focal point as a stop within a route rather than an isolated target. A strong composition can move through several intentionally ranked stops.
- Inspect lines and edges that run toward the frame. If they eject attention before the intended read is complete, interrupt, bend, overlap, darken/lighten, or counter them with another cue that returns attention.
- When several plausible routes exist, establish hierarchy among them instead of assuming every branch is a defect.
- Reduce the picture to a rough directional map and check whether the intended path still reads without detail.

## Don't
- Do not make every line literally point at the primary focal point; over-routing becomes mechanical and obvious.
- Do not prohibit branching paths. Multiple routes are legitimate when one is primary or their equivalence is intentional.
- Do not scatter equal-strength accents along unrelated routes and then expect the eye to know which sequence matters.
- Do not trap the viewer indefinitely inside the frame when a clean exit supports the intended cadence.
- Do not use arrows or directional devices independently of subject logic merely to force movement.

## Checklist
- There is a plausible entry into the picture or an intentionally immediate focal landing.
- The eye can move from that entry to the primary point of interest without fighting major contradictory cues.
- Secondary stops are ranked rather than accidentally equal.
- Strong frame-directed lines either serve a deliberate exit or are redirected before they dump attention prematurely.
- The route survives when detail is reduced to major lines, masses, and accents.
- Any competing route is intentional and appropriately subordinate, rather than an unresolved fork.

## Notes
Hultgren's original foundation moved line-of-action thinking from individual animals to the entire group, coordinating repeated and opposing directions, accents, and setting marks so separate subjects behave as one picture. Loomis broadens the same mechanism beyond group scenes: physical PDF p. 39 / printed p. 47 explicitly asks the artist to provide a planned pathway for the eye, catch lines that lead out of the subject, entertain the viewer with points of interest, and allow a pleasing continuation or exit. PASS therefore generalizes the owner from group composition to whole-picture attention routing. Loomis's warning against giving the eye “two ways to go” is retained only as a diagnosis of accidental competition; branching and multiple focal stops remain valid when hierarchy is controlled.
