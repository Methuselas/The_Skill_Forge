# Choose Line and Shape Character to Support Emotional Intent

## Pattern Rule
**IF** a composition is structurally readable but its dominant line and shape language feels emotionally neutral, generic, or contradictory to the intended effect
**THEN** name the emotional character you need and audition line/shape families whose directional, rhythmic, angular, curved, stable, compressed, expansive, radiating, spiraling, formal, or irregular qualities reinforce that intent; judge the result in the actual picture rather than treating any geometry as a fixed emotional code.

## Do
- State the intended feeling or character before changing the geometry: calm, severity, instability, grace, confinement, grandeur, agitation, playfulness, or another concrete target.
- Compare a few low-cost structural alternatives. Change the dominant curves, diagonals, angles, repeated shapes, or directional rhythm while holding the subject idea substantially constant.
- Let the dominant structural language influence subsidiary forms so the picture feels designed as a whole rather than decorated with one symbolic shape.
- Evaluate the emotional read together with story, scale, viewpoint, value, color, and cultural context.
- Preserve structural clarity and subject recognition while shaping the line/shape character.

## Don't
- Do not memorize a universal dictionary such as “triangle always means danger” or “circle always means calm.”
- Do not force every object into the same geometric motif until the picture becomes mannered or unreadable.
- Do not use shape symbolism as a substitute for hierarchy, staging, value organization, or believable construction.
- Do not assume an emotional association transfers unchanged across styles, cultures, narratives, or scales.

## Checklist
- The intended emotional character can be named before the shape-language choice.
- The dominant lines/shapes support rather than contradict that intent.
- The picture still reads when reduced to its main directional and shape families.
- The chosen geometry is serving the current context rather than a memorized symbolic rule.
- Structural clarity and focal hierarchy remain intact after the emotional adjustment.

## Notes
Loomis's “relationship of line to emotional response” plate groups circles, ovals, spirals, radiating structures, triangles, formal divisions, irregular subdivisions, curves, and angular arrangements with differing emotional associations. The transferable capability is not the plate's period-specific one-to-one labels; it is the design decision to make the dominant line-and-shape character participate in the intended emotional read, then test that association in context.
