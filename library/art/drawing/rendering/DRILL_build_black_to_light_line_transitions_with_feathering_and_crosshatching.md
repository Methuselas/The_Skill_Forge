# Build Black-to-Light Line Transitions With Feathering and Crosshatching

## Practice Task
Build matched black-to-light transitions with feathering and crosshatching, then deliberately introduce common failures and correct them while holding the underlying form and target values constant.

## Target Skill
Construct line-based value transitions whose taper, spacing, layering, and direction soften a black mass, preserve the intended value gradient, and support the turning form without accidental hard seams or moiré.

## Setup
Prepare several equal-size abstract swatches or simple curved forms with the same solid-black end, open-light end, and target transition width. Work at a size that lets individual marks remain visible, and plan to inspect the studies both large and reduced.

## Instructions
1. Make a feathered version. Merge thick feather ends into the black mass, taper them toward the light, open the negative-space wedges progressively, and bend the family with the form when the surface turns.
2. Make a crosshatched version. Add one line family at a time, changing family length, spacing, weight, and intersection angle so the aggregate value moves gradually from black toward open ground.
3. Make an intentional **equal-length grid failure** in which the line ends align too mechanically; note the hard outer ledge or inner seam it creates.
4. Make an intentional **near-parallel interference failure** in which overlapping families produce a conspicuous moiré pattern; distinguish that optical effect from ordinary darkening.
5. Make an intentional **overworked failure** by adding more layers than the target value and form require.
6. Correct each failure with the smallest useful change: stagger family lengths, alter angle relationships, reduce or simplify excess marks, or rebuild the transition from the black edge outward.
7. Compare all versions at reduced size, where individual lines begin to fuse into aggregate tone. Check whether the intended value, edge softness, and form still read.
8. Repeat on one simple curved form rather than a flat swatch so the line families must also describe volume.

## Success Check
- The successful versions move from black into open ground without an accidental ledge or abrupt secondary seam.
- Feathering and hatching both preserve the intended aggregate value after reduction.
- Line direction supports the turning form instead of becoming ornamental striping.
- No moiré dominates unless it was deliberately chosen as the visual effect.
- Removing unnecessary marks from the overworked version does not weaken the required value or form read.

## Common Failures
- Drawing equal-width feather lines that soften an edge but do not actually graduate value.
- Ending many hatch lines at the same place and creating a new hard boundary.
- Crossing families at habitual angles without checking for mechanical grids or interference.
- Adding layers because the passage looks impressive up close even after the target value has already been reached.
- Curving marks decoratively without reference to the underlying volume.
- Judging only at full size and missing the way separate marks fuse after reduction.

## Notes
Feathering and crosshatching are different constructions of the same broader problem: controlling the ratio of dark mark to open ground across an edge. Feathering emphasizes taper and the geometry of white wedges entering a black mass; crosshatching emphasizes successive line families and their combined density. Practise both against the same target so the visible transition, not loyalty to one technique, remains the criterion.
