# Sculpt and Light an Invented Form for Rendering

## Practice Task
Invent a simple exaggerated form in 2-D, build a rough physical maquette that preserves those designed masses, light it with one clear source, and redraw the observed light and shadow.

## Target Skill
Test whether an invented form has coherent three-dimensional surface turns instead of guessing the lighting from a flat design.

## Setup
Start with a simple invented head, creature part, prop, or abstract character form whose bumps, recesses, and projections are clear enough to build quickly. Use clay, plasticine, kneaded material, paper massing, or another simple modeling medium. Use one dominant light source and a neutral viewing position.

## Instructions
1. Draw the invented form first. Decide which masses are intentionally enlarged, compressed, recessed, tilted, or projecting before touching the modeling material.
2. Build a rough maquette that preserves those exaggerations. Do not normalize the design toward realistic anatomy or ideal geometry merely because the physical model is easier to build that way.
3. Place the maquette under one clear light. Rotate either the light or the model until the large form turns, cast shadows, and reflected-light areas are easy to read.
4. Make a new drawing from observation. Establish the broad light, halftone, core/shadow, and cast-shadow behavior before chasing small surface incidents.
5. Compare the redraw against the original invented drawing. Mark any place where the imagined surface turn could not produce the observed light behavior you expected.
6. Revise either the construction or the lighting logic, then repeat the observation pass until the designed exaggeration and the rendered form agree.

## Success Check
- The physical model preserves the important exaggerations of the original invention rather than replacing them with a generic realistic form.
- One coherent light source explains the major light and shadow pattern on the maquette.
- The redraw uses the observed turning surfaces to clarify the invented volume instead of merely copying the silhouette of the model.
- Comparing the original and redraw reveals specific construction or lighting assumptions that can be corrected in later imagination drawing.

## Common Failures
- Spending so long polishing the sculpture that the exercise becomes a modeling project instead of a rendering test.
- Correcting the exaggeration toward realism, which removes the very volume the drill is supposed to test.
- Using several competing lights and then learning nothing clear about how the form turns.
- Copying every surface bump from the maquette instead of extracting the large light/form relationships.
- Treating the maquette as a permanent crutch rather than a temporary external check on invented spatial reasoning.

## Notes
Loomis proposes building comic or exaggerated forms in clay or plasticine, lighting them, and drawing the resulting light and shadow so the artist can develop a stronger sense of solidity. The retained drill generalizes that loop beyond comics: **2-D invention -> simple physical maquette -> controlled illumination -> observational redraw -> construction/rendering correction**.
