# Choose Color Strategy to Fit Subject, Purpose, and Viewing Context

## Pattern Rule
**IF** a picture needs an intentional color conception rather than merely accurate local colors
**THEN** choose the palette's overall intensity, restraint, contrast, and dominant relationships according to the subject, intended pictorial effect, viewing conditions, and surrounding field, then judge individual colors by how they serve that whole strategy
**ELSE** keep the color organization straightforward when the task is primarily descriptive and no stronger contextual color decision is needed.

## Do
- Define what the picture must accomplish before choosing how forceful, restrained, unified, or varied its color should be.
- Consider where and how the image will be encountered: page, cover, display, dark surround, light surround, small reproduction, or another context that changes how color masses read.
- When color is carrying communication, identify learned associations relevant to the intended audience—such as warning, brand, product, or cultural conventions—and decide deliberately whether to preserve, exploit, weaken, or violate them.
- Test an important learned color signal with wording, shape, or other cues reduced; if the message collapses, the color association is not carrying as much semantic load as assumed.
- For recurring brands, characters, factions, products, or series, separate identity colors that require continuity from supporting colors that may vary with context; change an identity color deliberately only when changing the identity or message is part of the goal.
- Scope culturally coded color to the actual audience as specifically as the task permits—region, community or religion, subculture, event, and relevant time period—and evaluate color-plus-symbol combinations together when the meaning depends on both.
- Judge large color areas more cautiously than small accents; broad high-chroma fields can dominate a picture much faster than limited spots of strong color.
- Let selected accents remain cleaner or stronger when immediate attention is required, while quieter support colors carry the rest of the picture.
- For harmony, establish a dominant color tendency and let subordinate or opposing families act as counterweights in deliberately unequal proportions; judge their strength by visual weight, not area alone.
- Test whether an apparently wrong color is actually wrong in isolation or simply wrong in relation to neighboring colors, values, lighting, and scale.
- Recheck the color conception at the whole-picture scale instead of correcting every local passage independently.
- After observational color relationships are established, make a separate whole-picture pass in which fidelity becomes subordinate to hierarchy and balance: reduce intrusive lights, darks, or high-chroma passages with the smallest useful value/chroma change before introducing an unrelated new hue.
- Step away from the reference during that pictorial pass and judge whether any secondary passage punches a hole, pops forward, or competes with the intended subject even if it is locally accurate.
- Define not only the dominant hue relationships but also which hue families will be muted, rare, or absent unless the subject genuinely requires them; full-spectrum availability is not a reason to distribute full-spectrum color through the picture.
- When color or value is deliberately exaggerated beyond observation, declare the change of evaluation mode: judge the altered passage against the picture's value structure, drawing, edge hierarchy, dominant color conception, and expressive intent instead of continuing to treat literal reference matching as the sole correctness test.

## Don't
- Do not choose a palette only from memorized emotional meanings assigned to individual hues.
- Do not treat learned color associations as universal laws or infer them from demographic stereotypes; verify that the intended audience and context actually support the convention.
- Do not assume a nation-, continent-, or tradition-level color dictionary applies uniformly to every subgroup or remains current across time.
- Do not assume maximum saturation produces maximum color impact.
- Do not ignore the surrounding page, field, reproduction size, or display conditions when they materially affect the read.
- Do not let local color accuracy override the larger pictorial purpose when the assignment requires hierarchy, mood, or immediate recognition.

## Checklist
- The color treatment supports the intended subject and response rather than functioning as decoration added afterward.
- Large color masses, accents, and muted support areas have deliberate relative strength.
- Dominant and counter-color families are intentionally unequal, with small high-chroma or high-contrast notes allowed to balance much larger restrained fields.
- The palette remains effective in its anticipated viewing or reproduction context.
- Individual color corrections improve the whole relationship rather than only making isolated patches more attractive.
- Identity-critical colors remain recognizably stable across related designs unless a deliberate redesign calls for change.
- Any culture-dependent color meaning is scoped to the intended audience and current context rather than treated as a universal property of the hue.
- The final color pass works as an autonomous picture: locally accurate passages do not remain if they damage hierarchy, balance, or focal control.

## Notes
Color strategy is a picture-level design decision. After an observational pass has established the scene's actual relationships, a later pictorial pass may deliberately depart from the reference when a locally faithful light, dark, or chroma accent damages the whole image. A restrained palette, forceful contrast, broad muted field, or small area of clean chroma can all be correct depending on the image's purpose and viewing context. Harmony does not require equal representation of opposing hue families: a picture can be more coherent when one family dominates and a smaller, visually stronger counter-color supplies balance or punctuation. Learned color associations can also carry semantic weight, but they belong to a particular audience, time, and use context rather than to the hue itself. Persistent identity colors should remain stable across related designs when recognition depends on them, while culturally coded color should be localized to the actual community and, when relevant, interpreted together with its accompanying symbol or ritual context. Historical claims that individual hues produce fixed psychological effects are not required for this decision; the durable skill is to organize color relationally around the intended pictorial job and then verify that organization in the context where the image will actually be seen.

`VAR_vandijk_separate_narrative_regions_with_contrasting_palette_families` uses broad palette-family contrast to separate large narrative regions. The contrast should reinforce a real story distinction and still coexist with the scene lighting.

`VAR_vandijk_differentiate_related_images_with_dominant_palette_signatures` treats dominant palette identity as a series-level differentiation tool. Preserve enough shared visual language for the set to belong together while making each entry distinct at first read.

`VAR_dahlig_use_temperature_and_color_intensity_as_loose_emotional_cues` uses temperature, chroma, and color contrast as supporting emotional biases rather than fixed psychological meanings. Let those choices reinforce the image's other cues, and back off when scene lighting, subject logic, surrounding color, or audience context makes the association unreliable.

`VAR_eissen_use_colored_ground_as_product_base_tone` treats the support itself as useful color information. Choose a mid-value ground near the product's dominant color, leave it visible where it can function as the base tone, and spend marks on the departures that describe light, shade, reflection, and accent.

A coherent palette can be defined as much by exclusion as by inclusion. Deliberately suppressing unrelated hue families can make the dominant relationship clearer while leaving rare exceptions available for necessity or emphasis.

`VAR_gurney_break_green_monotony_with_varied_mixtures_and_reddish_neutrals` Corrects synthetic or repetitive green handling by varying green mixtures across scales and weaving in restrained reddish or pinkish neutrals without making muted green a universal law.

`VAR_gurney_constrain_scheme_to_one_hue_family_across_value_and_chroma` Builds a final-picture monochromatic strategy from one hue family while allowing substantial value and chroma variation for unity, understatement, sequence distinction, or historical/archival effect.

`VAR_gurney_season_restrained_field_with_sparse_opposing_color_accents` Keeps the dominant field narrow or subdued while using a very small quantity of noticeably different, often opposing, color as focal punctuation or distributed seasoning; the strongest option may deliberately omit intermediate bridge colors so the accent stays stark.

Deliberate exaggeration changes the standard of evaluation. Once color or value is intentionally pushed beyond observation, maintain control through the picture's internal structure and declared intent rather than continuing to judge every altered note only by literal reference matching.
