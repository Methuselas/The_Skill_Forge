# Compare the Same Subject Across Tonal Treatments

## Practice Task
Render one compact subject several times while keeping the subject, viewpoint, and basic lighting substantially constant and deliberately changing the tonal treatment rather than the medium.

## Target Skill
Learn to choose a rendering treatment for what it does to form, mood, hierarchy, vitality, and subject character instead of letting one habitual finish dictate every picture.

## Setup
Choose a subject with enough simple form and value structure to survive several interpretations. Use the same or closely comparable medium for every study so treatment, not tool change, is the main variable. Prepare three or four equal-size studies.

## Instructions
1. Establish one simple construction, viewpoint, crop, and large light/shadow statement to use across all studies.
2. Make a **soft-first** version: establish broad tones, soften early, then recover only the edges, accents, and details needed for structure and focus.
3. Make a **crisp or brittle** version: retain more definite value boundaries and sharper accents where the subject and light can support them, while preserving a few softer passages so crispness has contrast.
4. Make a **planar or blocky** version: simplify rounded transitions into larger flatter value planes and straighter changes long enough to test whether stronger structure improves the read.
5. If useful, make one additional treatment that changes mark direction, surface handling, or degree of finish without changing the underlying subject problem.
6. Compare all studies at thumbnail size. Record what each treatment strengthens or weakens in form, depth, mood, hierarchy, material impression, and vitality.
7. Choose the treatment that best serves the intended picture, then make a fresh development without mechanically copying every local effect from the study.

## Success Check
- The studies remain clearly comparable because subject, viewpoint, and basic lighting did not drift materially.
- Each version differs through a coherent treatment decision rather than random finish changes.
- You can explain what the chosen treatment contributes to the picture and what it sacrifices.
- Softness and crispness are used relationally; no version becomes uniformly fuzzy or uniformly hard without an intentional reason.

## Common Failures
- Changing medium, pose, crop, and lighting at the same time, so treatment can no longer be isolated.
- Treating “soft,” “brittle,” or “blocky” as a surface filter instead of reorganizing edges, planes, and accents coherently.
- Assuming one treatment is universally superior after it works on a single subject.
- Copying a photograph more literally in every pass instead of interpreting the same subject in genuinely different ways.

## Notes
Loomis contrasts soft, brittle, blocky, and related approaches and explicitly recommends setting up one subject—or even one photograph—and interpreting it in several ways. PASS separates this from `DRILL_compare_same_subject_across_medium_behaviors`: that Drill isolates what different tools naturally do, while this Drill keeps the tool substantially stable and isolates the rendering treatment. Medium-specific paint chemistry, wetness, support, brush loading, and similar handling remain deferred to the Materials / Media curriculum.
