# Copy and Transfer a Master Color Scheme

## Practice Task
Copy the major color relationships of one strong exemplar, then transfer substantially the same palette into several copies of a simple design while changing where each color appears and how much area it occupies.

## Target Skill
Recognize that a palette is not a bag of independently harmonious swatches: hue, value, chroma, quantity, and placement interact, so the same colors can produce different results when their distribution changes.

## Setup
Choose a color exemplar whose reproduction is trustworthy enough for visual study. Prepare one small simplified copy area and three to five copies of a simple line design. Use a medium that lets you mix or select reasonably repeatable colors.

## Instructions
1. Reduce the exemplar to its major color families and large areas; ignore small surface detail that does not materially affect the palette relationship.
2. Make a small color copy aimed at matching the major hue, value, and chroma relationships rather than reproducing every object or brush mark.
3. Identify the extracted palette and estimate which colors occupy large, medium, and small portions of the exemplar.
4. Apply substantially the same palette to the first simple design, keeping the broad distribution reasonably close to the source relationship.
5. In later copies, deliberately change which shapes receive each color and alter the relative area occupied by the colors while keeping the palette itself as stable as practical.
6. View the versions together at reduced size or normal viewing distance. Compare unity, dominance, accent strength, balance, and whether any previously successful color now becomes harsh, weak, or monotonous because its quantity or placement changed.
7. State which distribution works best for the new design and why; do not assume the exemplar's exact allocation must be copied when the new shapes and purpose differ.

## Success Check
- The initial copy preserves the exemplar's major hue, value, and chroma relationships without requiring detailed imitation.
- Later versions keep a recognizably similar palette while materially changing color placement or area proportions.
- The learner can identify at least one case where the same color behaves differently because its quantity or surroundings changed.
- The final judgment considers the whole color arrangement rather than treating each swatch as independently harmonious.

## Common Failures
- Changing the palette so much between versions that distribution can no longer be compared.
- Copying surface detail instead of extracting the large color relationship.
- Assuming colors that worked in the exemplar must work equally well in every proportion or placement.
- Treating the exercise as a recipe for borrowing a master's finished scheme instead of as training in relational color judgment.

## Notes
Copying a strong color example can sharpen perception, but the more important test comes after the copy. Dow has the learner carry the same colors into a new design and vary their distribution, revealing that harmony depends not only on which colors are present but also on their quantity and arrangement. The exercise therefore separates palette selection from palette deployment and makes color proportion an observable design variable.
