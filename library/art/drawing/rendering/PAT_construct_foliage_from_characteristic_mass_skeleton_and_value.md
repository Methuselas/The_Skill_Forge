# Construct Foliage From Characteristic Mass, Skeleton, and Value

## Pattern Rule
**IF** a tree or foliage group must read as a particular growth character instead of generic vegetation texture **THEN** establish the characteristic outer mass first, place the trunk and principal branch skeleton that explains that mass, organize the main light-and-dark groupings, and add only enough smaller foliage marks to identify the surface **ELSE** when vegetation is distant or subordinate, simplify it to the broad mass and depth-appropriate value/detail level rather than forcing species-level description

## Do
- Identify the large growth character before drawing leaves: overall spread, taper, droop or lift, major asymmetry, and the characteristic breaks in the silhouette.
- Build the trunk and principal branches as a supporting framework for the visible foliage masses; let secondary branching emerge from that hierarchy instead of appearing as an unrelated web.
- Organize the principal light and dark masses before spending marks on leaf or twig texture so the tree holds together at a glance.
- Treat foliage contours as designed irregular masses rather than automatic rounded clouds; use straighter, angular, broken, drooping, or rising passages when the observed growth calls for them.
- Use a mark vocabulary that suggests the visible foliage character at the current scale instead of copying individual leaves that are too small to resolve.
- As distance increases, reduce branch articulation and surface marks while preserving the large silhouette and value grouping that still survive at that depth.
- After the large foliage mass and branch skeleton are solved, make the silhouette porous with irregular skyholes whose size and density fit species, season, and foliage transparency.
- Do not paint every small skyhole as untouched sky color; fine twigs and unresolved leaf layers can partially veil the opening and lower its apparent value.

## Don't
- Do not reuse one generic tree symbol for species or growth types that differ materially in silhouette, branching, or foliage habit.
- Do not begin with leaf-by-leaf texture and hope the larger tree structure will emerge afterward.
- Do not fill the crown with random marks that ignore the trunk, major branches, and the way masses are supported.
- Do not make every tree crown uniformly spherical or scalloped when the observed contour is more angular, broken, directional, or asymmetrical.
- Do not preserve foreground-level twig and leaf detail after scale or atmospheric depth has made that information unreadable.
- Do not turn skyholes into evenly spaced polka dots or use identical openings across unrelated tree structures.

## Checklist
- The overall silhouette carries a specific growth character before small texture is added.
- The trunk and principal branches plausibly support the visible foliage masses.
- The main light-and-dark grouping still reads when small foliage marks are mentally removed.
- Surface marks suggest foliage at the intended scale without becoming literal leaf inventory.
- Near and far vegetation differ in articulation consistently with the scene's depth structure.

## Notes
Vegetation reads most convincingly when its small marks are consequences of a larger structure. The useful sequence is mass first, then supporting branch framework, then broad value organization, then selective surface indication. This prevents both the generic rounded-tree symbol and the opposite failure of describing many leaves without establishing a coherent tree.

Distance changes how much of that structure survives. A near tree may expose branch rhythm and characteristic foliage breaks; a far tree may need only its dominant mass, value, and a few identifying directional cues. Use atmospheric depth and mark-density controls to reduce information rather than shrinking the same texture indefinitely.
