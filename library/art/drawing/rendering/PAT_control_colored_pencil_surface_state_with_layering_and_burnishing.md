# Control Colored-Pencil Surface State With Layering and Burnishing

## Pattern Rule
**IF** a colored-pencil passage must move between visible paper/mark texture and a smoother, denser, more saturated finish
**THEN** control the passage through pressure and tooth occupancy: begin in a light layered state that preserves visible ground and separable marks, then escalate toward burnishing only where stronger coverage, smoothness, blending, or shine is actually required
**ELSE** preserve the layered state when open texture and visible paper are part of the intended material read.

## Do
- Start lightly enough that the support remains visibly active while structure, value, and color relationships are still being established.
- Treat pressure as a continuous control: deepen or fill passages progressively instead of jumping immediately to maximum coverage.
- Keep layering relatively open when the intended surface is soft, powdery, fuzzy, granular, or visibly hand-drawn.
- Burnish when smoothness, stronger saturation, opacity, or shine is the target; use enough pressure to reduce the visible paper tooth and merge prior layers into a more continuous color field.
- Treat burnishing as control of surface occupancy and layer integration, not as an instruction to erase all visible mark structure; when the target surface needs both dense coverage and directional texture, preserve or deliberately reintroduce medium-scale marks whose direction and shape agree with the form and material.
- Combine layered and burnished passages within one image when different materials or local surfaces require different surface behavior.
- Protect critical lights and unresolved structural decisions before saturating the tooth heavily, because late recovery becomes less flexible once the surface is filled.
- Recalibrate the same pencils and pressure on the actual support, since tooth and ground can change the apparent tone, texture, and color.
- Treat support tooth as a potential material cue rather than only a limitation: when its native texture matches the target surface, preserve enough open layering for that texture to participate in the read; when its scale or direction conflicts with the material, suppress it through denser coverage or choose a better support.
- When open layering leaves a colored ground visibly active, treat the support's color and value as part of the final color system: test the intended pencils on that ground, let a compatible ground supply a quiet broad base where useful, and cover, neutralize, or replace the support when its bias works against the observed or intended passage.
- On fuzzy or strongly textured supports, treat native diffusion as a baseline handling tendency rather than a command that every edge remain soft: preserve lighter/open application where softness helps atmosphere or material, but recover selected overlap boundaries, focal accents, and structural edges with denser or narrower application when the image needs them.
- If the support cannot deliver the required hard-soft edge range after calibration, choose a different support instead of forcing every edge through excessive pressure.
- Stop adding nominally light layers when accumulated overlap is unintentionally closing the tooth and turning the passage opaque.
- Keep temporary graphite construction minimal and light; before pale or open layered passages, reduce unnecessary graphite and replace any required visible structure with the intended colored-pencil edge or mass so graphite does not gray or contaminate the color.

## Don't
- Treat layering and burnishing as merely two stylistic looks; they change how much support remains active and how later layers can interact with the surface.
- Burnish every passage mechanically when only selected surfaces need smoothness or shine.
- Smooth away all directional texture merely because a passage is burnished; dense coverage can still retain form-following streaks, longitudinal texture, granular glaze marks, or other material-specific structure.
- Depend on heavy pressure to repair unresolved drawing, value, or material logic after the tooth has already been filled.
- Assume the same pressure produces the same result across papers, boards, or colored grounds.
- Treat a named complexion, subject category, or material as requiring a fixed support color; exposed ground is a picture-level color choice that must be tested against the actual subject, illumination, and intended result.
- Let a soft or fuzzy support homogenize the whole image into one edge quality simply because its native texture diffuses marks.
- Hide graphite contamination by simply increasing pressure; heavy coverage may conceal the underdrawing only by prematurely closing the tooth and changing the intended surface state.

## Checklist
- Open layered passages still show intentional support texture or separable pencil marks where that behavior is wanted.
- Burnished passages are visibly smoother, denser, or more saturated for a clear material or finish reason.
- Any visible marks that survive or are reintroduced after burnishing have a clear form, material, light, or design job rather than reading as arbitrary pencil noise.
- Pressure changes are gradual enough to avoid unintended choppy bands in value or coverage.
- Important highlights and structural corrections were resolved before heavily filling the tooth.
- The chosen support has been calibrated rather than treated as an inert substrate.
- When a colored ground remains visible through open layers, its hue and value contribute intentionally to the final passage rather than introducing an uncontrolled cast.
- Any support texture left visible contributes at an appropriate scale and direction to the depicted material instead of substituting for form, light, or larger texture structure.
- Soft-support passages still contain intentional edge hierarchy: diffuse fields stay soft where useful, while required structural/focal boundaries remain recoverable and legible.
- Pale or open layered passages are not unintentionally grayed by leftover graphite construction.

## Notes
Colored pencil can occupy materially different surface states without changing medium. Light pressure leaves more paper tooth visible and keeps marks optically separable; progressive pressure and repeated layering increase coverage; burnishing compresses or fills the tooth so prior colors mix more continuously and the surface can read smoother, stronger, and shinier. The useful decision is therefore not simply “layer or burnish,” but how far to move along that state continuum for each passage while enough surface capacity remains for later correction and refinement.

Because open colored-pencil layers may remain optically permeable to the support and underdrawing, graphite is part of the surface-state decision: reduce temporary graphite before light passages instead of forcing later colored-pencil coverage to compensate for a contaminated ground.

Burnishing and visible texture are independent decisions. Heavy coverage may integrate layers and close the tooth while selected directional or granular marks remain legible, provided those marks continue to describe the form or material rather than merely exposing the pencil process.

Support tooth can also be recruited deliberately. An open layered passage may let a compatible paper or board texture participate in the depicted surface, while a smoother or denser passage can suppress that same tooth where it would contradict the material. A colored ground can participate in the same way: when open layering leaves it visible, its hue and value become part of the passage and should be chosen and calibrated as part of the final color system rather than treated as neutral paper. Strongly textured or fuzzy supports also bias edge behavior toward diffusion; use that softness selectively, recover harder boundaries where structure or focus requires them, and change support when its native edge or color range cannot serve the image. The support remains subordinate to solved form, light, material structure, and the actual observed or intentionally designed color relationships; do not turn Hammond's complexion examples into fixed paper-color recipes.
