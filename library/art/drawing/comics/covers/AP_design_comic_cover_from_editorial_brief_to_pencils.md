# Design a Comic Cover From Editorial Brief to Pencils

## Objective
Turn an editorial cover idea into a clear, high-impact pencil design by solving hierarchy, camera, production space, and story promise while the composition is still cheap to change.

## Steps / Flow
1. **State the cover job before drawing.** Identify the lead character or subject, required guest or supporting elements, the story situation the cover is allowed to reveal, and the production requirements that must remain usable. Treat the brief as a design constraint, not as a finished composition.
2. **Mark the live production zones.** Reserve the current title/logo area, trim/bleed/safe areas, and any likely copy zones before placing irreplaceable story information. Use the actual production specification for the target format; Marvel's historical half-inch/right-and-bottom trim guidance is source evidence, not a timeless dimension.
3. **Stage 0 — make several simple cover layouts.** Keep them cheap enough to compare. Vary subject scale, facing, overlap, camera height, eye level, crop, negative space, and the relationship between lead and supporting figures while preserving the same editorial premise.
4. **Review the layouts for hierarchy and punch.** Ask whether the intended star reads first, whether important faces/actions are visible enough for the brief, whether the figures occupy enough of the frame to carry impact, whether the camera strengthens the situation, and whether unused space is helping rather than merely empty. Treat these as editorial judgments, not mathematical absolutes.
5. **Check the cover as a promise, not a synopsis.** The image should provoke curiosity and communicate the issue's dramatic proposition without depending on a late-story reveal or giving away a surprise the brief intends to preserve.
6. **Select one layout and freeze its Stage 0 commitments.** Record the accepted camera, crop, hierarchy, production zones, and story promise. Do not average several rejected layouts together after selection.
7. **Develop the selected cover through normal staged construction.** Build the figures and setting from the approved layout, preserving the chosen hierarchy and reserved production areas as the drawing advances to finished pencils. If the cover stops working, return to the stage that owns the failed decision instead of hiding the problem with finish.
8. **Hand off a cover-ready pencil design.** Confirm that the lead still reads first, required supporting information remains legible, copy/logo zones are usable, critical content is safe from trim, and later inking/color/lettering can proceed without redesigning the whole image.

## Notes
Marvel's chapter compares four rough Nova/Spider-Man cover layouts before choosing one: the discussion weighs figure scale, whether the lead character is presented effectively, wasted space, guest-star visibility, and a more interesting high eye level. The next page adds production constraints for logo space, trim/bleed, flexible areas for copy, color-friendly openness, and a provocative image that does not reveal the ending. The final pages then carry the selected rough into more developed pencils.

This object is intentionally a **starter skeleton for comic-cover design**, not a complete doctrine. It does not yet own advanced splash-page composition, title/letterform integration, retail-thumbnail behavior, page-turn relationships, sophisticated teaser strategy, color scripting, or modern print/digital production specifications. Later splash-page study and deeper comics sources should extend this branch; if they reveal a stronger shared single-page-display foundation, factor that common doctrine upward rather than forcing future material into this first Marvel scaffold.

The source phrase "leave the drawing open for color" is retained here only as historical workflow context. The durable rule is to leave downstream production room appropriate to the actual pipeline, not to impose a fixed black-density rule on every modern cover.
