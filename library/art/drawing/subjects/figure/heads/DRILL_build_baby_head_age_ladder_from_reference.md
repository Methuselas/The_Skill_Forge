# Build a Baby-Head Age Ladder From Reference

## Practice Task
Make a small sequence of baby and early-childhood head blocks from clear reference, then redraw the same age stages from memory. Keep the exercise structural: large cranium, facial-plane length, eye/brow level, four face divisions, and chin growth matter more than eyelashes, hair, or finish.

## Target Skill
Remembering how the facial mass grows inside the larger young-child cranium so age reads from construction before surface cues.

## Setup
Collect three clear references spanning approximately **first year**, **second/third year**, and **third/fourth year**. A sleeping baby, photograph, or still image is appropriate; Loomis explicitly warns that a moving baby is difficult to study before the proportions are familiar. Draw all heads at roughly the same scale.

## Instructions
1. For the first reference, block the cranial ball and attach a **much shorter facial plane** than you would use for an adult. Mark the whole-head halfway line and the brow relationship before drawing features.
2. Divide the compact face below the brow into four working bands. Place only simple eye, nose, mouth-corner, chin, and ear markers so the grid can be judged without decorative detail.
3. Repeat from the second/third-year reference. Compare how much more facial height appears below the eyes, how the features sit higher in the whole head, and how the jaw/nose region has lengthened while the cranium still dominates.
4. Repeat from the third/fourth-year reference. Preserve the same construction logic but allow additional chin/facial growth and the corresponding rise of the brow, eyes, nose, and mouth within the total head.
5. Hide the references and redraw the three stages from memory using only the ball, facial plane, midpoint, four divisions, and simple feature markers.
6. Reopen the references and diagnose the first structural mismatch. Correct cranial-to-facial proportion or feature level before touching hair, eyelashes, expression, or rendering.
7. Optional transfer test: make one rapid sketch from a briefly available or naturally moving child reference and see whether the memorized age scaffold can be recalled without slow measuring.

## Success Check
- The three heads read as different early developmental stages before hair, tone, or expression is added.
- The youngest head has a clearly dominant cranium and compact, low facial mass rather than adult proportions scaled down.
- The age progression comes mainly from facial growth and the changing eye/brow relationship to the whole head.
- Individual skull shape can vary without destroying the developmental read.
- On the memory pass, corrections target relationships first instead of cosmetic baby symbols.

## Common Failures
- Drawing an adult face on a larger round skull.
- Using large eyes, tiny nose, or curls as the only age signal while the facial mass remains adult-sized.
- Treating the four divisions as exact measurements that override the reference.
- Letting hair or expression decide the age after the construction has already contradicted it.
- Making a polished copy from a photo without testing whether the underlying proportion was actually learned.

## Notes
Loomis's key practical problem is **retrieval under motion**: the baby may not remain available long enough for slow analysis. The proportion plates therefore function as a memorized construction scaffold. The drill preserves that purpose while treating his averages as study aids rather than fixed biological laws.
