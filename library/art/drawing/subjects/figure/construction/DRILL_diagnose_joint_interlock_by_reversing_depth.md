# Diagnose Joint Interlock by Reversing Depth

## Practice Task
Draw paired knees, elbows, and finger bends in which the advancing member reverses while the overall bend remains comparable.

## Target Skill
Use joint contour and overlap to state the near-to-far relation without breaking the continuous chain.

## Setup
Make three pairs of simple two-cylinder bends: one leg, one arm, and one finger. Leave room beside each for an intentionally wrong version.

## Instructions
1. In the first drawing of each pair, advance the lower or terminal member and interlock the joint with it.
2. In the second, reverse the depth so the upper or parent member advances, then change the joint contour accordingly.
3. Copy each bend once more and deliberately attach the joint overlap to the receding member.
4. Label the visible symptom in the wrong copy: swelling, cracking, override, detachment, or ambiguous depth.
5. Correct the wrong copy without changing the intended chain or bend angle.

## Success Check
- Each correct pair shows a clear reversal of which member advances.
- The joint changes its visible interlock but remains part of one limb or digit.
- The intentionally wrong versions produce recognizable diagnostic symptoms.
- The correction fixes depth without redesigning the whole member.

## Common Failures
- Changing the pose so much that the depth reversal cannot be compared.
- Treating the joint as a separate ball that floats between the shanks.
- Correcting the error by adding anatomy instead of changing the decisive overlap.
- Assuming the same contour works when the advancing member reverses.

## Notes
Hogarth demonstrates the rule by contrasting correct and deliberately incorrect knee interlocks, then carrying it into elbows and knuckles. The paired format makes the diagnostic difference visible and prevents the phrase "advancing member gets the joint" from becoming an unexamined slogan.
