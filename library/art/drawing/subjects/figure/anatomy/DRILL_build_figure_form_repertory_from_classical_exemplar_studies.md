# Build a Figure-Form Repertory From Classical Exemplar Studies

## Practice Task
Choose one human-form family, such as feet, hands, knees, facial features, heads, or torsos. Draw several strong classical examples of that same family, compare the recurring large-form and proportional relationships, and then extend the study with additional examples you select yourself.

## Target Skill
Converting repeated active studies of strong classical figure exemplars into a usable repertory of recurring human-form and proportion relationships without treating one idealized example as a universal template.

## Setup
Assemble several clear examples from strong drawings, paintings, plaster casts, sculpture, or museum works. Keep the set focused on one form family at a time so the comparison has a stable subject. The examples may differ in view, pose, individual proportion, and medium.

## Instructions
1. Choose one form family and place several examples where they can be compared together.
2. Make a structural study of each example rather than merely looking at it. Prioritize the large form, main proportions, orientation, and major attachments before surface finish.
3. After each study, note which relationships recur across several examples and which visibly change with the individual subject, pose, load, or viewpoint.
4. Compare the drawings as a set. Use the repetition to strengthen your memory of the form family while keeping the examples distinct rather than averaging them into one generic template.
5. Seek additional examples beyond the initial set. Museum study, classical casts, master drawings, and paintings can all extend the repertory when they make the form readable enough to study actively.
6. Repeat the cycle with another form family after the first set has produced relationships you can recognize and reconstruct more reliably.

## Success Check
- You can identify recurring large-form and proportional relationships across several examples of the same body part.
- Your studies preserve real differences in viewpoint, pose, and individual proportion rather than forcing every example into one memorized formula.
- The structural read remains clear even when you simplify or omit the source's surface finish.
- You can seek and study additional exemplars independently instead of relying only on one supplied plate or one canonical model.

## Common Failures
- Browsing or admiring references without drawing them and assuming passive viewing will become usable form knowledge by itself.
- Copying texture, shading, or stylistic manner before establishing the large form and proportion.
- Treating one classical exemplar as the universally correct shape for every living subject.
- Ignoring meaningful variation because several examples share the same broad anatomical organization.
- Mining every plate for a new anatomy formula instead of using the examples as repeated study material for already-known body structures.

## Notes
Mogilevtsev's final study section presents old-master drawings, plaster casts of antique sculpture, and classical paintings as material for learning human form and proportion, and explicitly argues that the information must be apprehended and memorized through drawing rather than passive viewing. The plates then group related examples—feet, hands, knees, facial features, heads, torsos, and a full figure—so the learner can study recurring organization across multiple instances. His literal claim that the form of the feet is always the same is not adopted as a universal anatomy law; PASS preserves the useful invariant-seeking practice while retaining actual variation in individual proportion, pose, load, age, and viewpoint.
