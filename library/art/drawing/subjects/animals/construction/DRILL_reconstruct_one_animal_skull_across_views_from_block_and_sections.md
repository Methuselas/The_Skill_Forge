# Reconstruct One Animal Skull Across Views From Block and Sections

## Practice Task
Take one animal skull through a simple type block, a planar construction, at least two changed views, and a final memory reconstruction.

## Target Skill
Holding one animal skull as a rotatable three-dimensional construction rather than memorizing a single diagram.

## Setup
Choose a skull reference with clear front, side, or three-quarter information. A physical model, 3D reference, or multiple photographs is ideal, but a well-documented skull plate can be used if views are limited.

## Instructions
1. Draw the simplest block or ovoid that preserves the skull type and mark its symmetry axis.
2. Add two or three cross-sections that prove the width, depth, and main plane gradients without adding anatomical detail.
3. Rebuild the cranial/facial division, orbit, jaw architecture, cheek arch, and main hollows over that block.
4. Draw a second view with the jaw or camera changed enough to expose hidden continuations. Carry the same symmetry and section logic through the rotation.
5. Draw a rear or opposite three-quarter study that forces the back of the cranial capsule and its ridges/arches to connect to the front construction.
6. Put the reference away and reconstruct the skull once more from the simple block. Use hatching only to model plane direction.
7. Compare the memory drawing with the reference and repair the first error at the block/axis/plane level before touching local anatomy.

## Success Check
- The simplified block is recognizable as the chosen skull type without relying on teeth or small features.
- Cross-sections reveal consistent width/depth changes across views.
- Hidden arches and hollows connect plausibly when the jaw or viewpoint changes.
- The memory reconstruction preserves the same cranial/facial architecture.
- Corrections are made to the construction rather than only to the final contour.

## Common Failures
- Copying one reference view accurately but being unable to explain the far side or rear of the skull.
- Adding many facets without a stable symmetry axis or block.
- Treating cross-sections as decorative bands instead of checks on actual spatial direction.
- Rendering lighting instead of modeling the form relationships being learned.
- Correcting a rotated skull by tracing silhouette differences rather than rebuilding the underlying solid.

## Notes
Bammes says constructional skull drawing trains visualization because the skull is the determining formal basis of the living head. His dog studies deliberately show front- and rear-three-quarter views, while the lion is examined with the jaw open so hollows, arches, and vaults become explicit. Repeating one skull across changed views turns those examples into a memory and rotation test rather than a copied plate exercise.
