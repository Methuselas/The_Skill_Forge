# Construct an Animal Skull From Type Block Through Planes and Cross-Sections

## Pattern Rule
**IF** a skull study contains many correct anatomical details but its three-dimensional type and plane relationships are difficult to hold through rotation
**THEN** reduce the skull first to the simplest block or ovoid that expresses its type, place that solid on a symmetry axis with spatial cross-axes, then rebuild the graduated planes, hollows, arches, and subsidiary masses while using cross-sections to keep their directions coherent
**ELSE** retain the simpler structural skull study when further faceting would add complexity without improving spatial understanding.

## Do
- Begin with a compact type-defining solid that makes the skull's overall width, height, length, and cranial/facial relationship easy to check.
- Carry a central symmetry route through the block and add cross-axes where they clarify the direction of planes or section changes.
- Erect the complex skull over the simple base as an architect would: ground-plan relationships first, then walls, arches, hollows, vaults, projections, and plane gradients.
- Use hatching to model and organize plane direction after the construction is secure; in this exercise the hatching describes form rather than a lighting scheme.
- Choose plane breaks that create useful three-dimensional contrasts, but let the actual skull decide where those breaks belong.

## Don't
- Do not begin from the full contour and then sprinkle cross-sections onto a shape whose spatial logic is already inconsistent.
- Do not mistake the horse prism, dog pentagon/circle, or carnivore ovoid for universal templates; they are source examples of type simplification.
- Do not facet every surface into a crystalline style after the major planes are already understood.
- Do not use dark hatching as cast-shadow rendering that obscures the construction being studied.

## Checklist
- The skull can be reduced to a simple solid that still preserves its type.
- The symmetry axis and cross-axes agree with the viewpoint and with one another.
- Major hollows, arches, ridges, and plane changes grow logically from the simple block.
- A rotated view preserves the same cranial/facial architecture.
- Hatching, if used, clarifies modeling and plane direction rather than hiding errors.

## Notes
Bammes's §8.2 turns the skull into an architectural construction problem. The horse is first reduced to a trihedral-prism-like block; the dog is summarized by an ovoid/pentagonal organization; lion studies expose hollows and vaulting by changing view and opening the jaw. His explicit sequence is simplification, spatial-axis control, graduated planes, and then complex components. The source's crystalline and architectural metaphors are construction aids, not mandatory finish styles.
