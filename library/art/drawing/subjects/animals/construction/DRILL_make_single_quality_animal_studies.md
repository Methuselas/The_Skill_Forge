# Make Single-Quality Animal Studies

## Practice Task
Make a page of quick animal studies in which each drawing is organized around one consciously selected impressional quality. The goal is not to finish the animal; it is to make one chosen characteristic read more clearly by deciding what information deserves attention and what can be left out.

## Target Skill
Identifying the strongest drawing-relevant impression in an animal and concentrating a study on that quality without dissolving into equal-weight detail.

## Setup
Choose six animal references or live subjects with visibly different qualities. Work small. Before drawing, keep a short list of Bammes's example categories nearby: proportion, function or action, surface texture, solidity, structure, delicacy, massiveness, and squatness.

## Instructions
1. Survey the first animal without drawing and write one short phrase naming what impresses you most.
2. Decide whether that phrase is primarily about proportion, action or function, surface, solidity, structure, or another clearly observable quality.
3. Make a quick study that spends most of its marks on the relationships needed to communicate that quality. Leave appealing but irrelevant observations undeveloped.
4. Stop midway and cover or ignore small details. Ask whether the chosen quality still reads from the large pose, proportions, masses, or principal surface treatment. If not, revise the large statement before continuing.
5. Finish only far enough to make the selected impression stable. Under the drawing, list two visible traits you deliberately omitted or subdued because they competed with the study's purpose.
6. Repeat on the remaining animals, choosing the strongest impression anew each time instead of reusing the same category by habit.

## Success Check
- Every study has one written governing phrase that can be matched to the drawing without a long explanation.
- Different subjects produce different emphases rather than the same generic treatment.
- The chosen quality is visible in large relationships before small detail is inspected.
- Omitted information feels intentionally subordinate rather than accidentally forgotten.
- The animal still reads as one coherent body even when many secondary observations are absent.

## Common Failures
- Choosing a vague goal such as “make it good” instead of one observable quality.
- Drawing the entire contour and every attractive feature before deciding what the study is about.
- Selecting texture or color, then losing the proportions and articulation that keep the marks attached to an animal form.
- Repeating the same preferred quality on every subject instead of observing what is distinctive in each one.
- Treating omission as carelessness rather than as a deliberate hierarchy of attention.

## Notes
Bammes proposes this as an observation discipline: look across animal forms, note what has the strongest effect on you, decide what kind of quality it is, and concentrate on that aspect alone. The practice is valuable because the failure he warns against is not lack of information but **too much equally weighted information**. The drill therefore trains selection and hierarchy before finish.
