# Block Animal Proportion With Broad Continuous Shapes

## Practice Task
Make a page of small animal proportion studies with a broad piece of chalk, crayon, graphite block, or similarly wide medium used mainly on its side. Build each animal from a few connected broad shapes instead of contour lines or small anatomical detail.

## Target Skill
Establishing an animal's large proportional relationships quickly with a broad tool by converting trunk and limbs into a few connected, overlapping geometric shapes.

## Setup
Choose a clear animal reference with readable trunk and limb proportions. Use a broad medium that can make a stroke roughly as wide as the intended trunk mass. Work large enough that the side of the tool can create a body shape in one or two passes, but small enough to repeat several studies.

## Instructions
1. Before drawing, compare trunk length-to-height, ground clearance, limb length, neck, and head scale. Decide the overall size of the study from the broad tool rather than from detail.
2. Lay the side of the tool down to state the trunk as one broad shape. Avoid outlining it first.
3. Keep the motion continuous where practical. Turn or swivel the broad tool so the forelimbs and hindlimbs emerge as simple angular, triangular, or trapezoidal shapes rather than thin contour pairs.
4. Overlap the broad shapes enough that the animal reads as one connected construction. Let intersections reveal how trunk and limbs relate; do not erase them into a clean silhouette during the drill.
5. Add only the minimum head, neck, and terminal indications needed to check the proportional ensemble. Stop before coat, muscle rendering, facial features, or small joint anatomy takes over.
6. Repeat the same subject two more times: once exaggerating trunk length or height slightly, and once correcting back toward the observed proportion. Compare which large change most alters the animal's type.

## Success Check
- The animal's type or conformation reads from the large shapes before descriptive detail.
- The trunk is established by a broad mass rather than a traced outline.
- Limbs are built as connected shape decisions with clear length and placement, not as parallel sticks added afterward.
- Overlaps remain legible enough to show how the broad shapes were assembled.
- The study can be completed quickly enough to compare several proportional versions on one page.

## Common Failures
- Choosing a tool so narrow that the exercise collapses back into contour drawing.
- Lifting and restarting so often that the body becomes a collection of unrelated patches.
- Making every limb a uniform rectangle instead of changing angle and width with the observed construction.
- Refining hooves, paws, fur, or facial features before the trunk-to-limb proportion is settled.
- Mistaking the broad geometric look for a finished style; it is a temporary proportion study.

## Notes
Bammes's §3.3 broad-medium exercise uses red chalk on its side so the trunk can be stated rapidly as a broad mass. He then swivels the chalk to create triangular and trapezoid-like limb shapes and builds the entire animal from overlapping, visibly geometric pieces. The durable lesson is not the color or the specific chalk: a broad medium can force whole-to-part proportional decisions faster than a fine point permits.

The exercise intentionally remains two-dimensional. It trains proportion and concise shape judgment before later structural and locomotive study; it does not replace three-dimensional anatomy construction when the task requires volume or foreshortening.
