# Construct a Kangaroo From a Compact Front Body, Dominant Hindquarters, and Long Tail

## Pattern Rule
**IF** a kangaroo block is reading like a generic upright animal with oversized feet or like a deer body with a long tail attached
**THEN** establish the strong hindquarter mass as the main body weight, keep the rib-cage/front-body mass comparatively compact, attach the long tapering tail as a major structural continuation from the rear body, and place the smaller forelimbs and head only after that large proportion statement is clear
**ELSE** use a more general gesture-to-mass scaffold when the subject does not need kangaroo-specific mass and tail relationships.

## Do
- Make the hindquarters the dominant body mass before refining the belly, thighs, or feet; Hultgren explicitly locates most of the animal's bulk there.
- Keep the rib-cage/front-body mass visibly smaller so the upper body does not compete with the pelvis and thighs for visual weight.
- Attach the tail broadly at the rear body, then taper it over its length. Treat it as a structural and directional member of the pose rather than as a decorative cord added after the body is finished.
- Let the tail participate in the whole-body sweep when the pose supports it. In standing or resting poses, check the actual reference for whether and how the tail contacts the ground instead of assuming one support formula for every action.
- Place the comparatively small forelimbs after the torso and hindquarter relationship is stable so they remain subordinate to the main construction rather than enlarging into a generic quadruped forequarter.
- Check the construction in both upright and extended poses so the hindquarter-to-tail relationship survives a jump, lean, or landing rather than working only in the iconic standing silhouette.

## Don't
- Build a deer-like or dog-like trunk first and try to make it read as a kangaroo by adding long feet and a tail afterward.
- Make the rib cage as large as the hindquarter mass; that erases the front-to-rear proportion contrast Hultgren uses to identify the animal.
- Reduce the tail to a thin gesture line when its base and taper are large enough to affect the silhouette and mass balance.
- Turn Hultgren's comparisons to deer or rodents into literal anatomical equivalences; use them only as source-specific visual shorthand and return to the actual animal for naturalistic structure.
- Treat the source's broad statements about the tail acting as a rudder or supporting weight as a universal biomechanical law across every gait or jump.

## Checklist
- The hindquarters dominate the mass statement before fur, feet, or facial details are added.
- The rib-cage/front-body block remains comparatively compact and does not turn the animal into a generic quadruped torso.
- The tail has a broad structural attachment, a clear taper, and a deliberate relationship to the pose's directional sweep.
- Forelimbs remain subordinate to the hindquarter-and-tail construction while still attaching through readable joints.
- The animal remains recognizably kangaroo-like when surface markings and small head details are removed.

## Notes
Hultgren's opening kangaroo page concentrates the construction around a small rib cage, relatively slight forelimbs, a long full-based tapering tail, and powerful hindquarters that carry most of the visible bulk. He also treats the tail as a strong line-of-action member. The following jumping sequence extends the body into one long launch arc and reverses that action on landing, reinforcing the existing animal-gesture and impact Patterns rather than requiring a separate kangaroo-jump mechanics object. PASS retains the mass-and-tail construction while bounding Hultgren's deer/rodent analogies and broad tail-mechanics wording as source shorthand rather than universal anatomy or biomechanics.
