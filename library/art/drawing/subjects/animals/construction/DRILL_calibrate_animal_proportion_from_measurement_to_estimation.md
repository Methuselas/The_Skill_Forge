# Calibrate Animal Proportion From Measurement to Estimation

## Practice Task
Measure a clear animal profile once with Bammes's head-length and trunk-rectangle method, then repeat the same large proportional judgment with progressively less measuring until the eye can estimate the framework and use exact measurement only to resolve uncertainty.

## Target Skill
Calibrating animal proportion from a measured profile framework, then estimating the same large relationships reliably enough that exact measurement becomes an occasional uncertainty check.

## Setup
Choose a live animal or clear side-view reference whose withers, croup, front of chest, rear projection of the pelvis, feet, neck, and head can be located without heavy foreshortening. Work small enough that several repetitions fit on one page. Use a compass or two pencils held like a compass for the measured pass.

## Instructions
1. Measure one head length from the tip of the muzzle to the rear limit of the skull and use it only as a temporary module for this subject.
2. Measure the front height from the supporting foot to the withers, the trunk length from the foremost chest projection to the rearmost pelvic projection, and the rear height to the croup. Join those limits into the main proportional rectangle.
3. Add a halfway line through the rectangle and compare the visible belly line against it. Then measure neck length with the same module and place the head length as a directional line rather than finishing the head.
4. Only after the large framework is established, place the limbs and a few major curve accents. Do not let hoof, paw, coat, or facial detail revise the rectangle silently.
5. Make a second study of the same subject without the compass. Estimate the front height, trunk length, and rear height first; then check only the relationship you are least sure about with exact measurement.
6. Repeat until the estimated framework lands close enough that exact measuring confirms rather than rebuilds the drawing. Then switch to another mammal and recalibrate instead of carrying the first animal's ratios forward.

## Success Check
- The measured study captures the subject's trunk height-to-length, ground clearance, belly level, neck length, and head scale as one coherent framework.
- Later estimated studies approach the same large relationships without reproducing the grid mechanically.
- Exact measurement is used selectively to diagnose uncertainty rather than on every line.
- The framework can be discarded while the animal's proportional type remains readable.
- Changing animals changes the measured relationships instead of preserving the previous subject's ratios.

## Common Failures
- Treating head length as a universal animal canon instead of a temporary module for one subject.
- Measuring secondary details before the trunk framework is stable.
- Using a strong three-quarter or foreshortened view as though its projected lengths were a clean profile comparison.
- Keeping the compass in constant use after the exercise has already exposed the large relationships.
- Correcting a bad proportional framework with coat, hooves, facial features, or other local identifiers.
- Memorizing Bammes's horse, cow, human, or ape example ratios instead of measuring the animal actually being drawn.

## Notes
Bammes calls the comparison of heights, lengths, and widths with one another and with the whole an analogous or simultaneous method. His practical sequence begins with a repeatable head-length module, establishes the front height, trunk length, rear height, enclosing rectangle, belly level, neck length, and head direction, and only then proceeds to limbs and accents. The durable teaching point is not the source's numeric example: it is the transition from exact measurement to trained estimation. Bammes explicitly says the goal is to work increasingly freely from life and to return to exact measurement only when something remains uncertain.
