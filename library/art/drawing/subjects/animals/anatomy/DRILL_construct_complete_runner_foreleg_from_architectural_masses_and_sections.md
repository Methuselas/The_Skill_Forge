# Construct a Complete Runner Foreleg From Architectural Masses and Sections

## Practice Task
Make one full structural study of a horse or comparable ungulate runner foreleg after reviewing its skeleton and functional muscle groups. Carry the study from the scapular shoulder mass through the hoof without beginning from the outer silhouette.

## Target Skill
Constructing a complete horse or comparable runner foreleg as a coherent architectural solid whose shoulder, forearm, carpus, tendons, and distal support retain their distinct sections and hard-soft transitions.

## Setup
Use a clear life, photographic, or anatomical reference in which the shoulder and distal leg are readable. Keep the runner-foreleg skeletal chain and functional-muscle study nearby for the first pass. Use pencil, chalk, or another medium that permits decisive structural modeling; avoid a rendering-heavy finish.

## Instructions
1. Re-establish the foreleg from the inside out. Verify the scapular direction, shoulder and elbow placement, forearm axis, carpus, metacarpus, and distal support before describing the living contour.
2. Build the shoulder area around the direction of the scapular spine. Bring the side of the shoulder girdle together as a triangular mass, let the narrow scapular form become more prominent toward the shoulder joint, and make the point of the shoulder project clearly at the front.
3. Construct the forearm as a pronounced cone-like mass that is flattened at the sides. Add one or two cross-sections so the section is explicit rather than implied by contour alone.
4. Compare adjacent sections instead of drawing one continuous tube. State the difference between the forearm and the broader carpal region, then distinguish the metacarpus as deeper front-to-back than it is wide.
5. Keep the lower leg mechanically spare. Let the flexor-tendon strands read taut, contrast the slender first phalanx with the swelling immediately above the hoof, and preserve the angular break of the elbow instead of smoothing it into a generic curve.
6. Remove or suppress coat detail and decorative modeling that do not clarify the structure. The study should still make sense as an architectural arrangement of connected masses.
7. Compare the result with the reference and name the first place where a section, projection, or hard-soft transition became generic. Rebuild that passage from the underlying skeleton and muscle organization rather than correcting only the outline.
8. If a ruminant comparison is available, make a small side study that tests the broader carpal mass and stronger angular breaks without assuming the horse and cow share identical proportions.

## Success Check
- The shoulder triangle is oriented by the scapula rather than invented from the surface silhouette.
- The point of shoulder, elbow, forearm, carpus, metacarpus, and distal support read as different connected forms instead of one tapering tube.
- At least one cross-section proves the forearm's three-dimensional cone-like, laterally flattened character.
- The carpal region is dimensionally distinct from both the forearm above and the metacarpus below.
- Tendon-dominant distal passages remain taut and spare without losing the articulated chain.
- Removing texture and minor contour detail does not destroy the structural reading.

## Common Failures
- Starting from the outside contour and trying to fit the scapula and joint structure into it afterward.
- Treating the shoulder as a round ball and losing the triangular scapular organization.
- Drawing the forearm, carpus, and metacarpus at nearly the same section and width.
- Softening the elbow, tendons, and hoof transition until the runner limb loses its hard structural accents.
- Using cross-sections as decorative bands rather than as checks on actual depth and flattening.
- Copying the cow comparison as a universal ungulate recipe rather than as a species-specific modification.

## Notes

Bammes treats the structural drawing as an intermediate learning stage that must rest on prior skeletal and muscle analysis. Figure 72 makes that "architectural" idea visible: the surface is organized by shoulder geometry, section changes, projecting joints, and hard-soft mass transitions rather than by an outline-first copy.

The prose on printed pp. 71 and 74 supplies the full checklist: scapular-spine direction controls the shoulder triangle; the scapula narrows and projects toward the joint; the forearm is a laterally flattened cone; the carpus, metacarpus, tendons, first phalanx, hoof swelling, and elbow each demand distinct sectional or angular treatment. The drill therefore completes the u16 skeletal and u17 musculature sequence at Stage 2 instead of creating a second skeletal study.
