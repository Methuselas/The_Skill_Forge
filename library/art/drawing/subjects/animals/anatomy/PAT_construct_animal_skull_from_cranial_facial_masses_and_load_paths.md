# Construct an Animal Skull From Cranial and Facial Masses and Load Paths

## Pattern Rule
**IF** an animal head is being copied from silhouette or memorized species features and its structure does not explain why the muzzle, orbit, jaw, cheek arch, and back of skull take their particular form
**THEN** separate the skull into cranial and facial masses, identify the main enclosed cavities and feeding/sensory demands, then organize the visible ridges, arches, jaw branches, and profile breaks as structural continuations and load-bearing routes between those systems
**ELSE** keep the lighter head block when the species structure is already securely understood for the present drawing.

## Do
- Start with the relationship between the rear cranial capsule and the forward facial skull instead of placing the eyes, nostrils, and teeth as independent features.
- Locate the major enclosed spaces first: braincase, orbital region, nasal passage, and oral/jaw space. Let their relative development help explain the skull type.
- Follow structural continuations through the zygomatic arch, jaw branch, cheek crest, brow or crown ridges, and back of the skull so the forms read as connected architecture rather than surface bumps.
- Compare herbivore, carnivore, and anthropoid examples by what changes in cranial/facial balance, eye direction, nasal length, jaw mechanics, and muscle-attachment architecture.
- Reduce each species to a memorable structural type, then correct that simplification against the actual skull or reference instead of treating the type as a biometric formula.

## Don't
- Do not memorize a horse, cow, dog, lion, or ape skull as an isolated contour recipe.
- Do not assume the cranial/facial boundary occupies the same place in every species.
- Do not copy Bammes's named architectural analogies as literal anatomy rules; they are mnemonic descriptions of different structural characters.
- Do not let a simplified type erase the individual animal, age, breed, or viewpoint visible in the reference.

## Checklist
- The cranial and facial skull can be identified as distinct but connected masses.
- The orbit, nasal passage, jaw, and chewing architecture have plausible structural reasons for their placement and scale.
- Major arches, ridges, and jaw processes connect into the skull instead of appearing as decorative protrusions.
- A comparison between two species can be explained by changed cranial/facial balance and function rather than by silhouette alone.
- The simplification remains adjustable to the actual animal.

## Notes
Bammes treats the animal skull as the concentrated expression of species construction. Section 8.1 divides it into a rear cranial skull and a forward facial skull, then relates their proportions to brain development, sensory organization, and the feeding apparatus. Herbivores emphasize the long facial skull and grinding system; carnivores shorten the facial skull while strengthening cutting, jaw-lever, and masticatory architecture; anthropoid apes shift the cranial/facial balance again. The durable drawing move is to understand the skull as a connected system of enclosed spaces and structural routes, not to memorize the source's individual horse, cow, dog, lion, or ape outlines.
