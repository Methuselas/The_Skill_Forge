# Model Animal Head Soft Tissue by Plastic Significance and Aperture Function

## Pattern Rule
**IF** a living animal head is being modeled as though every named facial muscle deserves equal surface volume
**THEN** separate the soft tissue into bulky chewing masses that materially change the head's plastic form and thinner aperture-control tissues that mainly move skin around the eyes, nose, mouth, and ears, then show each group according to the kind of visible change it actually produces
**ELSE** keep the soft description simple when the skull and major masticatory masses already explain the form.

## Do
- Give the masseter and temporal region enough volume to alter cheek and temple shape where the species makes those muscles plastically important.
- Treat most facial muscles as directional controls of malleable skin rather than as separate bulging anatomy forms.
- Read wrinkles, lip lift, nostril flare, eyelid action, and ear movement as effects of opening or closing sensory apertures over the skull foundation.
- Keep chewing-muscle pull related to the jaw pivot so cheek mass still belongs to a mechanical system rather than becoming arbitrary swelling.
- Let species differences change which soft forms are dominant; the living surface should complete the skull, not conceal its type.

## Don't
- Do not emboss every facial muscle onto the surface merely because it appears in an anatomy diagram.
- Do not flatten the masseter or temporal region when those masses visibly round or facet the head.
- Do not treat expression wrinkles as independent graphic symbols disconnected from aperture movement and underlying form.
- Do not let soft tissue erase the cranial/facial architecture already established in the skull study.

## Checklist
- Bulky chewing masses visibly alter form where appropriate.
- Thin facial controls change aperture shape or skin tension without becoming false anatomical lumps.
- Expression cues remain attached to the same skull and soft-tissue system.
- The jaw pivot and major chewing masses retain a functional relationship.
- Removing surface wrinkles still leaves a convincing species-specific head volume.

## Notes
Bammes distinguishes the head's masticatory muscles from most facial muscles by plastic consequence. The masseter and temporal muscles can materially round or facet the living head, especially in carnivores and anthropoid apes. Most facial muscles move skin around sensory openings and therefore show themselves more through creases, aperture changes, and expressive tension than through large volume. This hierarchy prevents an anatomy study from becoming a map of equally raised muscle labels.
