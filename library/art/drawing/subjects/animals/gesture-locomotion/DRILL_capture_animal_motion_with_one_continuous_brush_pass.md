# Capture Animal Motion With One Continuous Brush Pass

## Practice Task
Make repeated animal movement studies with a round watercolor brush, executing each study as a direct, connected brush statement completed in seconds rather than as an outline that is corrected and filled.

## Target Skill
Capturing the expression of animal movement in seconds with a direct connected brush statement instead of a corrected contour drawing.

## Setup
Use a flexible round watercolor brush that carries plenty of water, returns to a useful point, and is large enough for the intended study. Premix enough transparent watercolor that the brush does not have to stop for repeated color mixing. Begin with straightforward profile views before attempting strong foreshortening or overlapping groups.

## Instructions
1. Observe the whole movement first and commit to a simple connected route through the animal rather than planning a finished silhouette.
2. Start at the head and carry the animal toward the hindleg without breaking the study into a sequence of separately corrected contour fragments.
3. Let pressure and brush handling differentiate the forms. Press, trail, turn, and pull the brush so broad and narrow passages emerge within the same rapid statement.
4. Where forms overlap or intersect, avoid drawing every separating contour. Let the connected brush shapes carry the action while keeping the result readable.
5. If the pass breaks down, start the study again instead of repeatedly stopping to improve the same stroke sequence.
6. After profile studies become fluent, attempt three-dimensional or mutually overlapping views. Leave narrow blank-paper separations where needed so foreshortened masses do not merge into one amorphous shape.
7. Repeat enough studies that speed, continuity, and shorthand improve together; do not turn the final repeat into a slower finished painting.

## Success Check
- A study can be executed in a matter of seconds without a preliminary outline.
- The movement reads before local anatomy or surface detail is added.
- Brush pressure and direction produce useful form variation without repeated patching.
- Overlapping or foreshortened masses remain distinguishable through selective blank areas rather than extra contour inventory.
- The last repetitions are more fluent and economical, not merely more detailed.

## Common Failures
- Outlining the animal first and then filling the silhouette with wash.
- Using a brush so small that the exercise degenerates into careful line drawing.
- Breaking off repeatedly to correct, polish, or repair one study instead of beginning another direct pass.
- Adding separating lines at every overlap until continuity and movement disappear.
- Attempting complicated foreshortening before a profile can be stated fluently with the brush.
- Letting the wash merge all three-dimensional forms into one undifferentiated mass because no blank articulation was reserved.

## Notes
Bammes treats the watercolor brush as a training constraint for movement rather than as a finishing medium here. Figs. 46-50 show rapid animal studies made with a single consecutive application, while the text stresses quick work, concentrated observation, continuity, visual memory, and shorthand. His three-dimensional examples add an important limitation: a direct brush pass can lose form separation in foreshortening and overlap, so small blank areas become structural separators. The drill keeps that medium-specific tradeoff instead of generalizing every instruction to all drawing tools.
