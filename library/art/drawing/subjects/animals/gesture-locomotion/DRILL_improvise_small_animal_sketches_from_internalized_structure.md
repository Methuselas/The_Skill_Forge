# Improvise Small Animal Sketches From Internalized Structure

## Practice Task
Fill one page with small spontaneous animal sketches that begin without a prescribed pose or finished outcome, letting stored structural knowledge support the improvisation without turning the page into a polished anatomy exercise.

## Target Skill
Drawing animal action spontaneously without losing the structural knowledge accumulated through prior study.

## Setup
Use a familiar pencil, pen, felt-tip, brush, or other tool that does not require attention to learn. Work small enough that each animal can be stated quickly and compactly. Begin only after some prior observation or construction study of the animal type or movement family.

## Instructions
1. Start each sketch without naming an exact gait phase or final composition. Put down the first useful action or mass relationship that comes to mind and let the next mark answer it.
2. Keep the animal compact on the page so the whole action can be grasped and altered quickly instead of demanding broad areas of later elaboration.
3. Search boldly. If the first direction is weak, draw through it with a second or third line rather than erasing the page into caution.
4. Leave the sketch open. Do not force every study toward a final contour, complete anatomy, or presentation finish.
5. Vary pose, direction, compression, extension, or viewpoint from one sketch to the next while keeping enough of the animal's known construction that the improvisation does not collapse into symbols.
6. After several sketches, circle the ones whose movement reads immediately. For the weaker ones, name whether the failure came from missing structural knowledge, timid searching, or unnecessary elaboration.
7. Return to observation or anatomy study when the same structural uncertainty repeats; then make another short improvisation page and see whether the range of convincing forms has increased.

## Success Check
- The page contains multiple distinct actions rather than one pose repeatedly polished.
- Each study remains small, quick, and open enough that experimentation is visible.
- Searching lines clarify or redirect form instead of producing indiscriminate scribble.
- The strongest sketches communicate movement with very little descriptive detail.
- Repeated failures point to a specific gap that can be taken back to structured study.

## Common Failures
- Choosing an unfamiliar tool and spending the exercise learning the implement instead of improvising animal form.
- Making the sketches so large that empty outlines demand extensive filling and the exercise slows into rendering.
- Treating spontaneity as permission to forget proportion, pivots, or how the animal is put together.
- Erasing every exploratory line until the page looks cautious and predetermined.
- Forcing each sketch to a final solution instead of allowing useful unresolved alternatives.
- Repeating the same memorized animal symbol because the underlying form repertory is too small.

## Notes
Bammes frames this as freedom earned after study rather than freedom from study. He recommends familiar tools, small compact forms, bold repeated searching lines, and an open presentation with no demand for an ultimate solution. The following page supplies the corrective: apparently effortless shorthand depends on a large store of visual experience and knowledge of how forms fit together. Use the exercise both for fluency and as a diagnostic; when improvisation runs out of convincing options, return to the anatomy and movement study that enlarges the internal repertory.

`VAR_bammes_seed_animal_invention_from_random_marks` adds the §6.3 chance-mark route. Instead of beginning with a chosen animal action, make broad diluted watercolor marks with no predetermined concept, allow their accidental curves and masses to suggest possibilities, then use pen or a few additional strokes to crystallize one plausible animal figure. The tradeoff is deliberate: chance can break habitual symbols, but the resulting idea still has to answer to internalized structure and later functional diagnosis. Section 9.3 closes the loop by warning that unrestricted running marks lose articulation; when that happens, selectively arrest the flow with a few controlled edges or details rather than either filling every contour or letting the accident dictate the whole figure.
