# Read Horse Gait as a Support, Push, and Recovery Cycle

## Pattern Rule
**IF** a horse trot, canter, or related locomotion sequence has active-looking legs but no clear sense of which contacts are carrying or advancing the body
**THEN** read each phase by limb role—weight-taking support, push-off, pull-through or recovery, and gathering for the next contact—and track how those roles hand off around the repeating cycle
**ELSE** use the simpler moving-pivot analysis when exact gait phase is not important to the drawing.

## Do
- Judge the body and legs together; a limb that reaches toward the ground is not automatically the limb currently carrying the horse.
- Mark the contacts that take weight, the limbs that drive away from contact, and the legs that are folding or traveling toward the next placement.
- Follow the same body landmarks through several phases so the sequence reads as one continuing animal rather than unrelated attractive poses.
- Close the loop: compare the last phase to the starting phase and check that the next support relationship follows naturally.
- Use Hultgren's trot and canter pages as phase-analysis examples, then confirm precise gait timing from appropriate reference when accuracy matters.

## Don't
- Pose all four legs for maximum spread without assigning them different jobs in the cycle.
- Treat every hoof near the ground as equally weight-bearing.
- Copy one canter sequence as a universal timing chart for every lead, speed, horse, or camera view.
- Let the torso stay unchanged while the legs cycle mechanically underneath it.

## Checklist
- Each phase has at least one leg role that can be named as support, push, recovery/pull-through, or gathering for contact.
- The body position changes coherently with the handoff of support.
- Adjacent phases preserve the same horse proportions and joint chains.
- The last phase can return to the first without an unexplained jump in limb role or body position.

## Notes
The trot page is primarily visual, while the canter page explicitly labels push-off, weight-taking, rear-leg pull-through, gathering for contact, renewed rear support, and return to the beginning of the cycle. The transferable skill is to read locomotion by changing support roles rather than by memorizing a row of silhouettes. Hultgren's drawings are retained as animation-study evidence, not as a complete biomechanical gait specification.
