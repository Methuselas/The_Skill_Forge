# Use Slow-Motion Reference to Find Extreme Animal Action Phases

## Pattern Rule
**IF** a fast animal action is being guessed from memory, reduced to one generic pose, or missing the transitional extremes that make the movement read
**THEN** study successive slow-motion or frame-by-frame reference around the action, identify the strongest observed phase relationships, and construct those relationships before inventing or polishing the pose
**ELSE** use ordinary direct reference when the action is slow enough or the task only requires one stable pose.

## Do
- Inspect a short run of successive frames rather than selecting one attractive silhouette in isolation; the useful information is how the body changes from one phase to the next.
- Reduce the frames to the body trajectory, major masses, joint chains, and head/neck direction before surface detail so the extreme relationship is easy to compare.
- Look for phases where support, extension, compression, or reversal becomes unusually clear; Hultgren's deer pages use walking and jumping sequences to expose positions that are difficult to see at normal speed.
- Track the head and neck as moving parts of the sequence rather than fixing them to the trunk; compare their direction during takeoff, airborne travel, and landing against the rest of the body.
- Keep the observed sequence as evidence for the present subject. When exact species or gait timing matters, use appropriate current reference rather than treating one study sheet as a universal chart.

## Don't
- Average two distant frames into a vague in-between pose and call that motion study.
- Copy only the most stretched frame without checking what immediately precedes and follows it.
- Turn Hultgren's illustrated deer walk or jump into a fixed rule for every deer, speed, lead, terrain, or camera angle.
- Let slow-motion reference replace construction; the joints, masses, and attachments still have to remain coherent from frame to frame.

## Checklist
- The chosen action is supported by successive reference frames rather than memory alone.
- At least one extreme or transition can be described through a visible change in mass, support, limb path, or head/neck direction.
- The same animal proportions and attachment points survive across the compared phases.
- The final pose uses the observed phase relationship without claiming a universal gait law from one sequence.

## Notes
Hultgren's deer-animation pages show successive walk and jump phases, then explicitly notes that slow-motion pictures reveal very extreme positions. He also points out a changing head-and-neck relationship between landing and takeoff. PASS retains the observation method—slow the action down, compare adjacent phases, and find the informative extremes—while leaving the exact deer gait claims bounded to the source example.
