# Annotate Exploratory Sketches With Decision Cues

## Pattern Rule
**IF** an exploratory sketch contains a question, assumption, movement, local design reason, or promising branch that geometry alone will not preserve clearly
**THEN** attach a minimal local note, arrow, label, or linked detail cue exactly where that decision belongs
**ELSE** leave the sketch unannotated when the image already communicates the needed thought without help

## Do
- Write unresolved questions beside the feature that triggers them instead of storing them as detached prose.
- Use arrows to record movement, cause-and-effect, or which branch should be developed next.
- Label material, construction, or functional assumptions only when those assumptions affect how the form should be interpreted.
- Link a local enlargement back to its parent feature so the detail does not become an orphan sketch.
- Keep annotation short enough that the drawing remains the primary carrier of spatial information.

## Don't
- Do not narrate obvious contours, colors, or features that the sketch already makes clear.
- Do not place a large paragraph far from the geometry and expect the reader to infer which feature it governs.
- Do not let notes substitute for drawing a relationship that must be spatially understood.

## Checklist
- Every annotation preserves a decision, question, relationship, or next action that would otherwise be easy to lose.
- Arrows point to an unambiguous feature or motion path.
- Detail enlargements can be traced back to their parent location.
- The page remains scannable as a visual design record rather than becoming prose with decorations.

## Notes
Exploratory drawing often carries more reasoning than its contours can show. A small note can preserve why a feature exists, what remains uncertain, or which alternative should be revisited. The annotation is most useful when it is local and decision-bearing; explanatory text that merely repeats what the image already says adds clutter instead of memory.

`VAR_olofsson_pair_concept_notes_with_thumbnail_memory_cues` attaches a tiny recognizable sketch to each short concept note. The thumbnail is a retrieval cue, not a presentation miniature; use only enough drawing and accent to make the written idea easy to find and remember.
