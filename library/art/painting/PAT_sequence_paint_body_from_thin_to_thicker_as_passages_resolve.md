# Sequence Paint Body From Thin to Thicker as Passages Resolve

## Pattern Rule
**IF** a paint medium has meaningful body and uncertain early passages may need to be revised or overpainted
**THEN** keep searching passages relatively thin or low-bodied and increase paint body as placement, value, color, and edge relationships become trustworthy
**ELSE** use the body required by the intended visible effect when later revision cost is not a material constraint.

## Do
- Keep early paint easy to revise when drawing and placement are still moving.
- Increase body where the passage has become settled enough that substantial reworking is unlikely.
- Distinguish physical thickness from transparency: a thin passage may still be opaque, and a transparent passage is not defined by low body alone.
- Reserve the heaviest applications for statements whose form, value, color, and edge roles are already clear.

## Don't
- Do not bury uncertain structure under heavy paint and then treat the resulting correction cost as inevitable.
- Do not make "thin to thick" a mechanical timeline when a specific visual effect requires an earlier heavy statement.
- Do not use impasto as evidence that a passage is resolved.

## Checklist
- Early searching areas remain inexpensive to change.
- Increasing paint body corresponds to increasing decision certainty or a deliberate visible effect.
- Thickness and transparency are being judged as separate variables.
- Heavy passages are not concealing unresolved drawing or value structure.

## Notes
The sequencing rule is about the physical consequence of paint body on revision and overpainting. It complements, rather than replaces, separate decisions about transparency, opacity, edge construction, and surface finish.
