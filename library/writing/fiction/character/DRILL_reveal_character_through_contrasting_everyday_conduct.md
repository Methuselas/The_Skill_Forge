# Reveal Character through Contrasting Everyday Conduct

## Practice Task
Draft the same character performing an ordinary activity under two meaningfully different conditions, then revise the pair so material conduct changes the reader's inference without becoming automatic proof of personality.

## Target Skill
Use procedure, sensory interaction, selection, timing, adaptation, and consequence to reveal how a particular character meets a task in context.

## Setup
Choose a character with a current desire, one relevant capability or knowledge gap, and one material or social constraint. Choose an activity the story can make consequential: preparing or eating food, beginning the day, performing work, practising a skill, making something, travelling locally, or playing.

## Instructions
1. Define a private or low-pressure baseline and a contrasting condition that changes at least two of these: witness, authority, relationship, time, resources, bodily capacity, information, risk, or desired outcome.
2. When the activity is unfamiliar, research its ordinary sequence, tools, sensory feedback, common errors, safety limits, and insider vocabulary. Mark what remains uncertain and retain only details needed for action or inference.
3. List five observable decisions available during the activity, such as preparation, order, pace, improvisation, care, delegation, concealment, recovery from error, or stopping.
4. Draft the baseline scene without naming a trait. Make the character handle specific materials, tools, spaces, or bodily demands, and let at least one decision create a small result.
5. Draft the contrasting scene with the same governing desire still active. Change conduct only where the altered conditions, relationship, knowledge, or stakes can plausibly account for it.
6. For every intended inference, mark the evidence and at least one competing explanation. Revise any detail that depends on a stereotype about work, food, cleanliness, competence, leisure, class, culture, gender, age, or embodiment.
7. Select one difference that changes a relationship, available action, or consequence. Remove procedural detail that only proves research and sensory detail that does not affect experience or choice.
8. Ask a reader what changed in the inferred character and which actions caused that revision. If the reader supplies a fixed trait from only one condition, add qualifying evidence or reduce the claim.

## Success Check
- Both scenes enact the activity through specific materials, procedure, sensation, or spatial interaction rather than summarizing a lifestyle.
- The contrast changes at least two conditions while preserving the character's governing desire.
- Unfamiliar procedure is plausible, selective, and free of a jargon dump.
- At least one behavioral difference changes relation, action, knowledge, leverage, or consequence.
- Stable-character claims rest on evidence across conditions; a response explainable by pressure remains a situated hypothesis.
- No activity, food, job, leisure pursuit, competence level, or housekeeping practice functions as demographic or moral shorthand.

## Common Failures
- Writing an inventory of routine actions with no desire, resistance, or result.
- Treating efficiency, mess, dietary choice, competitiveness, technical skill, or physical ease as a complete personality diagnosis.
- Changing the character's underlying desire merely to manufacture contrast.
- Displaying every researched term instead of selecting details that affect action.
- Giving two scenes different surfaces but the same decision and inference.
- Explaining the intended trait immediately after the behavior has made an inference available.

## Notes
Ordinary conduct becomes characterization when a task exposes choices under particular conditions. Comparison protects the draft from mistaking one hurried breakfast, failed attempt, polished performance, or competitive response for the whole person. The variant `writing_fiction_variant_stage_relationship_through_competitive_or_cooperative_play` uses a shared rule-bound activity as relational pressure; its subject is how people act toward one another, not how to design the game.

`writing_fiction_variant_compare_embodied_responses_to_contrasting_news` tests emotional embodiment without turning the body into a codebook. Establish the character's ordinary movement, sensory access, current bodily condition, relation to the messenger, and public constraints. Draft the first moments after favorable news and after unfavorable news in the same setting. In each version, mark separately what the character feels internally, what an observer could see, what the character chooses to do, and what either party infers. Give every intended inference at least one competing explanation, then retain the details whose timing, deviation from baseline, and consequences make them useful. The two responses may overlap, reverse expectation, or remain mixed; a clean opposition is not a success requirement.
