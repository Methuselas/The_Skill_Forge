# Test Character Motivation from Need to Attainment

## Practice Task
Diagnose one character's operative need, compare aimless and goal-directed movement, then test whether approaching or achieving the stated goal exposes a coherent competing motive.

## Target Skill
Turn an explicit or reader-legible need into purposeful conduct while making self-defeat or disillusionment arise from established character pressure rather than arbitrary reversal.

## Setup
Choose a central character whose scenes feel inert, whose stated desire remains generic, or whose behavior near success is hard to explain. Prepare one ordinary walking situation and one consequential meeting, opportunity, or destination the character could move toward.

## Instructions
1. Inventory five need domains: bodily survival and comfort; safety, health, and resources; belonging and intimacy; esteem and status; fulfillment and purpose. Mark each as secure, threatened, or unmet for this character without assuming that the domains must activate in a fixed order.
2. State what the character claims to want, which need their repeated choices actually protect, what blocks it, and whether the character recognizes the connection. If awareness is incomplete, list two actions, avoidances, or sacrifices from which a reader could infer the operative need.
3. Draft a brief passage in which the character takes an aimless walk. Give the character no appointment or destination that can organize the movement.
4. Draft a comparable passage in which the same character walks to the consequential meeting, opportunity, or destination. Let purpose alter attention, pace, route, preparation, delay, or another material choice.
5. Compare the passages and mark the differences caused by the goal. Reject an unrelated surprise incident as evidence of motivation even when it makes the aimless passage more eventful.
6. Choose one attainment test and write the reason for choosing it: the character nears the stated goal but blocks success, or attains it and discovers that it does not satisfy the operative need.
7. Before drafting the threshold scene, name the competing fear, need, identity, excuse, or future cost that makes the response intelligible. Seed at least one sign of that pressure before the decisive action.
8. Draft the threshold scene through choice and consequence. Revise until the reader can infer both the pursued goal and the pressure that defeats or revises it without an explanatory diagnosis after the action.

## Success Check
- The completed need inventory marks every domain as secure, threatened, or unmet and does not treat the list as a universal order of fulfillment.
- Both walking passages were drafted under comparable conditions, and the goal-directed version contains at least two observable changes tied to its destination or appointment.
- The comparison names which differences came from purpose rather than merely asserting that the second passage is more interesting.
- An arbitrary accident, stranger, or spectacle in the aimless passage is identified as the rejected near-miss: it may create an event, but it does not demonstrate character motivation.
- The chosen attainment branch has a written reason, an established competing motive, and evidence planted before the apparently anti-goal response.
- When the character cannot name the operative need, at least two actions, avoidances, or sacrifices make it inferable to a reader.
- Blocking or revising the goal changes an action, relationship, available option, or consequence rather than ending as a private label.

## Common Failures
- Ranking the need domains mechanically instead of using them to locate current pressure.
- Giving the purposeful passage a different setting, crisis, or cast so the comparison no longer tests motivation.
- Adding random excitement to the aimless walk and crediting the character's desire for it.
- Calling self-sabotage irrational without identifying what the character protects by it.
- Revealing that the goal was false only after attainment, with no earlier discrepancy between the stated aim and operative need.
- Explaining the character's psychology after the decisive action instead of making motive legible through prepared behavior and consequence.

## Notes
Need domains are prompts for finding pressure, not a diagnosis or a mandatory ladder. A character may protect belonging over safety, sacrifice status for bodily relief, or pursue achievement while denying the intimacy actually at stake.

The aimless comparison isolates what a destination contributes to selection and behavior. An unplanned incident can rescue a passage as improvisation, but it does not prove that the character generated the movement. The attainment test then complicates purpose without discarding causality: apparent self-defeat becomes credible when success threatens another need or identity, while disappointment becomes credible when the stated goal has functioned as a proxy for something it cannot supply.
