# Trace One Embodied Condition through a Character's Life

## Practice Task
Hold one physical condition stable while generating varied material, social, and personal consequences, then draft two scenes that show the character responding differently across time or context.

## Target Skill
Make embodiment consequential without deriving personality from appearance or treating one response as inevitable.

## Setup
Choose one physical condition relevant to a fictional character, such as height, mobility, pain, stamina, a scar, sensory access, hair, a chronic condition, or another bodily fact. If its lived consequences fall outside your experience, prepare bounded research questions and consult varied credible perspectives before drafting.

## Instructions
1. State the physical fact without personality labels, symbolic meaning, or presumed life outcome.
2. List at least three material affordances or constraints involving environments, tools, clothing, movement, work, rest, care, or access.
3. List at least three possible social responses, including contradictory responses from different people or institutions. Keep those interpretations separate from the physical fact, and mark any earlier response that plausibly shaped the character's expectations.
4. List at least three possible ways the character might understand, present, ignore, enjoy, resist, accommodate, or revise a relationship to the body. Do not derive confidence, anger, eating, desirability, or coping from one past event without intervening evidence.
5. Generate adaptations, skills, habits, and choices that could develop from repeated interaction among the body, environment, resources, and relationships.
6. Reject the first chain that makes morality, intelligence, temperament, desirability, or destiny follow automatically from the body. Select one route grounded in this character's desires, circumstances, and available options.
7. Draft one scene in which the embodied condition changes a concrete action, access problem, relation, or consequence without explanatory inventory.
8. Draft a second scene at another time or under changed conditions. Keep the bodily fact stable while changing environment, social response, resources, self-interpretation, or adaptation. When the scenes repeat a related social judgment, distinguish the learned expectation from the current person's actual conduct.
9. Compare the scenes and mark what remains continuous, what changes, and what caused the changed response.

## Success Check
- The physical fact is stated without a personality verdict.
- Material, social, and self-interpretive consequences are generated separately before a causal route is selected.
- More than one plausible response exists, including contradictory social and personal possibilities.
- Any unfamiliar consequential experience is researched rather than completed through analogy or confidence.
- Both scenes make embodiment affect action, access, relationship, or consequence instead of presenting a static portrait.
- The second scene changes response through identifiable conditions while preserving continuity of person and body.
- Any persistent expectation and changed response can be traced through intervening experience rather than assumed from age alone.

## Common Failures
- Using body shape, facial features, disability, or presentation as proof of virtue, vice, intelligence, class, or temperament.
- Treating prejudice from another character as objective narration.
- Making every person with the condition share the same coping method or desire.
- Listing hardships without capabilities, pleasures, ordinary concerns, or adaptation.
- Changing the physical fact between scenes so contextual effects cannot be compared.
- Explaining the entire causal chain instead of letting selected consequences enter action.
- Making one childhood judgment automatically produce the adult's confidence, anger, eating, coping method, or relationship choices.

## Notes
This exercise differs from counterfactual transformation because it does not replace the starting fact. It changes the surrounding conditions and the character's developing relation to them. The comparison exposes deterministic assumptions: if one body seems to permit only one personality or future, the option set is too narrow or insufficiently researched.

When an earlier and later scene contain a related social judgment, treat continuity as a question rather than a command. An old response may become expectation, vigilance, humor, masking, resistance, selective vulnerability, or something the character has ceased to grant authority. Name the intervening causes that make persistence or change plausible.
