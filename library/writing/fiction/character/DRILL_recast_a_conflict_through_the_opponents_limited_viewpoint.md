# Recast a Conflict through the Opponent's Limited Viewpoint

## Practice Task
Rewrite one dispute in third-person limited through the character whose position you resist, then revise until that person has a credible interior case without erasing the harm or disagreement.

## Target Skill
Inhabit an antagonistic or unsympathetic character through motive, attention, and self-justification instead of caricature or retrospective acquittal.

## Setup
Choose a conflict with two legible positions and a consequential point of disagreement. Use a fictional dispute or transform a remembered one so the exercise can change facts, motives, setting, and outcome freely.

## Instructions
1. State your default account of the conflict in three sentences, including what you think the opposing character wanted and got wrong.
2. List what the opponent believes is at stake, what evidence that person notices, what pressure remains hidden from the other side, and what self-description feels sincere rather than flattering.
3. Draft 250 to 400 words in third-person limited through the opponent. Exclude private thoughts from every other character.
4. Filter setting, gestures, and dialogue through what this character fears, values, misunderstands, or needs to prove.
5. Give the viewpoint character one defensible perception and one consequential blind spot. Do not insert an authorial verdict to tell readers how to judge either one.
6. Compare the draft with the three-sentence default account. Mark every place where the new access changed selected evidence, causal interpretation, or sympathy.
7. Revise any sentence that sounds like a prosecutor mocking the character or an advocate excusing the result. Keep the person intelligible and the consequences intact.

## Success Check
- The default account is written first, including what the opposing character is thought to have wanted and got wrong, so the starting bias is on the page.
- The opponent's stakes, noticed evidence, hidden pressure, and sincere self-description are listed before drafting.
- The draft holds third-person limited, with no other character's private thoughts entering.
- Setting, gesture, and dialogue are filtered through what this character notices rather than narrated alongside them.
- The opponent's case becomes credible without the harm being erased or the disagreement dissolved. A version where the conflict evaporates has replaced the exercise with a reconciliation.
- What the original account got wrong about this person is nameable afterwards, or the run states that nothing changed and why.

## Common Failures
- Writing the opponent as secretly agreeing with the preferred side.
- Substituting a tragic backstory for a present-tense motive that affects the scene.
- Making every hostile interpretation irrational so the exercise confirms the original judgment.
- Granting the viewpoint character information learned only after the dispute.
- Confusing empathy with forgiveness, innocence, or equal responsibility.

## Notes
Interior access expands imaginative range by forcing the writer to build causality from inside a position that initially feels wrong. The useful result is not moral equivalence. It is a conflict in which each action grows from a recognizable arrangement of evidence, desire, fear, and self-protection, allowing judgment to arise from the complete scene rather than from caricature.
