# Compress Cast without Flattening Main Characters

## Pattern Rule
**IF** a short or flash fiction draft spends scarce space introducing, naming, and distinguishing more people than its central action can develop
**THEN** eliminate or combine roles, reserve complexity for the small central cast, and identify minor figures only as precisely as their function requires

## Do
- Test whether two supporting roles can become one person whose relation to the protagonist carries both pressures.
- Keep the central cast near the number the story can place in consequential interaction; in very brief work, three main characters may already be plenty.
- Give retained names distinct sounds and shapes so the reader never spends attention sorting similar labels.
- Leave a one-appearance clerk, driver, neighbor, or passerby unnamed when role and action orient the reader more efficiently.
- Give a minor figure one precise physical or behavioral detail when it changes orientation, action, expectation, relation, or pressure rather than merely making the figure colorful.

## Don't
- Solve a crowded cast by reducing every person to a label.
- Name a minor figure merely because a real-world encounter would involve introductions.
- Keep two characters who perform the same narrative pressure without creating a meaningful contrast.
- Use similar names in a brief work where there is little space to reinforce identity.
- Treat a randomized name, nationality, gender, or bodily feature as completed characterization or decorative diversity.

## Checklist
- Each central character changes a choice, conflict, or consequence.
- No two supporting figures are interchangeable in function.
- Main characters retain conflicting qualities and desires after compression.
- Every supplied name earns the attention required to learn it.
- An unnamed minor figure remains easy to track through role and action.
- Any distinguishing detail performs a scene function without reducing identity or embodiment to a stock type.

## Notes
Cast compression is allocation, not simplification. Brief fiction gains room for depth by reducing how many people demand orientation. Flat characters can still serve as foils, obstacles, or messengers, but their narrowness must sharpen the central action rather than imitate full development.
