# Map Character Relationships by Knowledge and Pressure

## Practice Task
Build a relationship map around one focal character, then derive a scene or story turn from a pressured connection and an unequal distribution of knowledge.

## Target Skill
Design character relationships as an active network of wants, leverage, loyalty, conflict, and partial knowledge rather than as a static cast list.

## Setup
Choose a premise with one focal character and at least three other people who could affect that character's choices. Use a page large enough to draw connections among every relevant person rather than only spokes from the center.

## Instructions
1. Put the focal character at the center and write the immediate desire, duty, fear, or problem that makes the surrounding relationships consequential.
2. Add the people the focal character knows directly. On each connection, write what each person wants from, offers to, withholds from, or can threaten in the other.
3. Add people who matter to the situation but are unknown, forgotten, misidentified, or only indirectly connected to the focal character.
4. Draw connections among the surrounding characters. Label each connection with a specific relation, exchange, shared history, rivalry, dependency, or secret rather than a category such as friend or family alone.
5. Mark what the focal character knows about each connection as known, suspected, misunderstood, or hidden. When knowledge is asymmetric, mark what the other participant knows separately.
6. Circle two or three connections that can change a choice if they are used, broken, exposed, concealed, or misread. For each, write one resulting action and the option it removes or creates.
7. Select the connection with the strongest consequence. Outline or draft a short scene in which someone acts through that relationship or unequal knowledge and leaves at least one character with changed options.
8. Compare the scene with the map. Remove inert nodes, repair any unexplained knowledge transfer, and preserve offstage connections only when they can affect later action or interpretation.

## Success Check
- The map includes consequential connections among secondary characters, not only labels radiating from the focal character.
- At least one relationship records a two-way exchange, dependency, rivalry, or pressure rather than a social category alone.
- Known, suspected, misunderstood, and hidden information are distinguishable where they change conduct.
- The drafted scene uses a mapped connection to alter a decision, relation, risk, or available option.
- A reader can trace the scene's turn back to the mapped relationship without receiving a separate explanation of the whole network.
- A static family tree or cast roster does not pass unless its connections generate action or changing interpretation.

## Common Failures
- Treating every relationship as equally important and producing an unreadable web with no pressure center.
- Naming affection, hostility, or kinship without showing what either person can do to the other.
- Marking a secret that never changes behavior, disclosure, or consequence.
- Giving every character the writer's complete knowledge and erasing misunderstanding or discovery.
- Forcing a surprising connection after drafting when no prior motive, access, or evidence can support it.

## Notes
The map is a private causal model, not exposition to copy into the story. Its value lies in revealing indirect pressure: one person can alter a relationship without the focal character knowing that person exists, and two secondary characters can create consequences that a center-and-spokes cast list conceals. Unequal knowledge becomes useful only when it changes what someone attempts, withholds, risks, or understands.
