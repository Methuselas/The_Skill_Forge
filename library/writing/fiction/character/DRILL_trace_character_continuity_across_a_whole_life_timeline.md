# Trace Character Continuity across a Whole-Life Timeline

## Practice Task
Map one character's life beyond the visible story, draft brief scenes at consequence-bearing transitions, and choose the bounded period the story should narrate.

## Target Skill
Maintain causal and psychological continuity across a fictional life while distinguishing useful private history from the selected story readers need.

## Setup
Choose a central fictional character with a current desire, contradiction, relationship, or problem. Mark the period the planned story currently covers, but treat that boundary as a design hypothesis to test.

## Instructions
1. Draw a timeline from the earliest family, birth, or childhood condition that can affect the character through a plausible later life. Mark the current story window separately.
2. Add six to eight transitions that change what the character wants, knows, can do, owes, fears, or expects. Choose them for consequence rather than filling conventional age categories.
3. For each transition, state its cause and one downstream effect on a relationship, opportunity, habit, belief, or later choice. Repair any event that appears only because a familiar life-stage stereotype supplied it.
4. Select four transitions spread across the life. Draft a brief scene at each in which action, speech, attention, or material circumstance makes both continuity and change observable.
5. Compare the scenes. Mark one trait, desire, relationship, or coping method that persists; one that changes; and the event that makes the change plausible.
6. If the planned story ends the character's life early, sketch one explicitly hypothetical later path as if that ending had not occurred. Use it to expose unrealized values or pressures, not to overwrite the chosen ending.
7. Identify the critical period the actual story should select. State what begins the governing pressure, what completes or transforms it, and why earlier and later events belong outside the main frame.
8. Draft an optional epilogue beat only when a later consequence remains structurally necessary. Test the story window with and without it, and retain it only when it changes closure or interpretation rather than reporting subsequent biography.

## Success Check
- Every marked transition changes a relationship, opportunity, belief, ability, desire, or later choice.
- At least four brief scenes were drafted and compared rather than merely predicted from timeline labels.
- One persistent element and one changed element can be traced across the scenes through specific evidence.
- A familiar milestone with no character-specific cause is removed or revised, demonstrating that age categories did not write the life automatically.
- The selected story window has a stated beginning pressure and completion boundary, with a reason for excluding surrounding life.
- If a hypothetical later path was used, it reveals unrealized character pressure without silently becoming established story fact.
- The epilogue decision is defended by its effect on closure or interpretation, not by a preference to reveal what happened later.

## Common Failures
- Filling the timeline with culturally familiar milestones that have no cause in this character's circumstances.
- Treating every life event as equally important and drafting an unselected biography instead of a story.
- Changing personality completely between stages without an intervening pressure or decision.
- Preserving a favorite future after the selected story has made it impossible.
- Adding an epilogue that repeats closure or reports later facts without changing the completed movement.

## Notes
A story selects from a life larger than its frame. Designing that larger field can make present behavior feel inhabited and keep later consequences coherent, but the private timeline is not an obligation to narrate everything imagined. The exercise uses separated scenes to test continuity directly: chronology succeeds when pressure propagates through particular choices and relations, not when a list merely advances the character's age.
