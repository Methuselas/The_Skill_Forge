# Revise One Event through Summary Scene and Blend

## Practice Task
Write the same consequential event as compact summary, an enacted scene, and a selective blend, then retain the treatment whose narrative speed best matches the event's function.

## Target Skill
Select narrative speed and information density by consequence and reader effect instead of obeying a mechanical showing-and-telling rule.

## Setup
Choose a drafted or planned event that includes a change in knowledge, relationship, desire, or available action. Mark the facts readers must know, the inference they should make, and the duration the larger story must cross.

## Instructions
1. Write a summary of no more than 150 words that states the event's causal change, crosses any necessary time, and omits particulars that do not affect what follows.
2. Write the event again as a scene of no more than 300 words. Use selected action, setting, thought, and direct speech so readers can experience the turn without an abstract explanation of its meaning.
3. Underline details in the scene that create inference, character presence, or consequential timing; cross out details whose only effect is to make the passage longer or more pictorial.
4. Write a blended version of no more than 250 words. Use summary for background and connective duration, then enter scene at the pressure point and leave after its consequence is established.
5. Compare the three versions for pace, orientation, emotional force, reader activity, and false signals of importance.
6. Select the version that serves the governing story. Import a sentence or detail from another version only when it performs a missing job without disturbing the chosen speed.
7. Place the selected passage back into its surrounding draft and test whether the transitions into and out of it preserve chronology and momentum.

## Success Check
- All three versions preserve the event's essential causal change.
- The scene earns its space through an effect that summary cannot provide as strongly.
- The summary remains concrete enough to convey movement without becoming a dossier or abstract verdict.
- The blended version changes speed at a consequential point rather than alternating modes mechanically.
- The final selection can be defended by reader effect, not by a general preference for more or less detail.
- Surrounding passages do not repeat information the selected version already makes inferable.

## Common Failures
- Treating the scene pass as a quota of sensory details rather than an enacted change.
- Making the summary vague enough that causality or elapsed time disappears.
- Changing the event's facts across versions so narrative mode cannot be compared cleanly.
- Expanding an incidental moment until readers expect a payoff the story will not provide.
- Combining every strong sentence from all three attempts and recreating the original imbalance.

## Notes
The comparison isolates narrative distance from content. Summary can carry years, causal context, or a narrator's revealing judgment; scene can make exact behavior, timing, and subtext available for inference. The blend is not automatically superior. Its value appears only when temporal reach and immediate experience both perform necessary, distinct work.
