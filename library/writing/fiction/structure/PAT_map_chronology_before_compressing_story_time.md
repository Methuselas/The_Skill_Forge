# Map Chronology Before Compressing Story Time

## Pattern Rule
**IF** a story compresses long duration, omits backstory, or moves between past, present, and future
**THEN** map the underlying event timeline first, then select and arrange only the temporal shifts that strengthen the narrated action
**ELSE** keep the presented order simple enough that chronology does not become an unnecessary task for the reader

## Do
- Separate the complete underlying chronology from the selected, arranged version readers will receive.
- Place every consequential event on a before-during-after timeline even when most of that line will remain outside the draft.
- Mark the dramatic present, if the story has one, and distinguish events happening now from habitual conditions, remembered episodes, and anticipated outcomes.
- Mark what each flashback or forward changes in the reader's understanding of the present scene.
- Signal elapsed time and the relation between selected scenes when compressing weeks or years into a few transitions.
- After mapping the full sequence, allocate scene and summary by narrative pressure rather than assuming every consequential event needs equal duration on the page.
- Know the omitted history well enough that present actions, memories, objects, and relationships remain consistent with it.
- In very short fiction, prefer a concentrated present action and admit temporal departures only when their yield exceeds their orientation cost.

## Don't
- Let a character's confusion about time become the writer's confusion about event order.
- Omit information because it has not been imagined, then call the resulting gap mystery or compression.
- Insert a flashback merely because it occurred earlier or contains vivid material.
- Spend more space entering and exiting a temporal shift than the shift contributes to the live story.

## Checklist
- The writer can state when every consequential event occurs relative to the narrated present, or name the recurrent temporal pattern when no stable dramatic present governs the form.
- The presented story selects from a coherent larger chronology rather than reproducing every available event.
- Each temporal shift supplies a fact, pressure, or reinterpretation the current action needs.
- Compressed transitions show enough event to preserve change rather than replacing the story with summary.
- Omitted history supports visible details without requiring readers to receive all of it.
- No continuity error depends on the order in which the draft happened to be written.

## Notes
Narrative time is selected time, not a transcript of duration. The underlying chronology contains the larger field of imagined events; the presented story chooses, omits, and rearranges from that field. A lifetime can pass in one sentence while a remembered second occupies pages. Mapping establishes what happened and when; `writing_fiction_anchor_narrative_time_in_dramatic_present_and_habitual_world` organizes present and recurrent time, while `writing_fiction_allocate_scene_and_summary_by_narrative_pressure` decides how much narrative space each selected event receives. That freedom works only when the writer remains oriented beneath the arrangement. Knowledgeable omission strengthens a story because the visible portion rests on a coherent whole; omission from ignorance leaves a hole that readers feel even when they cannot name it.
