# Write a Full-Disclosure Fiction Synopsis

## Pattern Rule
**IF** a receiver needs a brief evaluative account of a completed story or novel
**THEN** present the protagonist, situation, setting, governing problem, major causal turns, conflict, resolution, and ending as one coherent present-tense account within the requested length
**ELSE** use the receiver's requested form rather than substituting a synopsis for a query, sample, or nonfiction chapter outline

## Do
- Establish early who the story centers on, what problem or desire governs the action, and where the main conflict lies.
- Name the setting and essential characters only to the degree needed to understand decisions and consequences.
- Select the turns that change the causal direction of the story; connect them so a reader can see how one pressure, choice, or discovery produces the next.
- Reveal surprises, reversals, climax, resolution, and ending even when the finished story withholds them for suspense.
- Write fiction as a continuous whole, normally in present tense, unless the receiver specifies another form.
- Preserve the story's distinguishing premise and emotional or thematic stakes without trying to reproduce all of its dialogue, scenes, or prose texture.
- Ask a capable reader unfamiliar with the manuscript whether the account flows, covers the whole action, and leaves any causal question unintentionally unanswered.

## Don't
- Write teaser copy that hides the ending or asks rhetorical questions instead of explaining the story.
- List characters, chapters, or events without showing the causal conflict that joins them.
- Spend scarce space on minor subplots, backstory, atmosphere, or descriptive language that does not change the governing action.
- Praise the story, explain what the reader should feel, or argue for its quality in place of demonstrating its structure.
- Assume knowledge from the manuscript that an evaluator cannot recover from the synopsis itself.
- Apply the continuous-fiction form to nonfiction when a chapter-by-chapter outline is requested.

## Checklist
- The central character, situation, setting, and governing problem appear early.
- Major turns form a comprehensible causal line rather than an event inventory.
- Conflict, climax or decisive change, resolution, and ending are disclosed.
- Essential characters can be distinguished without an overloaded cast list.
- The account fits the receiver's requested length and presentation.
- A reader who has not seen the manuscript can explain what happens, to whom, why, and how it ends.

## Notes
A synopsis serves editorial evaluation, not the suspense experience of the finished work. Its compression tests whether the writer can distinguish structural necessity from scene-level richness. Full disclosure lets a receiver judge the complete design: whether pressures escalate, choices cause consequences, characters remain legible, and the ending resolves or transforms the central problem. The story's prose demonstrates style elsewhere; the synopsis demonstrates command of the whole.
