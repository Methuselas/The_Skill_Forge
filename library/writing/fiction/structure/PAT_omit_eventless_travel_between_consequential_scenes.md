# Omit Eventless Travel between Consequential Scenes

## Pattern Rule
**IF** a draft follows a character between locations while no relevant pressure, perception, decision, relation, or consequence changes
**THEN** cut or sharply compress the transit and begin the next scene at the earliest consequential moment in the new place
**ELSE** dramatize the journey when movement itself creates the story's action, constraint, discovery, or transformation

## Do
- State only the departure, elapsed time, or arrival fact the reader needs to remain oriented after the cut.
- Keep a transit beat when the route limits escape, changes status, exposes information, forces company, or makes the destination consequential.
- Test whether an arrival can carry the necessary transition through changed light, clothing, fatigue, weather, or another result of travel.
- Preserve private route and timing knowledge even when the prose jumps directly between scenes.

## Don't
- Record boarding, driving, waiting, and arrival merely because the writer imagined them in chronological order.
- Use scenic windows and passing landmarks to disguise a transition in which nothing develops.
- Cut travel that contains the story's governing ordeal, encounter, pursuit, or irreversible crossing.
- Jump locations without enough orientation to distinguish deliberate compression from a continuity error.

## Checklist
- Every dramatized journey changes what a character knows, wants, can do, or must face.
- Every omitted journey leaves the reader with sufficient time and location orientation.
- Arrival begins where pressure resumes rather than with routine unpacking or navigation.
- Travel time remains consistent with the underlying geography.
- Removing the transit improves momentum without erasing causality, atmosphere, or consequence.

## Notes
Narrative chronology does not require a transcript of movement. A cut can place a character at the next live location as readily as film does, provided the writer remains oriented beneath the omission. The exception is functional, not scenic: when the route changes the action or the traveller, the journey is already a scene.
