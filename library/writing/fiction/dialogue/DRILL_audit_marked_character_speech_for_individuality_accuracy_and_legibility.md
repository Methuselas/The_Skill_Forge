# Audit Marked Character Speech for Individuality Accuracy and Legibility

## Practice Task
Audit one passage containing conspicuously marked character speech, rebuild the voice from individual and social conditions, and compare revised marking levels until the dialogue remains specific without becoming stereotype or decoding work.

## Target Skill
Make accent, dialect, multilingual wording, slang, register, pronunciation cues, or speech difference serve a particular character and scene without treating standardized prose as the only unmarked human voice.

## Setup
Choose a scene in which one character's speech is marked more conspicuously than the surrounding narration or other speakers. State what the character wants from the listener, what changes by the end, and why the character's language history or speech difference matters now.

## Instructions
1. Map the character's individual repertoire: languages and dialects used, where and with whom each was acquired, current fluency, communities of use, occupational vocabulary, preferred forms of address, and contexts that prompt accommodation, code-switching, concealment, formality, or relaxation.
2. Name every access gap. Research the relevant present or historical usage, pronunciation, grammar, social meaning, and variation through multiple credible sources or informed perspectives. Do not turn one consultant, example, or community member into a universal authority.
3. Build an unmarked voice profile from attention, knowledge, motive, sentence movement, pace, directness, figurative habits, assumptions, evasions, and relation to the listener. Draft five lines from that profile before adding conspicuous surface markers.
4. Inventory every marker in the passage and label its job: syntax, idiom, word choice, rhythm, code-switch, formality, pronunciation cue, delivery, repair, or listener response. Remove markers whose only function is nationality, class, age, disability, exotic atmosphere, or comic difference.
5. Compare how narration and every other speaker are represented. Mark asymmetric respelling, grammar labeling, translation, explanation, or comic framing that makes one voice deviant while treating another accent or dialect as neutral.
6. Produce a minimally marked and a more strongly marked version. Read both silently for effortless attribution and aloud for rhythm, then identify where marking obscures content, forces a performance the page cannot teach, or makes the character sound less capable than the situation supports.
7. When the stakes or access gap warrant it, seek informed review for false notes, unintended meanings, readability, and harmful framing. Ask about this character and passage rather than asking anyone to approve representation of an entire group.
8. Select the lightest or strongest version that performs the intended job. Confirm that removing the marked feature would alter character, relation, setting, history, or consequence, and that the character still possesses desires, competencies, defects, pleasures, and choices beyond delivery.

## Success Check
- The language repertoire and present listener explain the selected register more precisely than a demographic label does.
- Voice remains distinguishable through attention, motive, syntax, rhythm, and relation before conspicuous spelling or vocabulary is added.
- Every retained marker has an identified scene function and supported usage.
- The audit compares all speakers and narration for asymmetric treatment.
- Silent and oral tests preserve both legibility and intended spoken movement.
- Informed feedback, when needed, tests specific craft choices without treating one person as representative of a community.
- The character's speech affects the scene without becoming the character's entire identity or value.

## Common Failures
- Starting from a nationality, region, class, age, or diagnosis and inventing a bundle of expected errors.
- Calling one voice standard while conspicuously encoding only the less powerful speaker's pronunciation.
- Using phonetic spelling, untranslated words, slang, profanity, or a catchphrase as the sole distinction among voices.
- Copying one observed speaker's distinctive expression or treating one source as a complete language model.
- Making reader difficulty stand in for authenticity.
- Turning imperfect fluency or a speech difference into automatic ignorance, comedy, innocence, menace, or revelation.
- Preserving researched markers after they cease to affect character, relation, setting, or action.

## Notes
All speakers have accents, dialects, registers, and situation-dependent repertoires. Marking becomes conspicuous only relative to an editorial and narrative baseline, so the audit examines who receives ordinary spelling and who must be performed for the reader. Accuracy matters, but voice is more than sound: a particular person selects, orders, assumes, withholds, and addresses language under social conditions.

The variant `writing_fiction_variant_signal_shared_story_language_through_selective_cues` establishes a translation convention instead of making fluent speakers perform foreignness in their own language. Name or imply the governing language early, render most dialogue fluently in the narrative language, and retain original wording only when the specific word changes address, meaning, status, sound, or plot.

The variant `writing_fiction_variant_represent_speech_difference_without_verbal_costume` begins from variability and interaction. Research what changes with fatigue, stress, familiarity, environment, communication support, or listener behavior; then render the minimum cues needed at pressure points. The scene should reveal what the character is trying to communicate and what others do with it, not ask respelling to carry the whole portrayal.
