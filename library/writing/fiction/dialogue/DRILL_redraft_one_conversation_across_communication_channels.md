# Redraft One Conversation across Communication Channels

## Practice Task
Write the same difficult exchange face-to-face, through audio only, and through a third mediated channel, then revise each version until the channel changes how the conversation can unfold.

## Target Skill
Use communication-channel affordances and constraints to change misunderstanding, concealment, revelation, timing, or consequence while preserving the conversation's governing pressure.

## Setup
Choose two characters with opposed or incompatible immediate wants. State the essential facts, relationship, intended scene change, and what each speaker knows or withholds. Keep those conditions invariant across all three drafts. Select a third channel such as live video, text messages, asynchronous correspondence, a recording, or a relayed exchange.

## Instructions
1. For each channel, inventory visual access, vocal access, touch and proximity, latency, persistence, privacy and possible witnesses, interruption, reachability, and any secondary activity one participant can conceal.
2. Draft the face-to-face version. Let posture, distance, gaze, movement, objects, and shared surroundings matter only where the viewpoint character can observe them and they alter the exchange.
3. Draft the audio-only version without compensating through explanatory dialogue. Use missing visual access, sound, silence, interruption, or concealed action to create one specific change in inference or conduct.
4. Draft the third version according to its actual conditions. Make latency, persistence, partial visibility, editability, replay, mediation, or audience change what can be attempted and understood.
5. Underline every moment that could occur only, or differently, because of its channel. Replace a merely decorative technology mention with a consequential constraint or remove it.
6. Compare the three endings. Record how the same wants and facts produced different misunderstanding, concealment, revelation, timing, relationship, or available action.
7. Select the version that best serves the larger story. Import no channel-specific beat from another version unless the selected channel can actually support it.

## Success Check
- Three complete versions exist, with the speakers' wants, essential facts, relationship, and intended scene change held constant.
- Every version identifies the cues it supplies, degrades, delays, preserves, or hides.
- Each channel causes at least one specific difference in inference, conduct, timing, or consequence.
- Physical action and nonverbal evidence remain observable through the selected viewpoint and channel.
- The chosen version can be defended by story effect rather than novelty or convenience.
- A near miss in which only formatting or device names change is identified and revised or rejected.

## Common Failures
- Adding narration that tells the remote participant what the missing cue would have shown.
- Treating face-to-face behavior as a fixed code whose meaning requires no context.
- Changing the characters' goals or facts so channel effects cannot be compared.
- Mentioning a device repeatedly without letting its limits affect the exchange.
- Giving text, recordings, or relayed speech the immediacy and reversibility of live conversation without explanation.
- Combining the most dramatic beats from incompatible channels into one impossible scene.

## Notes
Communication media distribute access unevenly. One participant may see an expression the other cannot, preserve words the speaker expected to vanish, revise a message before sending it, or perform an unrelated action without detection. The useful design question is not which channel seems modern or unusual, but which pattern of access and constraint creates the story's necessary pressure.
