# Make an Unmentionable Subject Legible through Subtext

## Practice Task
Draft a conversation about a plausible surface subject while both speakers respond to a consequential matter they cannot yet name, then introduce a witness and test whether concealment or disclosure creates the stronger turn.

## Target Skill
Build readable subtext from motive, risk, relationship, selection, evasion, silence, and action rather than from arbitrary obscurity.

## Setup
Choose two characters and define the hidden subject. State why each person cannot or will not name it, what each wants from the exchange, what each believes the other knows, and what would change if the subject became explicit. Choose a surface topic they have a credible reason to discuss.

## Instructions
1. List at least five forms of evidence the hidden subject could distort: word choice, topic changes, overprecise answers, unanswered questions, repeated phrases, pauses, gestures, object handling, or a refusal to leave.
2. Draft the conversation without naming the hidden subject. Keep the surface exchange coherent enough to justify every spoken turn while placing selected evidence that supports a specific reader inference.
3. Mark each clue and state what a reader can infer from it. Remove clues that merely signal vagueness and add footing where several unrelated explanations remain equally likely without purpose.
4. Introduce a third person whose presence changes permission, risk, allegiance, or the meaning of a prior line. Revise rather than merely adding the newcomer to the room.
5. Write two endings: one in which the subject remains unspoken but changes conduct, and one in which a credible shift in pressure permits or forces explicit disclosure.
6. Compare the endings for consequence, reader footing, character credibility, and residual pressure. Select the one that completes the scene's job without treating concealment as automatically superior.
7. Read the chosen version aloud. Revise lines whose coded quality sounds written for an audience rather than spoken by these people under these conditions.

## Success Check
- The hidden subject, each speaker's reason for avoiding it, and each speaker's immediate want are explicit in the setup.
- The surface topic remains a credible conversation rather than a transparent cipher.
- Readers receive enough converging evidence to infer the underlying pressure without an explanatory label.
- The third person changes permission, risk, allegiance, or interpretation.
- Both concealed and disclosed endings exist, and the retained one is chosen by consequence rather than a universal preference for omission.
- Removing the subtext would change action, relation, or understanding rather than merely simplify the prose.

## Common Failures
- Withholding the subject from readers by withholding every usable clue.
- Letting characters speak in cryptic abstractions no one would use with this listener.
- Choosing a symbolic surface object that has no natural place in the situation.
- Making both speakers share the same motive for silence and the same interpretation of every line.
- Adding a third person who changes nothing except cast size.
- Delaying disclosure after its social cause has disappeared or forcing it before any pressure earns it.

## Notes
Subtext is a relation between what the scene permits characters to say and what their behavior lets readers infer. A surface topic can carry displaced pressure when it belongs naturally to the setting and relationship; it fails when its only purpose is to hide authorial information. Omission and disclosure are alternate turns, not moral rankings. The stronger ending is the one that changes the story under credible social conditions.
