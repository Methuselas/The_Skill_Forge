# Stage and Audit a Multi-Speaker Dialogue Scene

## Practice Task
Design and draft a dialogue scene with at least four consequential participants, then audit its pressure network, floor control, attribution, and turn function until the group remains legible without equalizing every voice.

## Target Skill
Coordinate multiple speakers as an active network of wants, knowledge, alliances, interruptions, and changing options rather than as a queue of distinctive voices.

## Setup
Choose a meeting, meal, gathering, briefing, confrontation, or shared task that must change knowledge, power, commitment, relationship, or available action. Include at least four people capable of affecting that change, plus any ambient crowd presence the setting requires.

## Instructions
1. State the required scene change. For each active participant, record an immediate want, relevant knowledge, one relationship pressure, and a reason to speak, withhold, interrupt, redirect, or leave.
2. Separate active participants from ambient presence. Name and individually track people whose actions or interpretations can change the scene; render background attendees collectively or by role unless one becomes consequential.
3. Draw the pressure network among active participants. Mark alliances, rivalries, dependencies, unequal knowledge, who can challenge whom, and who possesses formal or informal control of the conversational floor.
4. Stage entrances, exits, sightlines, seating or standing positions, concurrent tasks, and objects that affect who can address, overhear, evade, or interrupt whom. Remove spatial information that changes no turn.
5. Draft the scene. Give every speaker a new paragraph, establish identity before ambiguity arises, and combine plain tags with consequential action beats. Differentiate voices through motive, attention, syntax, and relation without assigning each person a catchphrase.
6. Label every turn by function: claim, question, answer, evasion, alliance, challenge, disclosure, interruption, repair, decision, or changed option. Cut ambient banter and any turn whose removal changes neither orientation nor pressure.
7. Remove one participant at a time. Merge or delete anyone whose absence changes no knowledge route, relationship, decision, or available action; redistribute only facts the scene still needs.
8. Give the scene to a cold reader without a cast key. Ask the reader to identify each speaker, trace the dominant alliance or conflict, and state what changed. Repair attribution separately from voice or pressure defects.

## Success Check
- At least four active participants have distinct wants, knowledge boundaries, and relationships to the scene change.
- Individually tracked speakers are separated from ambient crowd presence by consequence rather than prestige or visual distinctiveness.
- Floor control, alliances, interruptions, and spatial access alter who can speak or what a turn can do.
- A cold reader can assign every line without relying on a catchphrase or demeaning physical label.
- Every retained participant changes knowledge, relation, pressure, decision, or available action.
- Turns are unequal in number when the scene's power and purpose require it; no quota substitutes for dramatic function.

## Common Failures
- Naming everyone present and creating a cast-memory problem before the exchange begins.
- Giving every participant equal speaking time regardless of power, motive, or consequence.
- Using quirks, accent, profanity, or repeated phrases as the primary attribution system.
- Adding an action beat to every line until movement becomes another tag tic.
- Preserving realistic cross-talk or banter that changes nothing.
- Letting all characters possess the same information or equal permission to interrupt.
- Identifying minor speakers by mocking body features, clothing, or demographic shorthand.
- Keeping a participant because the writer already invented a voice for them.

## Notes
Multi-speaker dialogue creates two simultaneous routing problems: readers must know who owns each turn, and the scene must show why that turn matters inside a network. Naming, paragraphing, tags, voice, movement, and spatial position solve attribution in combination. Wants, knowledge, alliances, and floor control create drama. Background presence can still exert pressure as witness, audience, institution, or crowd without requiring every person to become an individually tracked speaker.
