# Compress Real Speech into Purposeful Dialogue

## Pattern Rule
**IF** a fictional conversation reads like a transcript of routine talk or like polished prose divided among speakers
**THEN** select speech-like rhythms, fragments, interruptions, and revealing turns while cutting repetition and exchanges that do not change knowledge, character, relationship, or action

## Do
- Preserve contractions, unfinished sentences, strategic repetition, and offhand turns that distinguish spoken language from an essay.
- Make each retained exchange disclose a speaker, alter a relation, introduce necessary background, or move the story into a new condition.
- Shape sentence rhythms deliberately because dialogue exposes cadence more starkly than surrounding narration.
- Let one line perform several jobs when space is tight, such as revealing contempt while provoking the next decision.

## Don't
- Preserve every greeting, filler sound, repeated fact, or weather exchange merely because people really speak that way.
- Give every character complete grammatical sentences and the same formal register.
- Insert information both speakers already know in language designed only for the reader.
- Make efficiency so visible that every line sounds like a plot instruction.

## Checklist
- The exchange sounds speakable without preserving the clutter of a recording.
- Each retained passage changes at least one story condition or reader inference.
- The speakers do not share one polished authorial syntax.
- Repetition and interruption create pressure, rhythm, or characterization rather than dead space.
- Cutting any remaining routine exchange would damage orientation, relation, or movement.

## Notes
Fictional conversation offers the greatest hits of speech, not its complete outtakes. It creates spontaneity through selection and timing. Brief forms intensify the requirement, but even a story composed mostly of conversation needs every exchange to contribute beyond the fact that people are talking.
