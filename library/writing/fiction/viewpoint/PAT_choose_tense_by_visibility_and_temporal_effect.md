# Choose Tense by Visibility and Temporal Effect

## Pattern Rule
**IF** a fiction draft inherits a tense by habit rather than by the temporal relation it should create
**THEN** select past, present, or future according to how visible the tense should be and whether the action should feel remembered, unfolding, or projected

## Do
- Test the opening and a consequential turn in more than one tense before deciding which temporal stance governs the story.
- Consider how the tense handles summary, backstory, anticipation, and knowledge of later consequences, not only scene-level energy.
- Let an unusual tense work with the narrator's mode of address, such as a future sequence delivered as instruction or warning.
- Stabilize the governing tense once its effect has been chosen, marking time changes clearly.

## Don't
- Call past tense detached or present tense vivid without testing the actual prose.
- Use present tense while casually granting the narrator hindsight unavailable in the unfolding moment.
- Choose future tense only to make an otherwise conventional account look experimental.
- Shift tense during high action because urgency increased unless the temporal relation truly changed.

## Checklist
- The tense establishes a coherent relation between event and telling.
- Its degree of conspicuousness supports rather than competes with the story.
- The narrator possesses only the hindsight or foresight the chosen stance permits.
- Backstory and anticipation remain temporally legible.
- An alternate tense loses a named effect rather than simply sounding unfamiliar.

## Notes
`writing_fiction_variant_use_past_tense_as_unmarked_narration` treats past as a common, low-visibility choice rather than as a superior one. Its familiarity frees attention for other effects and facilitates retrospective movement.

`writing_fiction_variant_use_present_tense_for_conspicuous_immediacy` makes ongoing action and unsettled consequence more noticeable, an economy that can suit flash fiction. That visibility becomes a liability when every moment receives the same forced intensity.

`writing_fiction_variant_use_future_tense_for_experimental_projection` turns narration into an assertion about what will happen. It is strongest when prophecy, planning, coercion, rehearsal, or inevitability is the action, and weakest when the tense has no speakerly reason.
