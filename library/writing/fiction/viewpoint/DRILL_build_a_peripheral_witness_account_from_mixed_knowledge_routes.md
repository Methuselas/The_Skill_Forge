# Build a Peripheral Witness Account from Mixed Knowledge Routes

## Practice Task
Draft a first-person account centered on another character while making every witnessed, transmitted, inferred, reconstructed, and unknown element legible through the narrator's situated knowledge.

## Target Skill
Use a peripheral witness to preserve another character's mystery without granting the narrator objective authority or disguising invented access as fact within the telling.

## Setup
Choose one consequential event and a first-person narrator who directly experiences only part of it. Give the narrator a meaningful relationship to the central character, one later informant or record, and one gap that no available route can settle.

## Instructions
1. State what the narrator wants from the central character, what the narrator risks by telling, and why this account is being given now.
2. Divide the event into claims. Mark each as directly witnessed, remembered, reported by a named person, found in a record, inferred from consequences, openly reconstructed, disputed, or unknown.
3. For every transmitted claim, trace who first had access, how it reached the narrator, what each person wanted, and where selection, delay, misunderstanding, or self-protection could alter it.
4. Identify which inaccessible material should remain absent because that opacity makes the central character more consequential. Reject any detail included only to make the scene feel complete.
5. Draft 300 to 500 words. Establish the knowledge route before or as each consequential offstage claim appears, using the narrator's natural syntax rather than inserting an evidence label.
6. Include one bounded reconstruction only if it performs necessary narrative work. Signal its status before exact sensory detail or dialogue could be mistaken for memory or testimony.
7. Underline every claim the narrator did not witness. Revise or remove any whose route, certainty, and narrative function cannot be defended.
8. Give the draft to a reader without the private claim map. Ask which material was witnessed, learned later, inferred, reconstructed, or left unresolved; repair any accidental confusion while preserving deliberate uncertainty.

## Success Check
- Every consequential claim appears on the private map with a specific knowledge route rather than the catch-all label reported.
- At least one vivid but unsupported detail is rejected or revised; a map that approves everything demonstrates no access control.
- The transmission chain identifies access, motive, and one plausible distortion point for each later account or record.
- The draft signals reconstruction before readers could mistake invented dialogue or sensation for witnessed or transmitted material.
- The narrator's relationship and present motive visibly shape selection and interpretation, so peripheral position is not mistaken for neutrality.
- A reader can distinguish witnessed, acquired, inferred, reconstructed, and unresolved material without seeing the claim map, and any failed attribution is named.
- The central character remains partly opaque for a stated narrative effect rather than because the draft forgot to establish a route.

## Common Failures
- Treating hindsight as if elapsed time itself supplied knowledge.
- Calling the narrator an outsider while using that person's relationship only as a camera position.
- Presenting a later informant's version as what really happened without testing the informant's access or stake.
- Reconstructing exact dialogue and sensation while offering only a vague signal such as must have.
- Making every link uncertain until readers cannot tell which event the story asks them to believe occurred.
- Declaring the narrator unreliable merely because testimony was incomplete or distorted instead of building readable counterevidence around a consequential claim.

## Notes
A peripheral witness creates useful double pressure: the narrator can interpret a central figure while never fully possessing that person's interior life. The narrator is still a participant in the story world, however, and relationship creates bias as well as access. Later testimony, records, and consequences can widen the account, but hindsight only organizes what the narrator plausibly learned.

Fiction may render a reconstruction vividly, yet the telling situation still establishes what its narrator claims to know. A brief phrase, modal construction, attribution, interruption, or visible shift into speculation can preserve that contract without turning prose into an evidence report. Ordinary transmission error creates partial knowledge; designed unreliability begins only when readers receive sufficient friction to reassess the narrator's consequential claims.
