# Turn a Theme Map into Dramatic Choices and Consequences

## Practice Task
Map one working theme into competing human positions, generate situations that test those positions, and draft one consequential choice without stating the intended lesson.

## Target Skill
Use theme as a generative question or pressure field that produces dramatic action instead of as an abstract label imposed on a story.

## Setup
Choose a broad thematic interest that matters to the project, such as loyalty, freedom, inheritance, ambition, belonging, justice, or forgiveness. Treat it as a hypothesis to test rather than a message the story must prove.

## Instructions
1. Put the thematic interest at the center of a page. Generate associated beliefs, images, memories, institutions, fears, desires, contradictions, and costs without evaluating them during the generative interval.
2. Group the material into at least two positions that can conflict without making either position merely foolish or evil. Write what each position protects and what it risks.
3. Assign the positions to two or more characters whose circumstances give them different reasons to hold them. Add the emotion, loyalty, need, or prior consequence that makes each position actionable.
4. Generate three concrete situations in which the characters cannot preserve every value at once. For each situation, name the choice available and what each option would cost.
5. Extend each possible choice by one causal step: write the action it produces, the person or condition it affects, and the new option or pressure created by that consequence.
6. Select the situation that produces the strongest combination of character-specific motive, difficult choice, and irreversible or accumulating consequence.
7. Draft the decisive exchange or action without naming the theme or explaining which position is correct. Let behavior, material detail, speech, silence, and outcome carry the pressure.
8. Hide the original theme label and inspect the draft. Revise if the scene presents only an argument, if either position lacks a credible stake, or if the choice changes nothing.

## Success Check
- The map contains conflicting positions, costs, and concrete situations rather than a cluster of synonyms for the theme.
- Each principal character has a specific reason to protect a different value or interpretation.
- The selected situation forces a choice that cannot preserve every important value.
- The drafted action produces a material, relational, emotional, or causal consequence.
- With the theme label hidden, a reader can identify the value conflict from enacted evidence while still seeing room for more than one interpretation.
- A scene that merely states opposing opinions does not pass unless conduct or consequence changes the situation.

## Common Failures
- Starting from a moral conclusion and designing one character only to prove it.
- Generating abstract associations without converting any of them into people, conditions, or actions.
- Giving one side all the evidence, virtue, intelligence, or narrative authority.
- Calling a conversation dramatic when nobody must decide, risk, lose, or alter anything.
- Explaining the intended meaning after the scene instead of strengthening its enacted evidence.

## Notes
Theme is most useful during invention when it opens related pressures rather than closes interpretation. A spatial map can expose unexpected relations among values, emotions, events, and images, but the exercise is incomplete until those relations become choices with consequences. The resulting scene tests the thematic hypothesis; it does not certify it.
