# Match Story Scope to Required Accumulation

## Pattern Rule
**IF** a fiction premise depends on extensive worldbuilding, insider detail, prolonged suspense, repeated obstacles, or a delayed climax
**THEN** give the narrative enough length for that pressure to accumulate or choose a smaller story whose central effect can mature inside the available form
**ELSE** test whether compression would strengthen the premise by removing development it does not need

## Do
- List what readers must learn, anticipate, or experience before the intended climax can carry its full weight.
- Distinguish necessary buildup from explanatory history that can remain outside the narrated time.
- Estimate scope from the number and duration of required turns and from the intended treatment: scenic enactment, summary, descriptive density, viewpoint access, and formal compression all change how much narrative space the material needs.
- When working short, enter near the consequential event and select a premise whose world can become credible through a few exact details.
- Expand the form when cutting would make the setting thin, the suspense instantaneous, or the final union, loss, or revelation unearned.
- Allow a long chronology to remain brief when parable, tale, montage, or selective summary carries its effect, and allow a short elapsed time to expand when close perception and detailed action are the point.

## Don't
- Force a convention built on gradual anticipation into a space that permits only summary.
- Substitute compressed exposition for the lived sequence of obstacles that creates the story's effect.
- Keep a large premise in a tiny form merely because short work appears easier to finish.
- Enlarge a compact story with backstory and complications that do not alter its central movement.

## Checklist
- Every prerequisite for the main effect has enough narrative time to register.
- The chosen length supports the required world, anticipation, and complication without padding.
- The treatment and length ask readers to inhabit compatible amounts of detail, scene, summary, and elapsed time.
- Backstory included in the draft changes the reader's understanding of the present action.
- A shorter version would not hollow out the premise, and a longer version would not merely repeat its pressure.
- The form and the story promise ask for compatible kinds of attention.

## Notes
Length is a craft resource, not a prestige level or a direct measure of elapsed story time. Some effects require accretion: a convincing invented world needs texture, suspense needs duration, and a delayed relationship climax needs obstacles whose apparent force can grow. Other premises gain energy when the writer removes history and enters close to a decisive moment. A parable can cross years in a page, while sustained attention can expand one day into a long work. Scope is well matched when subject, treatment, and form supply enough narrative space for the effect and no more.
