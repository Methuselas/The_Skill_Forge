# Transform Documentary Material into a Fictional Artifact

## Practice Task
Analyze a diary, letter, journal, testimony, or comparable document that you may responsibly use, then create a new fictional artifact and embed its discovery in a scene where it changes conduct or interpretation.

## Target Skill
Transfer researched voice principles and factual anchors into an original story document without copying distinctive expression, confusing evidence with invention, or reducing the artifact to an information dump.

## Setup
Choose a document you may lawfully and ethically study. Prepare three blank sections labeled **Evidence**, **Transformation**, and **Invention**. Identify the intended fiction's truth contract before drafting.

## Instructions
1. Record the source document's maker, intended recipient or imagined audience, time, occasion, purpose, factual anchors, route of preservation, and limits as evidence. Mark uncertainty instead of completing it by guesswork.
2. Analyze transferable voice features without collecting phrases to reuse: register, sentence shape, rhythm, habitual selection, assumptions, evasions, and omissions.
3. Write a 100- to 200-word style study about wholly new content. Reproduce only the abstract voice features you identified, then compare the result with the source and replace any borrowed distinctive phrase or unusually close sequence.
4. Declare the fiction's truth contract. In the three prepared sections, separate facts you will retain from features you will transform and material you will invent. Give a reason for every retained factual anchor and every consequential invention.
5. Design the fictional artifact's maker, recipient, occasion, motive, knowledge, and plausible route into the story. Make at least two of these conditions exert visible pressure on what the maker says, avoids, or misunderstands.
6. Draft the artifact with a local movement: a question, decision, confession, contradiction, discovery, or changed understanding. End after that movement rather than after all available background has been delivered.
7. Write the scene in which another character discovers or receives the artifact. Render enough of it to let its exact voice matter, and make the reader-character's interpretation alter an action, relationship, risk, or next question.
8. Audit the complete exercise for phrase borrowing, historical and material accuracy, evidentiary limits, invented claims, and accidental contradiction between the artifact's voice and its telling situation. Revise every unsupported or decorative choice.

## Success Check
- The style study, fictional artifact, and discovery scene all exist as separate, complete outputs.
- The fictional voice transfers observable principles of register, syntax, rhythm, selection, assumption, or omission without copying distinctive expression.
- Retained facts and consequential inventions are separated, and each has a stated reason tied to credibility or story function.
- The artifact contains local narrative movement and the discovery scene changes conduct, relationship, risk, knowledge, or interpretation.
- Period, position, medium, and circulation are materially credible rather than signaled only by decorative archaism.
- A near-miss version that merely imitates surface style or unloads background can be identified and rejected by specific comparison.

## Common Failures
- Copying memorable phrases while calling the result a study of style.
- Treating an incomplete or biased document as comprehensive historical evidence.
- Silently converting uncertainty into fact or presenting invention under a truth-bound contract.
- Giving the artifact an antique surface but no maker, recipient, motive, or local movement.
- Letting the discovery confirm existing knowledge without changing any choice or interpretation.
- Explaining all of the artifact after the scene has already made its consequence inferable.

## Notes
The exercise separates observation from appropriation and research from invention. Documentary material can supply factual anchors, period assumptions, and models of linguistic behavior, but fiction still needs an original artifact whose telling situation and consequence belong to the designed story. Retaining a fact or inventing a detail is not automatically virtuous; the declared contract and the reason for the choice determine whether it is responsible and useful.
