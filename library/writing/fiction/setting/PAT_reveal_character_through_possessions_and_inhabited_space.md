# Reveal Character through Possessions and Inhabited Space

## Pattern Rule
**IF** a character's room, workplace, clothing, belongings, or material traces can carry characterization before or instead of direct explanation
**THEN** select objects, presentation choices, and arrangements whose use, condition, access, concealment, or personal meaning reveals the character's priorities, history, constraints, contradictions, and possible story pressure
**ELSE** omit the property inventory when the setting changes no consequential inference about its occupant

## Do
- Distinguish what the character chose from what was inherited, imposed, borrowed, inaccessible, neglected, repaired, or kept after its ordinary use ended; account for money, body, culture, institution, and occasion when they limit choice.
- Connect a possession or garment to conduct or history: who uses it, who provided or requires it, what sacrifice acquired it, why it is protected, and what its use, loss, recovery, exposure, or disposal would change.
- Let an absent character become imaginable through spatial habits such as cleared surfaces, blocked doors, worn paths, hidden containers, or objects preserved in the wrong room.
- Test cherished items for story pressure by asking whether one contains a secret, belongs to someone else, contradicts the public persona, or can force a later choice.
- Use a brand or label only when its period, group, status, practical, or personal meaning performs work that a generic description cannot; decide whether that specificity should date the story.

## Don't
- Treat expensive objects as proof of vanity, disorder as proof of moral failure, sparse rooms as proof of emotional emptiness, or clothing and food as self-interpreting identity evidence without further context.
- List decor and possessions after the reader has already formed the necessary inference.
- Explain the occupant's psychology immediately after the selected objects have made it available.
- Give every object a symbolic burden; ordinary use and practical constraint can characterize without becoming code.

## Checklist
- Each retained object, garment, or arrangement reveals a specific priority, relation, habit, history, constraint, or contradiction.
- At least one detail depends on the occupant's personal meaning rather than its generic cultural association.
- The space feels inhabited by this character rather than furnished for a demographic category.
- Any proposed plot seed follows from ownership, access, requirement, concealment, damage, memory, use, loss, recovery, or disposal instead of arbitrary coincidence.
- Removing the descriptive passage would erase character evidence not supplied more efficiently elsewhere.

## Notes
Possessions become richest when the story exposes what they mean to their owner or user. A grand chair may reveal less through its price or appearance than through the years of self-denial it was meant to repay. Clothing likewise characterizes through the relation among presentation, occasion, access, obligation, comfort, memory, and an observer's interpretation—not through a garment carrying a fixed meaning. A room or belonging can therefore perform two jobs at once: make a person present even in absence and expose material history that can become action.

The variant `writing_fiction_variant_test_character_presentation_across_social_contexts` compares presentation under unlike demands. Inventory private, occupational, formal, and relational options only long enough to identify consequential contrasts; then draft the acts of selecting, wearing, adjusting, hiding, borrowing, refusing, or being denied an item. Preserve constraints that make the choice partial, and let another character's reading remain attributable rather than objectively true.
