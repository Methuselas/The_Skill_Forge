# Reveal Character through Possessions and Inhabited Space

## Pattern Rule
**IF** a character's room, workplace, belongings, or traces can carry characterization before or instead of direct explanation
**THEN** select objects and arrangements whose use, condition, concealment, or personal meaning reveals the character's priorities, history, contradictions, and possible story pressure
**ELSE** omit the property inventory when the setting changes no consequential inference about its occupant

## Do
- Distinguish what the character chose from what was inherited, imposed, borrowed, neglected, repaired, or kept after its ordinary use ended.
- Connect a possession to conduct or history: who uses it, what sacrifice acquired it, why it is protected, and what its loss or discovery would change.
- Let an absent character become imaginable through spatial habits such as cleared surfaces, blocked doors, worn paths, hidden containers, or objects preserved in the wrong room.
- Test cherished items for story pressure by asking whether one contains a secret, belongs to someone else, contradicts the public persona, or can force a later choice.

## Don't
- Treat expensive objects as proof of vanity, disorder as proof of moral failure, or sparse rooms as proof of emotional emptiness without further evidence.
- List decor and possessions after the reader has already formed the necessary inference.
- Explain the occupant's psychology immediately after the selected objects have made it available.
- Give every object a symbolic burden; ordinary use and practical constraint can characterize without becoming code.

## Checklist
- Each retained object or arrangement reveals a specific priority, relation, habit, history, or contradiction.
- At least one detail depends on the occupant's personal meaning rather than its generic cultural association.
- The space feels inhabited by this character rather than furnished for a demographic category.
- Any proposed plot seed follows from ownership, concealment, damage, memory, or use instead of arbitrary coincidence.
- Removing the descriptive passage would erase character evidence not supplied more efficiently elsewhere.

## Notes
Possessions become richest when the story exposes what they mean to their owner. A grand chair may reveal less through its price or appearance than through the years of self-denial it was meant to repay. The room can therefore perform two jobs at once: make a person present even in absence and expose an object whose history can become action.
