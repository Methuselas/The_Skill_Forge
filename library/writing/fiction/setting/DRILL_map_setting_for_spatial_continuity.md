# Map Setting for Spatial Continuity

## Practice Task
Sketch a story's main location, trace every character movement through it, and repair contradictions before deciding how little of the map readers need.

## Target Skill
Maintain credible spatial relations and travel time while keeping private setting knowledge larger than explicit description.

## Setup
Choose a fiction draft that uses one or two recurring locations. Prepare a blank page and the scenes in chronological order.

## Instructions
1. Draw the outer boundaries, entrances, exits, fixed furniture or landmarks, and important sightlines.
2. Mark approximate distances and the ordinary time required to move between consequential points.
3. Trace each scene's route in a different color or line style, noting where characters enter, stop, observe, and leave.
4. Compare repeated uses of doors, windows, roads, rooms, terrain, and travel time; circle contradictions.
5. Revise the map or the draft according to the version that better supports character and conflict.
6. Mark which few spatial facts readers require for orientation and leave the rest as private continuity knowledge.

## Success Check
- Boundaries, entrances, exits, fixed features, and sightlines are drawn before any route is traced.
- Distances and ordinary travel times are recorded, since contradictions surface in duration more often than in geography.
- Every scene's route is traced with entries, stops, observations, and exits marked, including scenes assumed to be simple.
- Contradictions are circled rather than resolved in passing, and at least one is found. A map producing none was drawn from the draft rather than tested against it.
- Each contradiction is repaired in whichever of map or draft better serves character and event, with the choice recorded.
- How much a reader needs is decided last, so the full map exists to be withheld rather than never built.

## Common Failures
- Drawing attractive scenery without mapping character routes.
- Changing room dimensions or door positions to solve each scene locally.
- Treating all locations as equally important to orient.
- Copying the complete floor plan into prose.
- Ignoring travel time between separately mapped places.

## Notes
The map is a diagnostic instrument, not a deliverable the story must describe. Its value is the confidence it gives omission: the writer can reveal only the active edge of a place while movements remain anchored in a coherent whole.
