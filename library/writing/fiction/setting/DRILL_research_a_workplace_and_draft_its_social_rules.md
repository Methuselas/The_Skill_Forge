# Research a Workplace and Draft Its Social Rules

## Practice Task
Learn how an unfamiliar workplace materially and socially operates, then draft a newcomer's first encounter with its unwritten order.

## Target Skill
Convert selected workplace evidence into fictional hierarchy, belonging, and consequence without dumping research.

## Setup
Choose a workplace you do not know from the inside. Arrange a short conversation with a consenting knowledgeable person, or use reliable documentary and observational evidence when an interview is unavailable.

## Instructions
1. Prepare questions about layout, equipment, recurring sounds and smells, specialist names, daily rituals, status markers, common mistakes, rewards, sanctions, and rules newcomers usually learn late.
2. Gather the answers responsively, following up on details that reveal how the work or hierarchy actually operates.
3. Sort the findings into material system, formal rule, unwritten rule, privilege, sanction, and newcomer hazard.
4. Invent a new employee with one capability that helps and one assumption that the workplace will punish.
5. Draft up to 1,000 words of the first day. Let routes, objects, corrections, permissions, and observed conduct reveal the order before anyone explains it fully.
6. Make one misunderstood or successfully inferred rule change the newcomer's standing or next available action.
7. Remove specialist details that establish neither work, atmosphere, hierarchy, character, nor consequence.
8. Ask the knowledgeable person, when available, whether the retained details are plausible without asking them to approve the fiction's invented events.

## Success Check
- Research identifies both material operations and social rules.
- The scene distinguishes formal authority from informal influence.
- The newcomer's status changes what can be seen, understood, or attempted.
- At least one setting detail creates a constraint or opportunity that alters action.
- Specialist information is selective and plausible rather than displayed for its own sake.
- The invented consequence follows from the researched system without claiming that it actually happened.

## Common Failures
- Asking only what the workplace looks like and missing how belonging is regulated.
- Turning the scene into an orientation lecture.
- Filling dialogue with jargon that no character needs in the moment.
- Treating one person's account as proof of every workplace in the field.
- Keeping every researched detail because gathering it required effort.

## Notes
The drill uses research to create pressure rather than scenery. A badge, route, machine, or phrase becomes useful when access to it marks status or a mistake around it has consequences. The factual workplace supplies operating conditions; the fictional character and event remain inventions.
