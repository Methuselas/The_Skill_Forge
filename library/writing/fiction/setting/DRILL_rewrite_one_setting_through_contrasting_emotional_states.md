# Rewrite One Setting through Contrasting Emotional States

## Practice Task
Describe the same place twice through one character under sharply different emotional pressures while keeping its physical facts consistent.

## Target Skill
Use selection, attention, naming, and interpretation to make emotion alter a setting without falsifying its geography.

## Setup
Choose a place with a recognizable atmosphere and list six fixed facts that must remain true in both versions. Give one character two opposed immediate pressures, such as feared illness and hopeful ownership.

## Instructions
1. Write a passage of up to 250 words as the character enters under the first pressure.
2. Rewrite the visit under the second pressure without changing the six fixed facts.
3. Mark which details recur, which become newly visible, and which receive a different interpretation.
4. Check the relation between the place's conventional atmosphere and the character's state. Allow support, resistance, guilt, relief, alienation, or another mixed response instead of forcing agreement.
5. Remove direct emotion labels and any altered fact used only to make the second version easier.
6. Underline the three choices that most strongly changed the reader's experience, then state whether each worked through selection, language, sequence, or association.
7. Draft a third paragraph that shifts from one pressure toward the other while the character remains in place.

## Success Check
- Both versions describe recognizably the same physical location.
- The character's pressure changes attention and interpretation rather than merely adding emotion words.
- At least one detail resists the character's mood and creates a mixed response.
- The transition paragraph changes the place's felt meaning without replacing the setting.
- The strongest differences can be traced to deliberate craft choices.

## Common Failures
- Replacing the place with different weather, furniture, or geography in each version.
- Tinting every detail to match one simple mood.
- Naming anxiety, joy, grief, or hope instead of embodying its attentional effects.
- Changing only adjectives while preserving identical selection and sequence.
- Treating an inherently sad or cheerful place as incapable of emotional opposition.

## Notes
The comparison makes perceptual filtering observable. A graveyard beside a house can become threat, perspective, guilt, or relief without moving a stone. The third pass adds the harder task: letting a setting register an emotional turn rather than merely illustrating a stable state.
