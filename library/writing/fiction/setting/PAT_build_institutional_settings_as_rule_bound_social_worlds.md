# Build Institutional Settings as Rule-Bound Social Worlds

## Pattern Rule
**IF** a story enters a workplace, school, hospital, prison, military unit, or other institution whose internal order affects what characters can do
**THEN** establish the place's material systems, jargon, rituals, hierarchy, rewards, sanctions, and membership rules through the character's position inside them
**ELSE** keep institutional machinery light when it creates no pressure beyond ordinary location

## Do
- Identify how newcomers, established members, gatekeepers, dissenters, and dependants receive different access, information, protection, and scrutiny.
- Attach rules to material evidence such as badges, routes, equipment, uniforms, locked areas, schedules, forms, or privileged rooms.
- Learn the local names and routines that an insider would use, then select only those needed to make power and work legible in the scene.
- Let acceptance, confusion, competence, resistance, or rule-breaking change the character's available choices and standing.

## Don't
- Substitute generic corridors, desks, uniforms, or technical vocabulary for an operating social system.
- Explain a hierarchy in a detached briefing when a route, interruption, correction, or denied request can reveal it in action.
- Display every researched procedure after the few consequential rules are established.
- Treat all members as equally constrained when rank, experience, role, or informal influence changes how the place works for them.

## Checklist
- The institution has at least one recognizable rule, ritual, reward, and sanction relevant to the story.
- Physical organization and social hierarchy reinforce or productively contradict one another.
- The viewpoint character's insider or outsider status changes what is noticed and understood.
- A breach, misunderstanding, or successful use of the system can generate consequence.
- Removing the institutional setting would require changing the character's options, relations, or conflict.

## Notes
Institutions behave like enclosed worlds because they regulate language, movement, belonging, and consequence. Their fictional value is not the amount of specialist information they contain but the pressure created when a character must learn, exploit, endure, or resist their operating order.
