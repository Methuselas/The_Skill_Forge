# Control Setting Detail Density by Story Need

## Pattern Rule
**IF** a fiction draft leaves scenes blank and blurry or slows them with accumulated place description
**THEN** add only the orientation and pressure-bearing details the particular story needs, and cut descriptions that neither develop character nor move action

## Do
- Name a real location when its specificity supplies useful associations, authority, or orientation; use a generic label when exact naming would demand irrelevant explanation.
- Expand description where the reader cannot picture an action, understand a constraint, or feel a consequential atmosphere.
- Compress place into incidental details when a full scenic pause would interrupt the governing movement.
- Test a cherished passage by removing it and checking whether character, conflict, orientation, or consequence changes.

## Don't
- Equate abundant description with a convincing world.
- Leave physical action ungrounded because the writer privately knows where everything is.
- Preserve a page of scenery chiefly because writing it revived a pleasant memory.
- Apply one fixed quantity of description to stories with unlike purposes and lengths.

## Checklist
- Readers know enough about where and when to follow consequential action.
- Every extended description changes atmosphere, character, conflict, or understanding.
- Named places earn the expectations and associations they introduce.
- No section becomes a geography lesson detached from the story's people.
- Deleting a retained detail would remove a specific function, not merely reduce decoration.

## Notes
Setting can range from richly rendered to nearly implicit. The useful measure is integrity, not volume. A story that depends on landscape may need sustained description; another can let readers assume a contemporary home from a few dropped particulars. Short fiction makes decorative excess more visible because every descriptive pause competes directly with action.
