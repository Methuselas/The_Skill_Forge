# Preload Writing Workshops with Asynchronous Annotation

## Pattern Rule
**IF** live workshop time should diagnose recurring craft decisions rather than collect everyone's first reaction
**THEN** have peers annotate the shared draft before meeting, read and extend one another's comments, then build the live agenda from the aggregated feedback
**ELSE** reserve a silent annotation period at the start of the meeting and collect the notes before discussion begins

## Do
- Keep the draft and all preliminary feedback in one shared location so the writer and facilitator can see comments together rather than reconstructing them from separate messages.
- Ask readers to anchor each note to an exact passage and name the craft effect, question, or point of confusion it creates.
- Require readers to scan existing annotations before adding their own; they can reinforce a pattern, add a contrasting reading, or move to an uncovered passage.
- Before the live session, identify repeated observations, disagreements, and decisions that need conversation; use those clusters to choose the workshop's targets.

## Don't
- Don't spend the live meeting reading the draft for the first time when an earlier annotation pass is feasible.
- Don't equate the platform with the method. Highlights, margin notes, comments, or a collected worksheet can all support the same sequence.
- Don't let duplicated comments create the illusion of breadth; consolidate repeated reactions and use meeting time on consequences and revision options.

## Checklist
- Did every participant read the complete draft before the meeting?
- Is each comment tied to a passage rather than offered as a floating verdict?
- Can the facilitator name the two or three strongest feedback clusters before discussion starts?
- Does the live agenda move beyond comment collection into interpretation and revision choices?

## Notes
The source presents this sequence through a discontinued vendor course space, but the screenshots and accompanying prose make the durable distinction clear: preliminary online critique centralizes the class's feedback and lets the in-person conversation begin at a deeper, more targeted level. The useful unit is asynchronous annotation followed by synchronous synthesis; no particular product feature owns it.
