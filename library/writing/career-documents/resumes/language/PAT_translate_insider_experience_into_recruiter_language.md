# Translate Insider Experience into Recruiter Language

## Pattern Rule
**IF** a resume describes work through acronyms, ranks, internal labels, military terminology, passive constructions, or other language an external recruiter may not share
**THEN** translate the experience into plain, active, specific occupational language while preserving recognized terms that aid accurate matching

## Do
- Spell out a private or ambiguous acronym on first use and keep the abbreviation only when it remains useful afterward.
- Convert a rank, unit, division, or internal process into the function, responsibility, and scope an outside reader needs to understand.
- Put the candidate in the grammatical subject position when the candidate performed the action.
- Replace a general positive claim with the exact task, result, qualification, or tool that supports it.
- Retain established industry terminology when it is the shared language of the target occupation rather than one organization's shorthand.

## Don't
- Assume a recruiter can decode the previous employer's organizational chart or vocabulary.
- Remove recognized occupational terms in pursuit of plainness until the experience becomes difficult to search or classify.
- Use active voice to overstate ownership of work the candidate only assisted or observed.
- Substitute aggressive, boastful, or fluffy language for evidence.

## Checklist
- Every acronym and internal label can be understood without access to the former workplace.
- Military or organization-specific experience is stated as a transferable function without erasing its true setting.
- Sentence subjects and verbs accurately identify who performed each action.
- Searchable role language survives the translation where the candidate can support it.
- Praise words have been replaced by specific work or results.

## Notes
Recruiters need two vocabularies at once: plain language that exposes what happened and shared occupational terms that make the match retrievable. Internal shorthand supplies neither when the reader lacks the organization-specific key. Translation should reveal function and scope, not strip away accurate domain language or inflate participation into leadership.
