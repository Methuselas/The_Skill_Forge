# Use Recognizable Job Language for Resume Discovery

## Pattern Rule
**IF** a resume will be searched, filtered, or rapidly matched against a vacancy
**THEN** express truthful experience with the specific, commonly recognized job terms that human and software reviewers are likely to seek
**ELSE** prefer clear conventional role language over private shorthand or clever titles

## Do
- Extract candidate terms from the advertisement, position description, and selection criteria before drafting the relevant sections.
- Cover the searchable categories the role actually uses: job titles, skills, technologies, qualifications, certifications, licences, and languages.
- Replace broad claims such as software proficiency with the names of tools the candidate can genuinely use.
- Translate an unusual internal title into its commonly understood counterpart while retaining the original title where accuracy requires it.
- Check sector usage in several current job descriptions or with practitioners when entering an unfamiliar field.

## Don't
- Add a term solely to increase match frequency when the candidate cannot support it.
- Favor a witty or fashionable title that hides the occupation a recruiter is trying to find.
- Use a dense keyword block that disconnects terms from the experience or qualifications that substantiate them.
- Treat a static industry keyword list as universal, exhaustive, or more authoritative than the vocabulary of the actual vacancy.
- Assume one employer's internal vocabulary is the language used across the whole field.

## Checklist
- The vacancy's central terms appear where the candidate has matching evidence.
- General capability labels have been replaced with relevant named skills, tools, or credentials.
- Unusual titles are paired with a recognizable occupational equivalent.
- Every important term remains defensible in an interview or reference check.
- A reviewer can locate the main match without decoding organization-specific wording.

## Notes
Online databases and busy recruiters both reduce a candidate to retrievable criteria before they encounter the fuller career story. Specific nouns and short phrases make genuine qualifications findable. There is no permanent universal list: search terms vary with the vacancy, recruiter, occupation, and current market usage. The move is translation, not stuffing; the document should use the market's shared vocabulary without inventing experience or surrendering readable prose.
