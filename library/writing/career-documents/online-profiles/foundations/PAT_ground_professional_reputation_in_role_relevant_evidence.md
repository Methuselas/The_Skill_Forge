# Ground a Professional Reputation in Role-Relevant Evidence

## Pattern Rule
**IF** a career profile must communicate what the candidate is known for
**THEN** choose role-relevant qualities that are supported by qualifications, work results, recurring conduct, and informed outside observation
**ELSE** describe established capabilities without manufacturing a personal-brand claim

## Do
- Start with a target role or role family, then separate candidate qualities into personal attributes, professional attributes, and training or credentials.
- Attach each proposed quality to behavior another person could have observed, such as meeting commitments, organizing difficult work, helping customers, or delivering accurate results.
- Ask colleagues, peers, or former managers for both favorable and corrective descriptions, then compare their language with the candidate's self-assessment.
- Turn a broad trait into a contribution by connecting it to a work setting and outcome rather than publishing the adjective alone.
- Treat an important target quality that lacks evidence as a development goal, not as profile copy.

## Don't
- Choose flattering labels only because they are common in vacancy descriptions.
- Let an aspirational image contradict repeated online posts, workplace conduct, or the evidence displayed in the profile.
- Discard less favorable feedback before checking whether several credible observers noticed the same gap.
- Present a qualification as proof of a personal trait it does not demonstrate.
- Build the reputation around qualities unrelated to the work the candidate wants to be found for.

## Checklist
- Every stated quality matters to the target work or professional community.
- Each quality has at least one defensible source in conduct, evidence, credentials, or informed feedback.
- Self-description and outside observation have been compared rather than assumed to agree.
- Unsupported target qualities are marked for development or omitted.
- The profile's words and the candidate's visible actions reinforce the same reputation.

## Notes
Professional reputation is the pattern other people infer from conduct and evidence, not a slogan applied to a profile. Self-assessment identifies the intended signal; outside observation tests whether that signal is already legible. The gap between them is useful only when it changes behavior or development, not when it is hidden beneath stronger adjectives.
