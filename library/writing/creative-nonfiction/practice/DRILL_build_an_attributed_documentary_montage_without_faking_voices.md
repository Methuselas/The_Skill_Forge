# Build an Attributed Documentary Montage without Faking Voices

## Practice Task
Arrange several trustworthy documentary fragments around one bounded question, using explicit attribution and authorial bridges to create movement without inventing testimony or smoothing disagreement into certainty.

## Target Skill
Make documents and voices interact dramatically while keeping quotation, paraphrase, contemporaneous record, retrospective testimony, reconstruction, interpretation, and unknown material distinguishable.

## Setup
Choose a bounded biographical, autobiographical, or historical question supported by three to five materials you may responsibly use. Include at least two different source positions or document types. Preserve complete working copies and record each item's maker, date or temporal relation to the event, purpose, route of access, and relevant limitations.

## Instructions
1. Classify every candidate fragment as exact quotation, faithful paraphrase, contemporaneous record, retrospective account, reported speech, authorial interpretation, reconstruction, or unresolved material.
2. Map each contributor's access, stake, intended audience, elapsed time, editorial mediation, and exposure to the event's consequences. Do not turn the map into a numerical credibility score.
3. Select only fragments that establish, answer, contradict, or complicate the active question. Exclude a vivid voice that adds atmosphere but no necessary evidence or pressure.
4. Draft a short montage using speaker labels, document labels, typographic separation, or narrated attribution appropriate to the form. Keep the source class recoverable wherever readers might mistake mediation for direct speech.
5. Write brief authorial bridges that orient time, place, relation, and evidentiary limits. Separate an inference from the testimony that prompted it.
6. Reorder the fragments at least once. Compare whether chronology, contradiction, delayed corroboration, or another relation creates the clearest inquiry without manufacturing causality.
7. Run a voice-integrity audit. Verify quotations and paraphrases, remove composite or invented speech, and retain meaningful conflict when the record does not support resolution.
8. Run an authority audit. Confirm that sound cues, dramatic pacing, source quantity, archival survival, and the writer's confident narration have not made one fragment prove more than its basis permits.

## Success Check
- Every borrowed voice and document has a visible attribution route.
- Exact words, paraphrase, reconstruction, and authorial interpretation remain distinguishable.
- Arrangement creates development without inventing an exchange that never occurred.
- Competing accounts retain differences relevant to access, position, and consequence.
- Authorial bridges orient and interpret without impersonating neutrality.
- Removing any fragment either damages the inquiry or reveals that the fragment was unnecessary.

## Common Failures
- Writing plausible dialogue for a real person and presenting it as recovered testimony.
- Letting speaker labels create documentary authority for words that were reconstructed.
- Treating several voices as an objective composite merely because they disagree.
- Cutting uncertainty or contradiction so the montage lands on a cleaner conclusion.
- Using dramatic sound, pacing, or juxtaposition to imply a causal relation the evidence does not support.
- Allowing the author's bridge to settle a dispute that the surviving material leaves open.

## Notes
Documentary montage turns selection and adjacency into argument. A letter beside a journal entry, interview, official record, or later reflection can expose relations that continuous summary would flatten. The form remains nonfiction only when its drama comes from supported language, explicit mediation, and accountable arrangement. Attribution is therefore not clerical decoration: it tells readers whose words they encounter, when and why those words were produced, and how much authority the montage may responsibly draw from them.
