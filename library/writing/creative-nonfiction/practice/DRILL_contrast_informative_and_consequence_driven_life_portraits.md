# Contrast Informative and Consequence-Driven Life Portraits

## Practice Task
Write one compact life portrait led by information and another led by a consequential displacement, then revise the informational version so its facts form meaningful relations without acquiring invented causes.

## Target Skill
Move between expository fact, concrete summary, and brief narrative pressure while preserving the truth permissions of the chosen subject.

## Setup
For the first round, create an explicitly fictional biographical subject and four dated or otherwise checkable-seeming facts about the person's life. State that the material is invented. For a later evidence-bound transfer, use only facts that have been verified or responsibly qualified.

## Instructions
1. Draft a paragraph of no more than 150 words whose primary job is to inform readers about the subject through the four facts.
2. Draft a second paragraph of no more than 150 words in which circumstance forces the subject to leave a much-loved home. Establish the cause, one embodied choice or action, and one physical or emotional consequence.
3. Underline every relation created by sequence, contrast, causation, motive, or consequence in both paragraphs. Circle facts that remain an inert catalog.
4. Compare the passages for orientation, specificity, movement, emotional pressure, and the amount of inference invited from readers. Do not assume that greater drama automatically means greater usefulness.
5. Rewrite the informational paragraph so at least two facts alter the meaning of another fact. Use chronology, contrast, recurrence, or supportable consequence; do not invent a causal link merely to make the profile cohere.
6. If transferring the exercise to a real person, label every retained relation as documented, reported, inferred, disputed, or unknown. Remove or qualify any connection whose fluency exceeds its evidence.
7. Select the mode or combination appropriate to the larger work: information when readers need context, concrete summary when duration matters, and brief scene when a choice or consequence deserves immediate experience.

## Success Check
- The first drafts expose a real difference between cataloged information and consequence-driven movement.
- The revision connects facts without falsely implying that sequence proves causation.
- The narrative passage contains an active circumstance, response, and aftereffect rather than emotion alone.
- Information and scene are judged by their function in the larger inquiry, not by a rule that one is always superior.
- Any real-person transfer preserves the status and limits of its evidence.

## Common Failures
- Forgetting that the invented first round uses permissions unavailable in biography.
- Adding melodrama instead of tracing a specific consequence.
- Turning chronological adjacency into an unsupported causal claim.
- Replacing useful context with scene merely because scene feels more literary.
- Treating a few selected facts as a complete judgment of the person.

## Notes
Facts become narrative material through their relations, but relation is also where distortion can enter. The fictional first round makes experimentation safe and visible; the evidence-bound transfer tests whether the same structural intelligence can work without manufacturing actuality. Informative and narrative modes are complementary: one supplies footing and duration, while the other concentrates choice and consequence.
