# Balance Emotional Regard with Particular Character Evidence

## Practice Task
Revise one portrait dominated by affection, grief, admiration, anger, or condemnation until the emotion remains strong but depends on particular, supportable evidence rather than generic declaration.

## Target Skill
Make emotional regard credible through selected conduct, objects, habits, contradictions, and consequences while keeping the narrator's feeling distinct from a total judgment of the person.

## Setup
Choose a bounded passage about a real person that you are entitled to portray. Copy it twice. In the first copy, mark every direct emotion or judgment; in the second, mark every observable action, spoken phrase, object, physical routine, contradiction, and consequence. Note which details come from direct observation, memory, report, record, or inference.

## Instructions
1. State the passage's governing regard in one precise phrase, such as protective affection, unresolved grief, reluctant admiration, frightened anger, or earned condemnation.
2. Circle generic declarations that repeat the regard without changing it. Keep one only when its placement or phrasing creates a necessary turn.
3. Select three to five supportable particulars from different channels: action under pressure, characteristic speech, use of an object, bodily or domestic routine, effect on another person, or a contradiction that complicates the dominant feeling.
4. Draft a version that lets those particulars carry most of the emotional weight. Place the strongest detail where it changes the reader's understanding rather than where it merely illustrates an announced verdict.
5. Add one proportionate counter-detail when the original portrait idealizes or demonizes the person. Do not manufacture balance; use only evidence the essay can support.
6. Audit every inferred motive. Replace unsupported explanation with conduct, attribution, qualification, or acknowledged uncertainty.
7. Compare the original and revised versions for reader effect. Identify whether any removal has made the passage evasive, cold, falsely neutral, or less accountable, and restore only what the emotional movement actually needs.
8. Finish with a sentence or action that leaves the person specific and the narrator's regard legible without converting either into a universal lesson or complete life judgment.

## Success Check
- The governing regard is stated in one precise phrase rather than as general warmth or hostility.
- Generic declarations are circled and removed, with any survivor kept for a placement or phrasing that creates a necessary turn.
- Three to five particulars come from different channels, so the evidence is not all speech or all action.
- At least one particular complicates the dominant regard rather than confirming it.
- The emotion remains strong once the declarations are gone; where it weakens, the missing work is identified rather than a declaration restored.
- A reader who does not share the writer's relation to the subject arrives at the regard from the evidence alone.

## Common Failures
- Replacing generic praise or blame with picturesque details that reveal no conduct, relation, or consequence.
- Treating one touching, comic, or cruel incident as a complete character verdict.
- Adding a token flaw or virtue solely to simulate fairness.
- Inventing motive so the selected details appear to support the narrator's preferred feeling.
- Flattening grief, affection, anger, or condemnation into detached neutrality in the name of restraint.
- Using cultural background, illness, class, age, or family role as an automatic explanation of behavior.

## Notes
Emotional force and evidentiary discipline are not opposites. Strong regard becomes more persuasive when readers encounter the particulars that produced, altered, or resisted it. The aim is neither sentimentality nor emotional suppression, but a proportionate portrait in which feeling belongs visibly to the narrator and the represented person remains more specific than that feeling.
