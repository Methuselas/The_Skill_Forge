# Audit a Diary across Entry, Revision, and Publication Layers

## Practice Task
Compare a contemporaneous diary entry with a later revision or publication-ready version, then make every change of knowledge, audience, and editorial authority legible.

## Target Skill
Use diary form without treating dates, intimacy, or apparent spontaneity as proof that the surviving text is private, contemporaneous, complete, or unedited.

## Setup
Choose one entry you are entitled to use. Preserve an untouched copy and record its entry date, intended or imagined addressee, and what the writer could know at that time. If no historical revision exists, create a clearly labeled later revision of your own entry for the exercise.

## Instructions
1. Mark what the entry records as event, perception, feeling, report, inference, or uncertainty. Flag explanations that seem aimed at someone other than the stated recipient.
2. Map the original telling situation: actual writer, stated addressee, implied reader, privacy expectation, immediate pressure, and plausible circulation.
3. Create or inspect a later layer. Mark additions, deletions, reordered material, sharpened descriptions, corrected facts, anonymized identities, and judgments made possible only by hindsight.
4. Attribute every alteration to the entry writer, a later self, an editor, a translator, or another identified role. Leave authorship unresolved when the available record cannot establish it.
5. Compare the reader effects of the untouched and revised versions. Identify where continuity, intimacy, suspense, self-portrait, or apparent candor increased because of craft rather than because the later version is more immediate.
6. Restore necessary distinctions between contemporaneous text and later intervention through labels, notes, typography, framing, or separate sections appropriate to the destination.
7. Audit identity protection and omission. Confirm that each change reduces exposure or improves focus without transferring blame, changing an outcome, or concealing a consequential contradiction.
8. Write a short editorial statement naming which layer readers encounter, who changed it, what kind of change occurred, and what the resulting text cannot prove.

## Success Check
- Entry-time knowledge remains distinct from hindsight and later research.
- The stated addressee, implied reader, and eventual audience are not collapsed into one recipient.
- Additions, deletions, anonymization, translation, and external editing have attributable roles or explicit uncertainty.
- Dates and intimate address organize the form without being treated as guarantees of spontaneity or privacy.
- Reader-facing framing preserves consequential differences between the surviving layers.
- Editorial changes do not silently alter responsibility, outcome, or evidentiary status.

## Common Failures
- Treating a dated heading as proof that every sentence was written on that date.
- Calling a diary private while its explanations and revisions anticipate strangers.
- Folding a later correction into the entry as though the writer knew it at the time.
- Assuming publication preserved the complete original merely because the published text retains diary form.
- Using anonymization or omission to conceal a consequential fabrication or shift responsibility.
- Letting an editor's arrangement appear to be the diarist's uninterrupted voice.

## Notes
Diary form creates a powerful present-tense relationship among event, writer, and recipient, but a surviving diary may contain several acts of composition. The first entry selects experience; a later self may revise that selection; editors, translators, and publishers may alter what reaches readers. Separating those layers does not weaken the form. It lets intimacy, continuity, and retrospective meaning work without borrowing authority from a false appearance of untouched immediacy.
