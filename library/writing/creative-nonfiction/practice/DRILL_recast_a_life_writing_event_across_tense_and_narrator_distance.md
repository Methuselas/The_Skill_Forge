# Recast a Life-Writing Event across Tense and Narrator Distance

## Practice Task
Write the same supportable event in past and present tense, then test a version that makes the relation between the experiencing person and reflective narrator explicit.

## Target Skill
Choose tense and narrator distance by their effects on access, hindsight, voice, and reader trust without changing the event's evidentiary basis.

## Setup
Choose one consequential event that can be rendered responsibly in 150 to 300 words. Make an evidence key distinguishing remembered, observed, documented, reported, inferred, reconstructed, disputed, and unknown material. Fix the event boundary and do not add new facts during the comparison.

## Instructions
1. State what the person inside the event knew, wanted, feared, and could perceive at that time. Separately state what the later narrator knows and why the event is being revisited.
2. Draft the event in past tense. Permit hindsight only where the reflective narrator is audibly present, and mark every judgment unavailable to the earlier self.
3. Draft the complete event in present tense. Restrict the experiencing voice to contemporaneous knowledge; do not let grammatical immediacy smuggle in later causes, outcomes, or polished interpretation.
4. Draft a third version that deliberately coordinates both positions through framing, interruption, juxtaposition, or a clear temporal shift. Keep each voice's knowledge and diction distinguishable.
5. Compare the versions for sensory proximity, reflective range, uncertainty, irony, pace, emotional distance, and the amount of temporal explanation required.
6. Audit every concrete detail and quotation against the evidence key. Restore qualification wherever a more immediate version has made reconstruction sound remembered or verified.
7. Choose the version whose temporal relation serves the inquiry, or combine them only where the shift changes access or interpretation rather than merely increasing excitement.
8. Read the selected version without tense labels. Revise until readers can infer who is perceiving, who is interpreting, and how much hindsight governs each passage.

## Success Check
- All versions preserve the same event boundary and factual status.
- Present tense changes temporal experience without being treated as proof of accuracy.
- The earlier person possesses no knowledge available only after the event.
- Reflective commentary adds a distinct interpretation rather than paraphrasing the scene.
- Every shift in tense or narrator distance is legible and performs a named job.
- The selected version retains necessary uncertainty while creating the intended access and pressure.

## Common Failures
- Changing details between versions so tense is no longer the variable being tested.
- Assuming past tense is detached or present tense is vivid without comparing the complete event.
- Giving a child or earlier self the vocabulary, judgment, or causal knowledge of the later narrator.
- Treating dialect, sensory density, or present-tense narration as recovered authenticity.
- Removing evidentiary qualifications because they interrupt an immediate scene.
- Alternating then and now without establishing which consciousness controls each sentence.

## Notes
Tense is a formal relation between event and telling, not a measure of truth. Present tense can foreground uncertainty and bodily sequence; past tense can carry retrospective organization with less grammatical strain; a dual-temporal version can make the gap between experience and interpretation itself the subject. The evidence key remains invariant so the exercise measures craft rather than rewarding invented precision.
