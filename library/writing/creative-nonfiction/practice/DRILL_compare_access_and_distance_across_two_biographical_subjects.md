# Compare Access and Distance across Two Biographical Subjects

## Practice Task
Research and portray the same bounded aspect of one personally known subject and one public subject, then diagnose how relationship and access changed the evidence, voice, confidence, and ethical burden of each passage.

## Target Skill
Choose and disclose biographical distance deliberately without treating intimacy as accuracy, public records as completeness, or imaginative identification as knowledge.

## Setup
Choose one willing person known to you and one public or historical figure. Select the same narrow aspect for both, such as a work routine, repeated practice, characteristic decision, or relationship to a place. Obtain permission before interviewing the known person, and avoid private or high-risk material unsuitable for a short exercise.

## Instructions
1. Write a relationship statement for each subject: how you know of them, what access you have, what you feel or assume, and what you cannot responsibly claim.
2. Form the same three focused research questions for both subjects. Gather three or four relevant facts for each from proportionate sources, distinguishing observation, interview, record, prior publication, report, inference, and unknown.
3. Audit the evidence sets for asymmetry. Note where familiarity supplies embodied context but encourages assumption, and where documentary distance supplies breadth but loses private, sensory, or corrective knowledge.
4. Draft one portrait of 120 to 180 words for each subject. Keep the topic and approximate scale constant while choosing a narrator position and tone fitted to the available relationship.
5. Mark every evaluative adjective, interior claim, causal link, and assertion of typical behavior. Identify its basis and revise or qualify anything whose confidence exceeds the evidence.
6. Exchange the passages' narrative distance: make the familiar portrait more formally detached and the public portrait more personally present without inventing access. Record which changes clarify position and which merely perform intimacy or authority.
7. Compare reader effects, factual footing, omitted context, privacy exposure, and the degree to which admiration, grief, conflict, or cultural familiarity shaped selection.
8. Write a method note stating which distance each portrait should keep, what further research or consultation it needs, and what relationship disclosure a larger work would owe readers.

## Success Check
- Both passages address a comparable bounded aspect at a comparable scale.
- Emotional closeness, documentary access, imaginative identification, and factual confidence remain separate variables.
- Each consequential detail has an identified basis and each interior claim is supportable or qualified.
- The known subject's cooperation has not been treated as third-party clearance or proof.
- The public subject's available record has not been treated as complete merely because it is extensive.
- The final narrative distance is selected for inquiry and trust rather than inherited unconsciously from the writer's relationship.

## Common Failures
- Choosing subjects or topics so different that distance cannot be isolated from content.
- Letting familiarity replace research or letting research quantity imitate familiarity.
- Writing a warmer voice and mistaking it for ethical or factual intimacy.
- Turning a public figure into an icon, type, villain, or object of imaginative possession.
- Using the exercise to expose a known person who did not agree to participate.
- Treating the subject's approval as control over supported facts or permission to portray everyone around them.

## Notes
Relationship changes biography before the first sentence is written. It determines which questions occur, which sources are available, which details feel obvious, and which harms the writer can foresee. Comparison makes those pressures visible. The aim is not to prove that closeness or distance is superior, but to make each account declare and compensate for the kind of knowledge it can and cannot possess.
