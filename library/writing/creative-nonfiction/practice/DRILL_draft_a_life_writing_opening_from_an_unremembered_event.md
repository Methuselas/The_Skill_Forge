# Draft a Life-Writing Opening from an Unremembered Event

## Practice Task
Draft competing openings for a consequential event outside direct memory, using qualified reconstruction in one and explicit acknowledgment of the gap in the other.

## Target Skill
Make an absence of memory or information create honest curiosity while keeping known fact, report, inference, impression, and invention legible.

## Setup
Choose a birth, early childhood episode, family event, historical interval, or other beginning the narrator cannot directly remember. The gap must matter to a genuine autobiographical or biographical inquiry. Do not use material whose investigation would be unsafe or improperly expose another person.

## Instructions
1. State the work's nonfiction truth contract and current motive. Write the question this missing interval creates for the larger project.
2. Build an evidence table with six categories: directly known, documented, reported by another person, inferred, felt or imagined, and unknown. Record disagreements and unavailable witnesses without resolving them by preference.
3. Research any material fact that can be checked proportionately. If a report remains the only basis, preserve who reported it and the degree of confidence needed in the prose.
4. Draft an opening of 100 to 180 words using bounded reconstruction. Signal the basis of consequential details and omit precision that exists only because a scene feels incomplete.
5. Draft a second opening of the same event and scale by acknowledging the central gap directly. Connect the absence to a present question, relationship, decision, or consequence so it creates pressure rather than empty mystery.
6. For each version, mark what readers are likely to assume is fact, what question makes them continue, and what experience the opening promises. Correct any assumption the wording creates unintentionally.
7. Choose the version that best serves the inquiry and truth contract, or combine them only if the resulting status of every consequential detail remains clear.
8. Recheck the chosen opening after drafting the larger work. Replace it if the gap was merely decorative or if the project discovered a different governing pressure.

## Success Check
- The nonfiction truth contract and current motive are stated before drafting, since together they set what reconstruction is permitted.
- The evidence table separates directly known, documented, reported, inferred, felt or imagined, and unknown, and disagreements remain unresolved rather than settled by preference.
- Checkable facts are checked proportionately, and where a report is the only basis, who reported it and with what confidence is preserved.
- Both openings are written, so qualified reconstruction and explicit acknowledgment are compared rather than one chosen in advance.
- Neither version lets an inference migrate into the documented category, verified against the table rather than against memory of it.
- The choice is defended by what each opening does for the larger project's question, not by which reads more smoothly.

## Common Failures
- Narrating an inaccessible event with cinematic certainty because the scene is plausible.
- Treating a repeated family story or unconscious image as verified memory.
- Announcing that memory is unreliable without identifying the particular uncertainty.
- Using a gap that has no consequence beyond the opening paragraph.
- Refusing accessible research so ignorance can serve as a hook.
- Combining reconstruction and acknowledgment until readers cannot tell which details claim to be true.

## Notes
An unremembered beginning does not force a choice between sterile silence and fabricated scene. Qualified reconstruction can use records and reports while showing their limits; direct acknowledgment can make the missing fact itself part of the inquiry. Both routes succeed only when the absence is bounded, consequential, and consistent with the reader's factual expectations.
