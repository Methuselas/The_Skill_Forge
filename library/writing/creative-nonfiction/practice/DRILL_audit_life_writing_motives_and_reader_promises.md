# Audit Life-Writing Motives and Reader Promises

## Practice Task
Identify the motives acting on a biographical or autobiographical project, test how each would change its selections and reader promise, and choose a working configuration that remains open to discovery.

## Target Skill
Use purpose as a revisable craft constraint without reducing a life to one explanation, disguising commercial or corrective aims, or confusing a writer's motive with proof of authority.

## Setup
Choose a life-writing project, substantial fragment, or clearly described idea. Gather its title, opening, current notes, intended audience, and any private statement of why the writer wants to make it.

## Instructions
1. List every plausible motive without ranking its respectability: recording events, preserving a person or place, exploring identity or a state of mind, answering a question, correcting another account, seeking peace, creating art, reaching readers, earning income, or another specific purpose.
2. Separate evidence from inference. Mark motives the writer has stated, motives demonstrated by selection or framing, and motives merely suggested by title, publicity, or your own response.
3. For each motive, write one sentence naming what it would cause the project to include, emphasize, investigate, or omit.
4. Identify two motives that cooperate and two that create pressure. State how an unresolved conflict could distort evidence, overexpose a person, flatten the narrative, or promise readers a different work from the one being drafted.
5. Write a working purpose statement containing the active inquiry, the writer's stake, the intended reader experience, and the governing truth contract. Permit more than one motive when each changes a real decision.
6. Test the title and opening against that statement. Record what each makes a reader expect, but treat that interpretation as a hypothesis rather than proof of the writer's purpose or authority.
7. Revisit the audit after a substantial drafting interval. Revise the purpose statement when the material reveals a more accurate reason for continuing, then identify one selection or framing decision that should change with it.

## Success Check
- Stated, demonstrated, and inferred motives remain distinguishable.
- Multiple motives are retained only when they govern different consequential choices.
- Conflicts among correction, intimacy, art, preservation, audience, and commerce are visible rather than moralized away.
- The working purpose guides selection while leaving the inquiry capable of discovery.
- The title, opening, truth contract, and intended reader experience make compatible promises.
- A later audit can change the purpose without making the earlier drafting interval invalid.

## Common Failures
- Choosing the most admirable motive while hiding the one that actually governs selection.
- Treating a title, relationship, access claim, or dramatic opening as proof of superior truthfulness.
- Naming audience so broadly that no contextual or ethical decision changes.
- Freezing an initial purpose after the draft has discovered a different center.
- Using motive to excuse unsupported claims, retaliation, or unnecessary exposure.

## Notes
Purpose is both generative and diagnostic. It can suggest where to begin, which facts need investigation, and what kind of experience the work offers, but it cannot establish the truth of the resulting account. The audit remains useful because life-writing motives often overlap and change: revisiting them turns that complexity into deliberate selection rather than concealed contradiction.
