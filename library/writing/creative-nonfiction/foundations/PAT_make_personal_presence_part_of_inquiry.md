# Make Personal Presence Part of the Inquiry

## Pattern Rule
**IF** a creative nonfiction draft either hides behind supposedly neutral facts or lets the writer's presence overwhelm the subject being examined
**THEN** position the selecting, perceiving, feeling, and interpreting self at the center, beside others, near the edge, or offstage according to the part that presence must play in the inquiry
**ELSE** preserve the current authorial position while checking that its degree of visibility remains purposeful

## Do
- Identify why this writer noticed this subject and which experiences, tastes, commitments, or limits shape the available account.
- Place the writer at the center when inner life or personal action is the subject, share the foreground when relationships drive the inquiry, and move toward the edge when another person, place, or body of information needs primary attention.
- Let reflection disclose how encounters with people, places, research, or memory alter the writer's understanding.
- In reported work, acknowledge participation, access, and point of view instead of imitating an invisible collector of facts.
- When the first-person pronoun stays offstage, let consequential choices of syntax, diction, emphasis, and arrangement keep the writer's perspective legible.
- Examine self-disclosure for what it contributes to inquiry rather than for how candid or dramatic it appears alone.

## Don't
- Pretend selection is objective merely because the prose avoids first-person pronouns.
- Insert memoir fragments that do not change the subject's meaning or the writer's investigation.
- Use confession as a guarantee of honesty when the surrounding claims remain unsupported.
- Make personal charm, humor, or authority crowd out people and facts the essay claims to examine.
- Treat the number of first-person pronouns as a reliable measure of how strongly the writer controls the work.

## Checklist
- Readers can tell what connects the writer to the subject.
- The writer's central, shared, peripheral, or offstage position matches the attention owed to the subject and other people.
- The work reveals how selection and interpretation shape this version of events or information.
- Personal material performs reflection, access, context, or transformation rather than decoration.
- The writer's limits and stakes are visible where they affect trust.
- Removing the personal presence would erase a consequential dimension of the inquiry.

## Notes
Presence varies by form and by the job of a particular passage. Memoir may place the writer's life at the center; an essay about a relationship may divide attention; nature writing or literary journalism may keep the writer at the edge while still revealing attention, access, judgment, and response. Offstage does not mean absent: voice survives in sentence construction, word choice, emphasis, and the route through the material. The important distinction is not I versus no I, but a position fitted to the inquiry versus either false neutrality or needless domination.

`writing_creative_nonfiction_variant_declare_biographer_relationship_and_distance` makes the conditions of a biographical account available for scrutiny. Name what contact, affection, conflict, expertise, invitation, archive access, or distance permits the writer to know, then name the blind spots or pressures it creates. Emotional closeness can provide intimate evidence while intensifying partiality; professional or historical distance can widen context while limiting embodied knowledge. Neither position certifies accuracy, and the number of first-person statements is less useful than whether consequential selection and interpretation remain legible.

`writing_creative_nonfiction_variant_declare_traveler_position_and_limits` treats movement through a place as situated access rather than automatic authority. Keep itinerary, time, weather, bodily condition, transport, language ability, hospitality, restriction, dependence, and prior expectation available when they shape what the traveler notices or misses. Distinguish direct observation from another person's explanation, later research, and the writer's interpretation. A journey can expose the observer's changing assumptions and relationship to place, but brief contact, hardship, or sensory intensity does not authorize a culture-wide conclusion.
