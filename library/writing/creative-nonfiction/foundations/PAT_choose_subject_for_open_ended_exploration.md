# Choose a Subject for Open-Ended Exploration

## Pattern Rule
**IF** a creative nonfiction topic arrives as an obligation, a settled thesis, or a display of knowledge with nothing left for the writer to discover
**THEN** choose or reframe a subject that carries genuine personal interest, unanswered questions, and enough angles for the act of writing to change the writer's understanding

## Do
- Test whether the subject continues to provoke curiosity after its obvious anecdote, opinion, or fact has been stated.
- Begin from a small daily detail when it opens onto a larger uncertainty about relation, identity, belief, history, or experience.
- Record what the writer currently thinks while leaving space for memory, research, and encounter to complicate that position.
- Follow a promising aside or contradiction when it deepens the inquiry, then relate it back to the work's developing center.
- Prefer a subject the writer wants to spend time with over one chosen solely because it sounds important.

## Don't
- Treat the essay as a delivery system for a conclusion already immune to evidence or reflection.
- Confuse open-ended exploration with wandering that never accumulates purpose.
- Inflate an ordinary detail merely to make the writer appear significant.
- Choose a controversial position only to perform independence from popular opinion.

## Checklist
- The subject contains at least one question the writer cannot answer before drafting.
- Personal investment is strong enough to sustain rereading, research, and reconsideration.
- Small particulars connect to broader stakes without losing their specificity.
- Digressions return with changed pressure or understanding.
- The emerging conclusion records discovery rather than restating the opening assumption.

## Notes
An essay is an attempt: it can test, speculate, backtrack, and revise its thinking in public. That flexibility does not remove design; it changes design from proof of a fixed thesis into a shaped record of inquiry. A modest subject may support substantial discovery when examined closely, while a grand assigned topic can remain inert when the writer has no live question inside it.

`writing_creative_nonfiction_variant_investigate_overlooked_event_or_life` directs that curiosity toward the portion omitted by an official or dominant account. Research the neglected event or participant rather than inventing the missing life, and use the recovered evidence to test what the larger story hid or simplified. The route is useful when marginal detail changes understanding, not when obscurity alone is treated as importance.
