# Match Models to Draft Scale

## Pattern Rule
**IF** you are choosing published work to study before writing a bounded piece
**THEN** prioritize excellent complete works close enough to the target length and complexity that their whole-form decisions can transfer into the planned draft
**ELSE** use a larger work only for one explicitly named local move rather than as the architectural model

## Do
- Write a compact target brief first: intended form, approximate length, available drafting time, unfamiliar genre decisions, and expected revision depth.
- For a four-page story, study a deft short-short as the main whole-form precedent rather than assuming a 6,000-word story demonstrates what four pages can hold.
- Keep the quality threshold high while reducing scope. Concision is the constraint; weak or artificially simplified prose is not the answer.
- Label each reading either a whole-form model or a local craft example such as dialogue, compression, or an ending.

## Don't
- Don't choose a famous long work as the main production precedent merely because it is canonical; admiration does not make its scale transferable.
- Don't plan a brief piece while surrounding yourself with examples whose length and complexity silently imply a different project.

## Checklist
- Can I close-read the complete model within the project budget?
- Can my intended draft solve a comparable number of structural problems at its planned length?
- Is the model strong enough to reward imitation without inviting a copy of its subject or voice?
- If the example is larger than my draft, have I named the exact local move I am studying?

## Notes
The source makes the scale test concrete by contrasting Raymond Carver's roughly 6,000-word “Cathedral” with the much briefer “Popular Mechanics” for a short classroom story. The transferable distinction is not masterpiece versus beginner work. Both examples should be excellent; the useful model is the one whose full design can inform the piece actually being written.
