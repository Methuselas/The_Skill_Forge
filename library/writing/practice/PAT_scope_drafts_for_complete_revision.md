# Scope Drafts for Complete Revision

## Pattern Rule
**IF** you are learning an unfamiliar form or working within a fixed drafting and feedback budget
**THEN** choose a complete short piece small enough to restart, close-read, critique, and substantially revise instead of spending the entire budget producing pages
**ELSE** reduce the number of simultaneous projects until the longer work still receives a full revision cycle

## Do
- Budget backward from the desired feedback and revision passes, leaving room for structural rethinking rather than allocating all available time to the first draft.
- Choose a complete form whose boundaries fit the experiment, such as a lyric poem, short-short story, brief creative nonfiction piece, or ten-minute play.
- Set the length low enough that deleting a failed opening or beginning again remains a credible choice.
- Use the saved page budget for another look: a clean new draft, a close critique, and a pass focused on the form's governing decisions.

## Don't
- Don't confuse fewer pages with lower standards; the constraint exists to support closer analysis and more thorough revision.
- Don't rush through several forms at lengths that leave time only for first drafts.
- Don't treat the short work as a disposable fragment of a future “real” piece. Make it fulfill the promises of its own form.

## Checklist
- Could I restart after discovering that the draft's governing idea is wrong?
- Can the complete piece receive passage-level feedback within the time available?
- Is at least one structural revision planned before proofreading?
- Will the draft reach an ending rather than stop as an exploratory opening?

## Notes
The source links scale to behavior: a writer three pages into a four-page story is more willing to start over than one ten pages into twelve, while a short complete work can receive closer reading and response. Shortness is therefore a revision control, not merely a way to sample more genres.
