# Make Figurative Language Unexpected and Fitting

## Pattern Rule
**IF** literal wording is flat or a poem needs a comparison, substitution, or wordplay to create a special effect
**THEN** build a relation that is surprising enough to renew attention and apt enough to reveal the subject more precisely
**ELSE** prefer direct language to a figure that adds only decoration or cleverness

## Do
- Separate the subject from the comparison term and state what credible qualities form the bridge between them before writing the line.
- Search beyond the first familiar likeness, then keep the candidate whose differences create useful energy without breaking the underlying fit.
- Use one productive metaphor or simile as an organizing center when its implications can generate and order several parts of the poem.
- Audit association-based substitutions for inherited shorthand; metonymy and part-for-whole language lose force when convention supplies the entire connection.
- Moderate personification and punning by asking whether the human attribute or double meaning deepens the poem rather than merely displaying technique.

## Don't
- Use an overfamiliar vehicle whose meaning arrives before the reader has to imagine anything.
- Assume that naming a figure of speech makes a line poetic when the relationship itself is predictable or inaccurate.
- Award human behavior to an object until the result becomes unintentionally ridiculous.
- Keep a pun whose comic pressure conflicts with the surrounding tone or whose second meaning contributes nothing beyond the joke.

## Checklist
- The relation between subject and figurative term can be explained without relying on a stock phrase.
- The connection is unexpected yet remains appropriate to the poem's context and tone.
- The figure reveals, complicates, or organizes the subject rather than merely renaming it.
- Conventional associations, human attributes, and wordplay have been tested for cliche or accidental absurdity.
- Removing the figure would subtract a specific insight or structural function.

## Notes
A metaphor applies one term to another; a simile signals the likeness explicitly. Both succeed through the bridge between the things, not through the labels used to classify them. Other figures shift the mechanism - association in metonymy, part for whole in synecdoche, human qualities in personification, multiple meanings in a pun - but they share the same burden: the departure from ordinary language must produce a controlled effect worth the risk of familiarity, excess, or tonal drift.
