# Redraft One Poem Under Competing Titles

## Practice Task
Create several title frames for one rough poem, let each frame drive a distinct revision, and select the pairing whose expectation and retrospective meaning best serve the work.

## Target Skill
Title-directed poetic generation and revision.

## Setup
Choose a rough poem with live material but an uncertain center or merely descriptive working title. Preserve a baseline copy and prepare separate copies for at least four candidate frames.

## Instructions
1. Write candidates with unlike functions: a direct subject, a charged image or phrase, a defining relation, and a frame whose meaning could change after the ending.
2. Before revising, write one sentence for what each candidate makes a reader expect from the opening.
3. Give each copy a bounded revision pass. Change images, order, emphasis, voice, or ending wherever the active frame creates a stronger demand; do more than replace the heading.
4. Remove the headings temporarily and compare what each pass discovered, intensified, distorted, or made redundant.
5. Restore the headings and reread each complete pairing, noting how the candidate sounds before the first line and what it means after the last.
6. Select the pairing with the most useful double action. Import material from another version only when it performs a missing job without weakening that action.
7. If any candidate uses borrowed language, verify attribution and reuse requirements before treating it as selectable.

## Success Check
- The candidates have unlike functions — direct subject, charged phrase, defining relation, and a frame whose meaning shifts after the ending — rather than being variations on one idea.
- The expectation each creates is written before revision, so the frame acts as a hypothesis rather than a description of what happened.
- Each pass changes images, order, emphasis, voice, or ending where its frame demands it, and a pass that only replaced the heading is redone.
- Headings are removed for the comparison, so drafts are judged on what they discovered.
- The selected pairing is defended by both expectation and retrospective meaning, not by which title sounds best alone.
- At least one pass produced something worth keeping in a draft that was not selected.

## Common Failures
- Generating synonyms that all perform the same labeling function.
- Choosing the cleverest isolated phrase without testing its promise against the poem.
- Preserving every change from the preferred revision even when some belonged only to its discarded frame.
- Using obscurity to simulate depth or direct explanation to remove all interpretive activity.
- Discovering borrowed wording only after selection and assuming attribution can be solved later.

## Notes
The comparison isolates framing as a compositional force. A useful candidate does not merely describe existing content; it alters what the writer notices, selects, and orders, then remains available for reinterpretation after the poem has moved. Saving separate copies keeps those pressures legible and prevents a single compromise draft from hiding which frame produced which discovery.
