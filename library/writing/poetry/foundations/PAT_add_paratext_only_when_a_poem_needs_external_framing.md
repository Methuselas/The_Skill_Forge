# Add Paratext Only When a Poem Needs External Framing

## Pattern Rule
**IF** a poem requires an outside text, unfamiliar term, or missing context for the intended reading to begin or complete
**THEN** supply the smallest necessary epigraph, gloss, or note in the position that matches its function, with appropriate attribution and reuse clearance
**ELSE** remove the external frame and make the poem carry its own situation

## Do
- Place an epigraph before the poem when a prior voice, relation, tonal key, or interpretive lens must be active from the opening.
- Place a gloss, footnote, or endnote outside or after the poem when a term or contextual fact should be available without controlling the initial encounter.
- Test the poem both with and without the addition; keep it only when the intended effect or intelligibility materially changes.
- Shorten the supporting material until it performs one necessary framing job without becoming a second argument beside the poem.
- Identify the author and work behind retained language, and confirm that quotation, attribution, and reuse fit the intended venue.

## Don't
- Borrow authority or prestige from a famous line when the poem has no consequential relation to it.
- Use a note to explain an image, emotion, or movement the poem itself has failed to make available.
- Leave a quotation's origin unknown because it entered through a notebook or generative exercise.
- Retain a drafting prompt after the poem has transformed or outgrown the relation that once made it useful.

## Checklist
- The external material performs a named contextual, relational, tonal, or lexical function.
- Its position matches whether the information must precede the poem or remain available outside its first movement.
- Removing it creates a specific loss rather than merely making the presentation less literary.
- The poem remains responsible for its own tension, movement, and completion.
- Every retained quotation or borrowed phrase has suitable attribution and reuse handling.

## Notes
External framing changes the order in which a reader receives information. An epigraph enters the poem before its first line and therefore exerts early pressure; a gloss or note can preserve the initial encounter while supplying context at the edge or afterward. Material that generated the draft does not automatically deserve this authority in the finished work. The lightest successful frame is usually the one that supplies what readers genuinely need while leaving the poem to create its own meaning.
