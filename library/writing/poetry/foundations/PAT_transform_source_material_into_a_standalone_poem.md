# Transform Source Material into a Standalone Poem

## Pattern Rule
**IF** another poem, artwork, news account, performance, or media work supplies the starting pressure for a new poem
**THEN** identify its most charged action, tension, image, or viewpoint and transform that force into an independently intelligible poetic experience
**ELSE** choose a more generative source or begin from material that already carries personal verbal energy

## Do
- Locate the moment of maximum energy rather than responding to the source's whole subject indiscriminately.
- Name what makes that moment work—an image, contradiction, withheld fact, formal move, or shift in perspective—before attempting an equivalent effect with new material.
- Move beyond description or summary by introducing an imaginative departure, personal association, or consequential viewpoint the source does not fully supply.
- When facts matter, keep them available during drafting while testing perspectives outside the most obvious participant or narrator.
- Remove, transform, or credit borrowed wording according to the intended use; do not let an attractive line become unmarked authorship.

## Don't
- Assume that mentioning a recognizable work, event, or character gives the poem significance of its own.
- Retell a source from beginning to end when one charged detail can create stronger pressure.
- Depend on the reader's prior knowledge to supply the poem's missing situation, stakes, or movement.
- Preserve borrowed language merely because the draft began from it.

## Checklist
- The draft can be followed by a reader who has not encountered the initiating material.
- The response transfers a specific source mechanism rather than copying only subject matter or surface style.
- Description, summary, and reference have been converted into new imaginative or personal movement.
- Any retained wording is handled with the attribution appropriate to the context.
- The poem has its own tension and completion rather than ending where the source's relevance has been demonstrated.

## Notes
Source response is most productive when attention shifts from what a work is about to where it becomes most alive. A painting can launch a private narrative, a news item can open through a peripheral witness, and another poem can contribute a line or structural pressure that the new work eventually transforms or removes. The source is a springboard: it initiates discovery but does not remain responsible for making the finished poem whole.
