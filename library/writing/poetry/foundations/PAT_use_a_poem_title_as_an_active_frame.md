# Use a Poem Title as an Active Frame

## Pattern Rule
**IF** a working title merely labels a draft, or the poem lacks a productive frame for generation and rereading
**THEN** audition titles that create different expectations, redraft under the most consequential frames, and keep the one whose meaning deepens across the complete poem
**ELSE** retain the current title only after testing what it causes a reader to anticipate before the opening and reconsider after the ending

## Do
- Try unlike functions: name the governing subject directly, foreground a charged image or phrase, establish a relation, or introduce a frame whose significance changes during the poem.
- Read the opening under each candidate and identify the question, tone, scale, or interpretive route it supplies before any line can answer it.
- Reread the ending against the same candidate and check whether the completed poem complicates, redirects, or enriches the initial expectation.
- When a candidate exposes a stronger center, redraft the poem under that pressure instead of treating titling as a final labeling task.
- Verify attribution and reuse requirements before retaining language borrowed from another work in the title.

## Don't
- Summarize the poem so completely that the title removes discovery from the reading.
- Choose an obscure or dramatic phrase whose promise the poem never fulfills.
- Preserve the first working title because it records the drafting prompt after the poem has moved elsewhere.
- Reduce the choice to a fixed descriptive-versus-evocative taxonomy when another function better serves the work.

## Checklist
- Before the opening, the title creates an accurate and useful field of expectation.
- After the ending, the same words retain, change, or gain meaning rather than becoming redundant.
- A competing title was tested by its effect on the draft, not only by how attractive it sounds alone.
- The selected frame serves the poem's governing movement without explaining away productive ambiguity.
- Any borrowed wording is permitted and attributed as the use context requires.

## Notes
A title operates twice: first as an instruction for entering the poem, then as language the reader mentally revisits after the poem has changed its context. That double action makes titling part of composition. A direct label can concentrate attention, while an image, phrase, relation, or oblique frame can generate productive uncertainty; none is inherently superior. The test is whether the frame changes how the poem can be written and read without promising a different work.
