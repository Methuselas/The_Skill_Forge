# Construct Persona Through Perception Desire and Verbal Form

## Pattern Rule
**IF** a poem speaks through another person, an animal, an object, or a selected self distinct from the whole author
**THEN** build the speaker from bounded perception, desire, knowledge, relation, and a verbal form shaped by how that presence encounters the world
**ELSE** keep the authorial resemblance while analyzing the textual speaker with the same distance

## Do
- Begin with observable properties, circumstances, affordances, and pressures before inventing interior claims.
- Decide what the speaker most wants to communicate and what it cannot, will not, or does not know how to say.
- Let physical form or habitual action influence syntax and sound only when the result remains more than a verbal gimmick.
- Select details the persona would notice and comparisons it could plausibly make from its position.
- Permit human, animal, object, and allegorical readings to coexist when the poem sustains them without reducing the speaker to one message.

## Don't
- Build a voice from accent, catchphrase, species stereotype, or obvious pun while leaving attention and desire generic.
- Claim that imaginative translation reveals another being's actual consciousness.
- Treat empathy as sufficient knowledge when a human identity or lived condition requires research and accountability.
- Let theatrical delivery conceal language that does not establish the persona on the page.

## Checklist
- The speaker's noticed world differs materially from the author's default account.
- Desire, limitation, syntax, rhythm, and selected detail cooperate.
- Invention remains legible as construction rather than factual access to another consciousness.
- The persona develops beyond its opening premise or conceit.
- Removing the speaker would erase a necessary interpretation rather than merely change pronouns.

## Notes
Persona is a way of perceiving and interpreting, not an impersonation contest. A nonhuman starting point can reduce stock characterization because the writer must invent a relation among body, environment, want, and language. Even a closely autobiographical I is selected and shaped; recognizing that construction permits honest distance without denying the poem's intimacy.
