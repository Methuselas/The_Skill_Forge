# Treat Poetic Voice as a Flexible Poem-Specific System

## Pattern Rule
**IF** a poem sounds anonymous, inconsistently quirky, or constrained by pressure to reproduce one recognizable authorial identity
**THEN** derive its speaking presence from the current speaker's attention, vocabulary, syntax, rhythm, omissions, tone, and relation to the subject and listener
**ELSE** preserve the effective system while allowing later poems to require different choices

## Do
- Mark phrases anyone might supply without close attention and replace them from the speaker's particular encounter.
- Mark private turns that only the writer can interpret and decide whether context, revision, or removal will make their pressure available to readers.
- Track recurring words, sounds, sentence movements, subjects, and leaps across several pieces as evidence of affinity rather than as requirements.
- Let silence and transitions contribute: what the speaker skips, delays, or refuses can be as characteristic as stated content.
- Judge each choice by the voice of this poem before deciding whether it belongs to a broader personal range.

## Don't
- Define voice only as a combination of tone and subject while ignoring the language that produces both.
- Force signature words or mannerisms into a poem to make authorship recognizable.
- Call the first fluent draft authentic and protect it from examination.
- Treat repetition of one obsession or verbal habit as proof that every future poem should sound the same.

## Checklist
- The speaker can be described through interacting verbal choices rather than a personality label.
- Generic language and inaccessible private shorthand have both been examined.
- Recurring affinities generate meaning without becoming compulsory trademarks.
- Omissions and transitions fit the same attention and relation as the stated language.
- A different poem could adopt another voice without contradicting the writer's identity.

## Notes
A writer grows into a range by attending closely to language already available and by testing unfamiliar combinations. Some recurring words may carry unusually deep associations, but most language remains shared with readers; the craft lies in selection, placement, sound, and relation. Discovery and design therefore cooperate: the draft reveals tendencies, and revision decides which ones this poem can use.
