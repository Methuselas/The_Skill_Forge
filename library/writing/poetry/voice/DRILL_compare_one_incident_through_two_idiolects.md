# Compare One Incident Through Two Idiolects

## Practice Task
Retell one stable incident through a participant and a witness, then compare how access, attention, judgment, and language alter the account.

## Target Skill
Differentiating speakers through attention, knowledge, evaluation, syntax, and social relation.

## Setup
Choose a remembered or invented incident that can be described in 150–250 words. Define one participant and one witness with different physical positions, relationships, responsibilities, and permission to speak.

## Instructions
1. List the event facts that remain constant in both versions; if the incident is real, distinguish what you know from what you infer.
2. For each speaker, list what can be seen or heard, what matters most, what vocabulary is readily available, what cannot be known, and what the speaker risks by telling the story.
3. Draft the participant's account without consulting the witness list. Let selection, sentence movement, comparisons, omissions, and evaluation follow that position.
4. Redraft the same event through the witness while preserving the stable facts and changing the route through them.
5. Exchange one sentence between the drafts unchanged. Revise wherever it survives too comfortably despite the change in access and relation.
6. Remove any accent spelling, catchphrase, or slang that functions as the primary distinction; rebuild the difference through noticed detail and verbal organization.
7. Place the versions side by side and annotate the exact choices that make each speaker legible.

## Success Check
- The constant facts are listed first, and for a real incident what is known is separated from what is inferred.
- Each speaker's access, priorities, available vocabulary, unknowables, and risk in telling are stated before drafting.
- The participant's account is drafted without consulting the witness list, so the difference is produced rather than arranged.
- The stable facts survive both versions, checked against the list rather than against memory.
- Selection, sentence movement, comparison, omission, and evaluation differ between the accounts. A difference carried only by pronouns has not tested idiolect.
- What each speaker cannot know is visible in the telling itself rather than only in the planning notes.

## Common Failures
- Changing the event so radically that the drafts no longer test voice against a stable incident.
- Differentiating speakers only through pronouns, misspellings, or topical slang.
- Giving the witness knowledge available only to the participant.
- Treating imagined testimony about a real event as something the other person actually said or believed.
- Making one version neutral and the other conspicuously eccentric instead of building two complete idiolects.

## Notes
Holding the incident steady isolates the effects of position and language. The witness need not be less vivid than the participant; distance can redirect attention toward consequence, social judgment, or details invisible from inside the action. When the second account belongs to a real person, the exercise practices plausible perspective and does not establish that person's truth.
