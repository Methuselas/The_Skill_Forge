# Ground Haiku in Present Sensory Perception

## Pattern Rule
**IF** a short poem is being shaped as haiku through a preset syllable count or a generalized statement
**THEN** center a concise moment of awareness in particular time, place, beings, or objects and let exact sensory detail open the insight
**ELSE** preserve the observed moment while cutting any language that explains what the image already makes available

## Do
- Begin from something actually noticed through sight, sound, smell, taste, or touch rather than from a lesson that needs an illustrative scene.
- Establish the subject or situation quickly, then add a detail that requires the reader to approach it from an unexpected angle.
- Use seasonal or other time-and-place information when it makes the moment more particular instead of functioning as a decorative convention.
- Compress until each word contributes perception, relation, atmosphere, or movement while the image remains airy enough for a reader to enter.
- Treat a familiar three-line syllable pattern as one available structure, not as proof that the perceptual action has succeeded.

## Don't
- Count syllables accurately while leaving the poem without concision, awareness, or a shareable sensory moment.
- Inflate a small observation with poetic trickery that competes with the thing observed.
- State the insight as an abstract conclusion after the concrete image has already carried it.
- Restrict attention to pastoral nature when an urban, domestic, or social moment offers equally vivid real beings and objects.

## Checklist
- The poem locates attention in a particular experienced moment.
- Concrete sensory evidence carries more weight than explanation.
- The final detail changes or deepens the reader's approach to the announced subject.
- Simplicity comes from exact selection rather than generic wording.
- Line and syllable choices serve the perception instead of substituting for it.

## Notes
Concision, perception, and awareness are the form's working center. Historical practice often emphasizes season and placement in time, while later English-language practice varies line and syllable counts and admits a wider range of subjects. Across those differences, the durable demand is openness to existence followed by severe selection: a few well-chosen words make a moment available to another person.
