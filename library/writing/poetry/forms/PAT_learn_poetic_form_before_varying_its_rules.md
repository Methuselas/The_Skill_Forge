# Learn a Poetic Form Before Varying Its Rules

## Pattern Rule
**IF** a poem will claim or borrow from a named inherited form
**THEN** map the form's governing constraints against several accomplished examples and state which rules the draft will follow, combine, loosen, or break
**ELSE** describe the structure by its actual behavior rather than attaching a traditional name by resemblance

## Do
- Record the required line and stanza counts, meter or syllable pattern, rhyme scheme, repetitions, turns, and customary content features separately so one visible trait does not stand for the whole form.
- Match the written description against several poems and mark where strong practitioners agree, where they vary, and which departures preserve the form's recognizable action.
- Distinguish a core mechanism, such as a returning refrain or argumentative turn, from a convention that contemporary practice regularly adjusts.
- Combine schemes or alter a rule only after identifying what expressive freedom the change creates and what traditional pressure it removes.

## Don't
- Call an accidental missed requirement experimentation after the draft is finished.
- Infer a complete form from its line count while ignoring the rhyme, repetition, turn, or stanza logic that makes the count meaningful.
- Treat one famous example as the only permissible execution when the tradition contains established alternatives.
- Break so many governing constraints that the form's name promises an experience the poem no longer supplies.

## Checklist
- The form's constraints are written as a working specification rather than remembered as a single fact.
- Several examples have been checked for both compliance and deliberate variation.
- Each planned departure names the retained mechanism that keeps the formal relationship intelligible.
- The writer can explain why the chosen degree of fidelity helps this poem.
- A reader familiar with the form would recognize intentional adaptation rather than incomplete knowledge.

## Notes
Inherited rules can guide movement without dictating every choice. Traditions often contain families of schemes and accepted contemporary variations, so knowledge of the form creates flexibility rather than forbidding it. Deliberate adaptation begins by knowing what pressure a rule applies; otherwise a departure cannot be distinguished from a mistake and its cost cannot be judged.
