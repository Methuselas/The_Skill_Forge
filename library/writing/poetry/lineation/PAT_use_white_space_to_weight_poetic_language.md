# Use White Space to Weight Poetic Language

## Pattern Rule
**IF** short lines, sparse wording, or frequent stanza breaks leave substantial blank space around a poem
**THEN** treat that space as active silence and ensure every isolated word, line, and group merits the increased attention it receives
**ELSE** test whether crowded lines or dense groups overwhelm material that needs room to register

## Do
- Keep the essential images, sounds, and turns while letting peripheral explanation fall outside the poem's frame.
- Compare sparse and denser arrangements to hear how much pause and rereading each version invites.
- Increase verbal strength as the poem becomes shorter or more exposed; greater emptiness makes weak language easier to see.
- Balance horizontal space after lines with vertical space between groups so both kinds of silence serve the same intended effect.

## Don't
- Treat blank paper as unused area that should always be filled.
- Inflate thin language by spreading it across a page and assuming isolation creates significance by itself.
- Crowd many ideas into long lines when the reader needs time to absorb them.

## Checklist
- The most isolated language can withstand the extra scrutiny its placement invites.
- Every large gap creates a useful pause, emphasis, or sense of silence.
- Sparse passages contain enough specific material to reward rereading.
- Dense passages remain readable without flattening the poem's important moments.

## Notes
Empty space changes how readers value what remains. A very short poem or a page with many breaks makes each unit more conspicuous, so compression and strength must rise together. Silence can heighten a poem, but layout cannot substitute for substantial language.
