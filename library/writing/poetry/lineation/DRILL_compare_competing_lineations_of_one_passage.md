# Compare Competing Lineations of One Passage

## Practice Task
Arrange the same short passage into several lineated versions, compare what each boundary makes the reader hold and anticipate, then revise toward the strongest complete movement.

## Target Skill
Choosing line boundaries by syntax, attention, pace, and temporary meaning.

## Setup
Choose 60–120 words of your own rough poetic prose or a poem draft whose line breaks still feel automatic. Preserve one unlineated copy and do not change its wording during the first comparison.

## Instructions
1. Mark grammatical clauses, natural speech stresses, charged words, images, sound recurrences, and possible breath units on the unlineated copy.
2. Make a syntax-aligned version whose boundaries usually coincide with complete phrases or clauses.
3. Make an enjambed version that crosses selected phrases to create propulsion, expose important ending words, or permit a temporary assertion that later syntax productively revises.
4. Make a third version by asking what each line should contain under attention: one image, tonal movement, rhythmic unit, sound pattern, or deliberate fragment.
5. Read all versions aloud without pausing automatically at every boundary, then again while allowing the page shape to influence expectation and silence.
6. Annotate each version for closure, forward pull, suspension, emphasis, temporary meaning, confusion, and the relation between visible and heard rhythm.
7. Choose the strongest governing arrangement. Only now alter wording, length, indentation, or group boundaries so the language and selected lineation support one another.

## Success Check
- All initial versions use identical language, so lineation is the variable being tested.
- Every retained boundary performs a named syntactic, rhythmic, sonic, imagistic, or attentional job.
- Any temporary meaning is intelligible before continuation and becomes richer rather than merely corrected afterward.
- The final arrangement creates useful variation between closure, propulsion, and suspension.
- Oral and visual readings support the same governing movement or differ for a deliberate reason.

## Common Failures
- Wrapping at the available width and treating the resulting rows as choices.
- Changing the wording in every version so the effect of the boundaries cannot be compared.
- Ending repeatedly on weak function words without a specific reason for exposing them.
- Making every phrase run over because enjambment appears more poetic than closure.
- Selecting the strangest version even though its surprises do not survive completion.

## Notes
Holding language constant reveals what line boundaries do independently of diction. Syntax-aligned endings tend to settle and emphasize complete units; phrase-crossing endings can accelerate, suspend completion, or briefly release a second proposition. The third version prevents the comparison from collapsing into a binary rule by asking what deserves the reader's bounded attention. Wording changes come last because a chosen arrangement may then need different syntax or emphasis to become fully intentional.
