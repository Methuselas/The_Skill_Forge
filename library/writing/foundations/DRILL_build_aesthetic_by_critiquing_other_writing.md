# Build an Aesthetic by Critiquing Other Writing

## Practice Task
Critique another writer's complete piece closely enough to state what you value, why it works or fails for you, and which judgment should transfer to your own writing.

## Target Skill
Developing informed creative judgment by giving sustained, articulate attention to another writer's work.

## Setup
Choose a peer or professional work, including at least one piece whose experience, language, or aesthetic does not immediately resonate with you.

## Instructions
1. Read once for the whole experience before marking problems.
2. Identify the work's apparent aim and the decisions that create its effects.
3. State one quality you want to see in art, using evidence from the piece rather than taste labels alone.
4. Name one reservation and distinguish a craft problem from a difference in background or aesthetic.
5. Return later to an initially unfamiliar piece and test whether a second reading changes the judgment.
6. Transfer one discovered criterion to a current draft of your own.

## Success Check
- The critique is articulate about decisions and effects, not merely positive or negative.
- At least one judgment is informed by close evidence from another person's work.
- An unfamiliar perspective received a genuine second reading.
- The exercise produces a criterion that changes inspection of the writer's own draft.

## Common Failures
- Waiting only for one's own piece to be critiqued and treating attention to peers as a service rather than practice.
- Dominating discussion without making room to hear interpretations that challenge personal taste.
- Dismissing work outside one's experience because it does not resonate immediately.
- Converting aesthetic difference into an unsupported claim of technical failure.

## Notes
Hall Jones's claim is deliberately counterintuitive: workshop participants may grow more while looking closely at another writer's work than while receiving comments on their own. Starkey ties that outward attention to developing an aesthetic and becoming articulate and informed about writing. The second-chance reading extends the drill where differences of life experience initially obstruct perception.
