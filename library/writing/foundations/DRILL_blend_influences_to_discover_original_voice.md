# Blend Influences to Discover Original Voice

## Practice Task
Write through a chosen influence, then recombine what the imitation reveals with contrasting influences until the result no longer depends on one recognizable source.

## Target Skill
Discovering personal style by imitating, comparing, and recombining multiple literary influences.

## Setup
Choose one admired writer with a recognizable approach and two short passages that display the qualities you respond to. Choose a second influence with meaningfully different choices.

## Instructions
1. Write a short piece that imitates the first writer as closely as you can without copying phrases or content.
2. Mark which attempted qualities felt natural, forced, or unexpectedly revealing about your own inclinations.
3. Rewrite the piece through the second influence, changing its sentence movement, structure, stance, imagery, or other governing choices.
4. Make a third version that keeps only the moves that serve your subject, combining them rather than naming either source as the target.
5. Add one decision neither model supplied and revise until the piece has a coherent style of its own.

## Success Check
- The first two versions make the influence legible without copying source language.
- The comparison identifies abilities and inclinations, not only borrowed tricks.
- The final version combines more than one route and contains a choice not dictated by either model.
- A reader can discuss the resulting voice without reducing it to imitation of one author.

## Common Failures
- Avoiding imitation out of fear that influence automatically destroys originality.
- Copying surface mannerisms without discovering which underlying choices fit the writer or subject.
- Stopping at the favorite model instead of putting multiple influences into contact.
- Adding random differences that break coherence rather than creating a new style.

## Notes
Starkey explicitly recommends attempting the best imitation possible and predicts that the attempt will eventually expose its limits. Eugenides supplies the second movement: influence gives hints about ability and inclination, while originality is made by blending influences into a new style and vision. The drill turns that “blender” into successive observable drafts.
