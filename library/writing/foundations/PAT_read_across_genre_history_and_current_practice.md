# Read Across Genre History and Current Practice

## Pattern Rule
**IF** you intend to write seriously in a genre
**THEN** read extensively across its predecessors, recent anthologies, and current journals so craft choices answer both inherited possibilities and living practice

## Do
- Study predecessors for particular moves: meter, plot construction, comedy, sentence style, or the equivalent craft decisions in the target genre.
- Continue beyond assigned readings into complete anthologies and collections.
- Sample current print and online journals to learn what editors are publishing now, including experimental edges of the genre.
- Ask concrete field questions while reading: which voices recur, what forms are changing, and which choices remain durable across periods?
- Treat lifelong reading as part of writing practice rather than preparation that ends once drafting begins.

## Don't
- Do not rely only on canonical historical models and miss the genre's current conversation.
- Do not read only current fashion and lose the long set of alternatives that earlier work preserves.
- Do not confuse accumulating titles with studying what the work does.

## Checklist
- Does the reading set include both predecessors and current publication venues?
- Can each model be connected to a craft decision rather than prestige alone?
- Do you know what has already been attempted in the target form?
- Has recent reading changed or sharpened a choice in the current project?

## Notes
The source joins a thousand years of predecessors to contemporary anthologies, clearinghouses, and sample journal issues. Reading supplies more than admiration: Ovid can teach meter, Homer plot, Aristophanes comedy, and current venues reveal the live range of poems, fiction, essays, and plays. The two time horizons keep a writer from mistaking either tradition or novelty for the whole genre.
