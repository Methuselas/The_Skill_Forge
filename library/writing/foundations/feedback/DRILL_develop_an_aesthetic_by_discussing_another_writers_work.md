# Develop an Aesthetic by Discussing Another Writer's Work

## Practice Task
Closely read one finished piece or peer draft and lead a short discussion of its craft choices, effects, and the qualities you value in it.

## Target Skill
Evidence-based craft evaluation, aesthetic articulation, and balanced workshop participation.

## Setup
Choose one piece that every participant can read closely. Bring brief notes and mark at least three passages or structural choices worth discussing.

## Instructions
1. Describe three specific choices in the language, structure, imagery, characterization, pacing, or another relevant craft dimension.
2. Explain the effect of each choice before stating whether you value it; distinguish observation, inference, and taste.
3. Invite a different reading and connect that response to evidence in the piece. If you tend to stay quiet, contribute at least once; if you tend to dominate, ask a quieter participant to speak and leave room for the answer.
4. Finish by writing two concise statements of aesthetic preference and naming one craft move you want to test in your own work.

## Success Check
- Three specific choices are described from the piece, each locatable by another reader.
- Each effect is stated before any judgment of it, and observation, inference, and taste remain separately labelled.
- A different reading is invited and connected to evidence in the piece, not merely acknowledged as possible.
- The participation adjustment runs in the direction this speaker actually needs, and room left for a quieter participant is real rather than offered.
- The two preference statements name qualities rather than approving of the piece.
- One craft move is named for testing in the writer's own work, which is what separates this from a review.

## Common Failures
- Waiting only for your own work to be critiqued instead of studying what another writer's choices reveal.
- Offering general approval or rejection with no textual or structural evidence.
- Confusing personal taste with an objective defect.
- Disappearing from the discussion or crowding out other readers.

## Notes
Close attention to another writer's work often teaches more than listening defensively while one's own draft is discussed. Speaking about craft makes preferences explicit and turns vague taste into an informed aesthetic. Balanced participation also widens the evidence available to every writer in the group.
