# Keep Writing, Reading, and Talking Through Block

## Pattern Rule
**IF** an idea vanishes at the page or a draft stalls behind a hurdle that feels larger than current experience
**THEN** keep making attempts, read relevant work, and describe the difficulty to another listener until the hurdle becomes specific enough to work

## Do
- Treat block as a common part of creative work rather than proof that the project or writer is defective.
- Continue returning to the page; a failed attempt provides more diagnostic evidence than continued rehearsal in the head.
- Read while blocked to recover possible moves and standards instead of using reading as a permanent substitute for drafting.
- Say what the work is trying to do and why it has become difficult, even when the listener is not a qualified critic.
- Name the hurdle that exceeds present skill or experience so the next attempt can become smaller or more defined.

## Don't
- Do not panic when assignment pressure makes long-held ideas disappear.
- Do not wait for block to end before resuming every activity connected with the work.
- Do not romanticize the obstacle; its value comes from solving a problem that extends capability.

## Checklist
- Has another concrete attempt reached the page?
- Did reading supply a move or comparison relevant to the hurdle?
- Can the difficulty now be stated more precisely than “blocked”?
- Is the next attempt within reach of current skill while still advancing the work?

## Notes
Starkey combines three responses—keep writing, keep reading, keep talking—with Graves's claim that creative blocks contain problems whose solutions produce growth. The key shift is from a fatal condition to a bounded mismatch: the present hurdle is larger than the writer's current means. Continued contact with the page and language around the problem makes that mismatch inspectable.

`VAR_starkey_read_a_voice_resonant_book_during_a_slog` makes the reading route selective rather than broad. It uses a trusted book whose sound aligns with the wanted voice to restart verbal movement; avoid it when the draft needs structural diagnosis or enough contrast to escape imitation.
