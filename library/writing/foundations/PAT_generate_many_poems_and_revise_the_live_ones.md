# Generate Many Poems and Revise the Live Ones

## Pattern Rule
**IF** repeated work on one weak poem is consuming effort without producing a stronger poem, or every new draft is being treated as if it must succeed
**THEN** keep generating poems, discard the weak majority, and concentrate repeated revision on the poems whose language already gives evidence of life
**ELSE** continue revising the promising poem while maintaining a stream of new work.

## Do
- Write consistently enough to compare many attempts instead of judging poetic ability from one draft.
- Allow some poems to remain private and let others disappear without turning every attempt into a public artifact.
- Distinguish time already spent from evidence on the page; a labored poem is not automatically more valuable than one written quickly.
- Select for language, perception, movement, or surprise that remains compelling after the first burst has cooled.
- Rework the better poems repeatedly while continuing to make new ones whenever possible.
- Use the growing set of attempts to sharpen the difference between a poem worth developing and one kept alive by attachment alone.

## Don't
- Do not make effort already invested the reason to continue revising a dead draft.
- Do not stop generating after one worthwhile poem appears.
- Do not show every attempt merely to justify the time spent writing it.
- Do not mistake rapid emergence for immunity from revision.
- Do not revise only old work until selection judgment loses contact with fresh attempts.

## Checklist
- Is there a current stream of new poems as well as selected revisions?
- Can the reason for developing each selected poem be named from its language rather than its labor history?
- Have weak attempts been allowed to remain private or be discarded?
- Has each promising poem received more than one serious revision when needed?
- Is comparison across attempts making selection more discriminating over time?

## Notes
The source joins production volume to judgment. Many attempts will be thrown away, but writing them is not wasted: consistent contact with new poems trains the writer to distinguish the worthwhile work from the bad. Starkey also names a sunk-cost trap—poets may spend more time on a weak poem than on a strong one because the stronger draft arrived with more of its life already present. The retained practice therefore runs two tracks at once: revise the better poems and keep generating beyond them.
