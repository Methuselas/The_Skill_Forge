# Keep Autobiographical Satire Self-Possessed

## Pattern Rule
**IF** a comic first-person account of past exclusion risks reducing the speaker to a victim or making the younger self the joke's only target
**THEN** separate the experiencing and retrospective perspectives, direct the wit at the surrounding value system as well as the self, and end from the narrator's present authority
**ELSE** keep an undivided viewpoint when immediate vulnerability rather than retrospective satire governs the piece.

## Do
- Establish what the younger self believed or felt before the older narrator reinterprets that experience.
- Let the later perspective recover qualities the younger self could not yet recognize without pretending the exclusion never occurred.
- Put social preferences, gender expectations, and family scripts within range of the satire instead of making the speaker absorb every blow.
- Mark another person's judgment as inference when it was conveyed through gesture or silence rather than spoken aloud.
- Give the close a concise rejoinder that lets the present speaker answer the pressure accumulated earlier in the piece.

## Don't
- Do not turn self-directed wit into a sustained argument that the excluded younger self deserved contempt.
- Do not claim self-possession by erasing the cost of the remembered experience.
- Do not report an imagined judgment as certain speech merely because the narrator now understands the scene differently.
- Do not force a victorious ending that the preceding voice and evidence cannot support.

## Checklist
- Can the reader distinguish the younger experiencing self from the later narrating self?
- Does the satire expose a surrounding standard or expectation, not only a personal defect?
- Does the younger self retain dignity even when the narrator sees that self critically?
- Are inferred reactions clearly separated from statements the other person actually made?
- Does the ending answer the earlier pressure from a position the speaker has earned?

## Notes
The introduction to White's model explicitly identifies a refusal to make a victimized female persona the target of her own wit and connects that refusal with authority as a satirist. The poem then makes the claim observable. An adult voice revisits a younger self who did not share the adult's pity, revalues qualities that once carried social cost, imagines a mother's silent concern without converting it into quoted speech, and closes with a brief direct answer. The humor therefore moves among self-perception, social preference, family expectation, and present response rather than collapsing onto the excluded girl alone.
