# Begin Poems From Live Seeds Without Predetermining the End

## Pattern Rule
**IF** beginning a poem from a complete outline or predicted conclusion would leave no room for discovery
**THEN** start from a live idea, phrase, image, or hunch and draft until an unplanned relation or direction emerges before fixing the endpoint
**ELSE** keep only the amount of advance structure needed to enter the work.

## Do
- Choose a seed small enough to generate language without silently dictating a beginning, middle, and end.
- Write past the first expected association so the draft has a chance to reveal something the initial idea did not contain.
- Test an unexpected turn by whether it intensifies the poem's language or perception, not merely by whether it is surprising.
- Preserve a strong discovery through later revision even when the first pass arrived in a quick burst.
- When blank openness stalls the work, enter a form whose requirements create concrete next decisions.

## Don't
- Do not outline the conclusion so tightly that each line only confirms what was already known.
- Do not substitute an overused phrase or formulaic structure for the unexpected movement the draft failed to find.
- Do not wait indefinitely for inspiration when a small seed or chosen constraint can put language in motion.
- Do not confuse openness to discovery with accepting every accidental turn.
- Do not let a form's rhyme or meter drag the poem toward a stock ending merely to complete the scheme.

## Checklist
- Did the draft begin from a workable seed rather than a fully scripted result?
- Did writing reveal at least one relation, statement, or direction that was not known in advance?
- Does the unexpected material strengthen the poem rather than behave as random novelty?
- If a form was used, did its requirements generate choices without predetermining the close?
- Has revision protected the live discovery while removing cliche and formula?

## Notes
Starkey opposes poetic discovery to an overdetermined draft whose conclusion is visible before the first word. The source's practical entry materials are deliberately small: an idea, phrase, image, or hunch. A quick first emergence may still require extensive revision; speed is evidence of contact with live material, not proof of completion.

`VAR_starkey_enter_an_inherited_poetic_form_as_a_field_of_play` changes the entry method when openness supplies too few decisions. The sonnet, villanelle, cinquain, ghazal, pantoum, and other inherited forms turn restriction into a field of play shared with earlier poets. That route offers immediate constraints and precedent, but it demands careful study and practice and becomes a poor fit when the scheme produces formula instead of discovery.
