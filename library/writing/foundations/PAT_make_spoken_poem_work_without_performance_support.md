# Make a Spoken Poem Work Without Performance Support

## Pattern Rule
**IF** a poem intended for live delivery gets most of its force from charisma, instruments, crowd response, or familiar performance cues
**THEN** revise the words until their rhythm, imagery, compression, and freshness still carry the intended effect without those supports
**ELSE** use performance to intensify language that already works.

## Do
- Read the text without music, staging, audience reaction, or vocal embellishment and mark every place where the effect disappears.
- Identify whether the missing force should be carried by sound pattern, image, figurative language, syntax, compression, or a more exact phrase.
- Replace expressions that feel fresh only because the delivery is energetic with language that survives an unperformed reading.
- Preserve rhythms and repetitions that are genuinely encoded in the words rather than supplied only by the performer.
- Rehearse again after revision so vocal choices amplify, rather than conceal, what the language does.

## Don't
- Do not count a ready, rowdy audience as evidence that the poem's language is new.
- Do not let a charismatic delivery smuggle a cliche past the page-level check.
- Do not assume that every poetic song lyric will function as a self-supporting poem after its instruments and automatic cues are removed.
- Do not flatten spoken-word energy merely to imitate poetry designed only for silent reading.

## Checklist
- Can the text produce its central effect when read without accompaniment or performance flourish?
- Are the poem's rhythms and repetitions present in the words themselves?
- Does any phrase depend on crowd excitement to escape sounding familiar?
- Can each performance choice be described as amplification rather than rescue?
- After revision, does live delivery add force without changing weak language into apparently strong language?

## Notes
Starkey distinguishes poetry intended for the page from poetry intended primarily to be said aloud without dismissing either. The warning concerns borrowed support. A songwriter may rely on instruments, formulas, and automatic mood cues, while a charismatic performer can persuade listeners that stale language is fresh. The poem therefore needs a support-removal test: keep the medium's vocal power, but make the words bear the load before the performance returns.
