# Use Dialect and Multilingual Voice as Creative Material

## Pattern Rule
**IF** a writer's community, region, class, race, gender, or language history shapes how the work sounds
**THEN** treat those linguistic markers as possible sources of voice, heritage, audience connection, and creative argument before normalizing them away

## Do
- Identify which words, rhythms, switches, or grammatical patterns carry lived community knowledge.
- Decide what the language choice celebrates, challenges, or makes visible beyond surface flavor.
- Let more than one language or dialect coexist when that contact is part of the work's subject and identity.
- Consider which groups the voice reaches and how it complicates assumptions of a single default language.
- Edit the choice deliberately so a reader can distinguish expressive pattern from accidental inconsistency.

## Don't
- Do not import the expository goal of minimizing every social language marker into creative work without reconsideration.
- Do not flatten a border or community voice merely because it departs from standardized English.
- Do not use multilingual material as decoration detached from speaker, heritage, audience, or purpose.

## Checklist
- Which linguistic features carry identity or community experience?
- What effect would be lost if they were standardized?
- Is switching between languages or dialects purposeful and legible in context?
- Does editing strengthen the voice without erasing its source?

## Notes
Starkey contrasts academic normalization with Anzaldúa's embrace of a Spanish-English borderland dialect. Her language choice celebrates heritage, reaches different groups, and contests the myth of a monocultural United States. The reusable decision is not that every nonstandard form must remain unchanged, but that social language is creative material whose function must be understood before correction.
