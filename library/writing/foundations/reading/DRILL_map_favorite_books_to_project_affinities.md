# Map Favorite Books to Project Affinities

## Practice Task
Compare a personal set of admired books for recurring craft attractions, then convert the strongest affinities into a bounded experiment for a current or proposed project.

## Target Skill
Identifying recurring craft attractions across admired works and translating them into deliberate project choices.

## Setup
Choose eight to ten books you genuinely return to or remember strongly. Include more than one author, and keep the books available for brief verification rather than relying only on reputation or plot memory.

## Instructions
1. For each book, name one or two specific attractions under character, plot, setting, theme, structure, viewpoint, sentence style, or another observable craft dimension.
2. Replace broad praise such as “beautiful” or “gripping” with the choice and effect that earned it; inspect a passage when memory cannot supply evidence.
3. Group attractions that recur across different authors. Separate a shared mechanism from one writer's distinctive phrasing or mannerism.
4. Rank the clusters by durable fascination: which choices would you willingly investigate across a long project rather than merely admire once?
5. Compare the leading clusters with a current project idea. Select one affinity the project already supports and one that would force a useful experiment.
6. Draft 150 to 300 words of new material that tests the experiment on your own subject, characters, and language.
7. Annotate what transferred as a craft function and what remained too close to a model; revise or discard the borrowed surface.

## Success Check
- Every claimed affinity is grounded in an observable choice from at least one book.
- At least one cluster recurs across multiple authors rather than reflecting devotion to a single style.
- The selected experiment serves the current project instead of merely displaying influence.
- New material transforms the craft function through different subject matter and language.
- The exercise identifies both an attraction worth pursuing and a surface feature not to copy.

## Common Failures
- Listing genres or authors without naming what their writing actually does.
- Treating popularity, prestige, or childhood attachment as a craft explanation.
- Generalizing a personal preference into a rule all successful writing must follow.
- Copying syntax, imagery, plot furniture, or verbal mannerisms instead of testing the underlying effect.
- Choosing so many affinities that the practice draft has no governing experiment.

## Notes
Favorite books form an informal curriculum, but their lessons remain latent until admiration is decomposed. Recurrence across different writers helps distinguish a lasting craft attraction from a single charismatic surface. A bounded transfer test then reveals whether the affinity can animate the writer's own material or should remain part of reading taste rather than project design.
