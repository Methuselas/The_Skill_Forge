# Cut a Narrative by Half without Flattening It

## Practice Task
Reduce a substantial narrative to half its original word count while keeping the action intelligible, its sensory force specific, and its governing movement intact.

## Target Skill
Use severe reversible compression to expose indispensable narrative material without replacing specific evidence with summary.

## Setup
Choose a complete narrative passage between roughly 400 and 1,000 words. Preserve the original as a separate recoverable version. Record its exact word count, state the governing action or relation in one sentence, and mark three to five concrete details whose loss would materially weaken the reader's experience.

## Instructions
1. Set a target equal to half the original word count. Treat the number as a diagnostic constraint rather than a publication requirement.
2. Cut preliminary explanation before the governing action becomes active, repeated information, throat-clearing transitions, and commentary that arrives after the scene has made its point.
3. Halve any long speech or conversation. Preserve turns that change knowledge, desire, relation, or action; remove greetings, restatement, and exchanges whose absence changes nothing.
4. Compress repeated actions and descriptions, but do not replace precise sensory or causal evidence with a vague category such as somehow, things happened, or they argued.
5. Test every favorite sentence that leaves the main trajectory. Remove it temporarily even when its language is strong, then join the surrounding passages directly and read across the seam.
6. Count again. Continue until the target is reached without shrinking type, disguising clauses as fragments, or deleting words that grammar and orientation require.
7. Compare the complete and compressed versions for narrative clarity, causal continuity, sensory impact, pace, voice, and emotional pressure.
8. Restore material only when the comparison identifies a specific loss that the shorter version cannot otherwise repair. Keep the severe cut as evidence even if the final working draft settles at another length.

## Success Check
- The compressed version reaches half the original word count.
- Readers can still identify what happens, why each consequential turn follows, and where the narrative ends.
- Concrete actions, objects, sensations, or spoken turns carry the experience instead of generic substitutes.
- At least one passage once considered indispensable was tested by removal rather than protected automatically.
- Every restoration answers a named loss in clarity, causality, sensory force, voice, or emotional pressure.
- The exercise produces a defensible revision decision rather than a blanket preference for shorter prose.

## Common Failures
- Cutting small words everywhere while leaving whole expendable scenes, explanations, or exchanges intact.
- Replacing vivid particulars with abstract summary merely to satisfy the count.
- Removing causal links and calling the resulting confusion a faster pace.
- Protecting polished sentences whose removal lets the narrative join cleanly.
- Treating the half-length version as automatically superior because it obeys the constraint.
- Failing to preserve the original, which turns diagnosis into an irreversible wager.

## Notes
Extreme compression creates information ordinary trimming cannot. A mild cut allows every passage to argue that it is almost necessary; a fixed half-length target forces direct comparison among actions, explanations, images, and verbal pleasures. The exercise succeeds when it reveals where the narrative's weight actually lives. Its result may be the shorter version, a selective restoration, or a return to the original with clearer reasons for what remains.
