# Generate Working Titles from Story Elements

## Practice Task
Create a broad title set for one developing creative work, compare the attention each candidate directs, and select a temporary title with a scheduled review point.

## Target Skill
Generating and selecting temporary titles by the attention and expectation they create.

## Setup
Choose a story, poem, essay, script, or other creative project with at least a premise, fragment, or partial draft. Prepare short notes naming its central people, relations, locations, themes, objects, actions, and distinctive phrases.

## Instructions
1. Write at least two title candidates from each applicable source: character, relationship, place, theme, object, governing action, consequential phrase, and transformed allusion.
2. Continue through weak or obvious candidates without editing the list until at least four source categories have produced options.
3. Select six meaningfully different candidates. Beside each, write one sentence completing: “This title makes the work seem chiefly about . . .”
4. Mark any candidate that promises the wrong genre, reveals an unintended secret, overweights a minor element, or depends on another work's identity.
5. Rank the remaining candidates for current usefulness, not final brilliance: retrieval, discussability, fit with the live drafting question, and room for discovery.
6. Choose one and label it as the working title. Set a review point after the next major structural discovery or complete draft.
7. At the review point, repeat the attention sentence for the work as it now exists and keep, revise, or replace the title accordingly.

## Success Check
- Candidates arise from at least four different elements rather than variations on one phrase.
- The comparison identifies the emphasis and expectation created by each finalist.
- Misleading, overnarrow, derivative, or prematurely revealing options are visible.
- The selected title helps identify the project without pretending to be final.
- A concrete review point prevents familiarity from deciding the final choice automatically.

## Common Failures
- Polishing the first attractive phrase instead of generating a contrasting pool.
- Judging only sound or cleverness while ignoring what the title frames.
- Rejecting every option because the finished work is not yet known.
- Treating a useful temporary label as a promise that the draft may not outgrow.
- Changing the title repeatedly without any corresponding discovery in the work.

## Notes
Generating by source category prevents one early interpretation from monopolizing the title search. The attention sentence turns vague preference into a framing comparison, and the scheduled revisit keeps temporary language from becoming permanent through repetition alone. The exercise can be repeated late in revision with stricter standards for distinctiveness and reader promise.
