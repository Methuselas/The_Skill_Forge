# Choose Diction to Serve Purpose and Tone

## Pattern Rule
**IF** a draft's vocabulary feels plainer, more elevated, more antiquated, or more conversational than its intended effect
**THEN** set the level of diction deliberately and use departures from that level only when their contrast performs a specific tonal or rhetorical job
**ELSE** preserve the current register while checking that conspicuous words belong to it by design

## Do
- Compare an unusual candidate word with the matter-of-fact language around it; one elevated word can create more tonal pressure than a passage crowded with them.
- Match elaborate phrasing to a genuinely intricate movement of thought, and simplify the sentence when its structure displays refinement without clarifying or intensifying the subject.
- Test old-fashioned vocabulary against the work's present voice, setting, and audience before allowing its historical flavor to govern the passage.
- Begin with concrete, direct wording when control is uncertain, then raise the style only as far as the intended argument, description, or emotional effect requires.

## Don't
- Treat elevated vocabulary as inherently more literary than accurate ordinary speech.
- Scatter archaic words through a contemporary voice merely because they look poetic in isolation.
- Distort syntax to make a high style sound impressive; involution without control reads as strain.
- Assume that a shared level of diction gives every writer the same voice; selection and contrast still carry individual habits of thought.

## Checklist
- The baseline register can be described as plain, elevated, conversational, time-marked, or another intentional level.
- Every conspicuous word changes tone, emphasis, argument, or description in a way the surrounding language supports.
- Complex diction remains intelligible because the sentence carrying it is controlled.
- Archaic or specialized vocabulary has a present purpose beyond sounding literary.
- Replacing an elevated word with a simpler one would remove a wanted effect rather than merely reduce ornament.

## Notes
Diction is the selection of words; tone is the manner of expression their selection helps create. A mostly plain passage can make one elevated word unusually forceful because the contrast changes how the voice is heard. Sustained high style is not forbidden, but it asks for command of both vocabulary and sentence structure. Historical language carries the flavor of its era, so accidental archaism can displace a contemporary work into an unintended voice.
