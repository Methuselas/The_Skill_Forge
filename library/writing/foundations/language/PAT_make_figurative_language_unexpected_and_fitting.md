# Make Figurative Language Unexpected and Fitting

## Pattern Rule
**IF** literal wording is flat or a passage needs a comparison, substitution, or wordplay to create a special effect
**THEN** build a relation that is surprising enough to renew attention and apt enough to reveal the subject more precisely
**ELSE** prefer direct language to a figure that adds only decoration or cleverness

## Do
- Separate the subject from the comparison term and state what credible qualities form the bridge between them before writing the sentence or line.
- Search beyond the first familiar likeness, then keep the candidate whose differences create useful energy without breaking the underlying fit.
- Make verbs, modifiers, and nearby actions participate in the comparison's operative quality so the figure changes the whole sentence rather than arriving as an attached ornament.
- Test whether the figure belongs to this observer's knowledge, emotion, and immediate pressure; a fitting likeness can reveal the perceiver alongside the subject.
- Use one productive metaphor or simile as an organizing center when its implications can generate and order several parts of the work.
- Audit association-based substitutions for inherited shorthand; metonymy and part-for-whole language lose force when convention supplies the entire connection.
- Moderate personification and punning by asking whether the human attribute or double meaning deepens the work rather than merely displaying technique.

## Don't
- Use an overfamiliar vehicle whose meaning arrives before the reader has to imagine anything.
- Pair a fresh comparison with generic surrounding diction that leaves its implications inactive.
- Assume that naming a figure of speech makes language effective when the relationship itself is predictable or inaccurate.
- Award human behavior to an object until the result becomes unintentionally ridiculous.
- Keep a pun whose comic pressure conflicts with the surrounding tone or whose second meaning contributes nothing beyond the joke.

## Checklist
- The relation between subject and figurative term can be explained without relying on a stock phrase.
- The connection is unexpected yet remains appropriate to the work's context and tone.
- The figure reveals, complicates, or organizes the subject rather than merely renaming it.
- Conventional associations, human attributes, and wordplay have been tested for cliche or accidental absurdity.
- Removing the figure would subtract a specific insight or structural function.

## Notes
A metaphor applies one term to another; a simile signals the likeness explicitly. Both succeed through the bridge between the things, not through the labels used to classify them. Their strongest use often extends into grammar: the chosen verb, motion, or texture activates the same relation and lets the comparison carry situation as well as appearance. Other figures shift the mechanism - association in metonymy, part for whole in synecdoche, human qualities in personification, multiple meanings in a pun - but they share the same burden: the departure from ordinary language must produce a controlled effect worth the risk of familiarity, excess, or tonal drift.
