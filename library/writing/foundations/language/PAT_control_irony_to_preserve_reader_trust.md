# Control Irony to Preserve Reader Trust

## Pattern Rule
**IF** wording or a situation creates an incongruity between appearance and intended meaning or outcome
**THEN** make that gap deliberate and supply enough contextual pressure for intended readers to recognize the controlling stance
**ELSE** revise the contradiction or absurdity before it is mistaken for carelessness

## Do
- Identify whether the effect depends on a speaker saying one thing while meaning another or on events violating a reasonable expectation.
- State the literal reading a reader could take, then locate the contextual fact, tonal cue, or consequence that redirects it.
- Test phrasing whose unintended secondary meaning could make the voice appear naive, contradictory, or ridiculous.
- Calibrate how much guidance the intended audience needs; recognition should be possible without converting the incongruity into an explicit explanation.
- Preserve assurance in the surrounding language so the departure reads as controlled rather than as general confusion.

## Don't
- Assume that readers share the private knowledge that makes an ironic statement obvious to the writer.
- Leave an accidental contradiction in place because irony is common enough to provide a possible excuse.
- rely on irony when the literal reading would cause consequential misunderstanding and the context supplies no reliable correction.
- Explain the intended reversal so completely that the tension between appearance and reality disappears.

## Checklist
- The apparent meaning or expected outcome is clear enough for the incongruity to register.
- Available context supports the intended alternate meaning without requiring private explanation.
- Potential absurdities and unintended readings have been identified rather than discovered by accident after publication.
- The tone shows command of the gap instead of uncertainty about what the work means.
- A reasonable reader can distinguish deliberate irony from error while still doing some interpretive work.

## Notes
Verbal irony turns on a difference between what is said and what is meant; situational irony turns on a difference between what reasonably should happen and what does. Both depend on control. If readers cannot detect the intended gap, they do not experience irony - they experience a writer who seems unaware of the work's implications.
