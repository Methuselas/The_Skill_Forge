# 2. The actual boundary of Chapter 4

The chapter is about preserving the **size identity** of foreshortened forms whose apparent lengths change or disappear in depth.

Its source spine is:

```text
reduce body members to cylinders and barrels
→ use width or diameter as a constant factor
→ infer blocked members from visible evidence
→ apply the same logic to arms and hands
→ use joint interlock to declare the advancing member
```

The chapter sections are:

- **Cylindrical and Barrel Forms** — printed p. 105;
- **The Cylinder as a Rational Form** — printed p. 105;
- **Finding Constant Factors** — printed p. 107;
- **Width of Form as a Constant Factor** — printed p. 107;
- **The Arms** — printed p. 115;
- **The Hands** — printed p. 120;
- **The Joints** — printed p. 127.

Printed p. 134 introduces equal radii and ellipses as the bridge into Chapter 5’s problem of controlling length. Length control itself belongs to Chapter 5.

Chapter 4 is not primarily a lesson in:

- finished anatomy;
- lighting or rendering;
- exact universal human proportions;
- copying a reference image;
- literal Photoshop layer mechanics;
- proving that an image generator can preserve registration.

Those production questions entered because the chapter’s simple-form construction was tested against the project’s four-stage workflow.

# 3. The cylinder is a rational Stage 2 carrier

Hogarth begins with the cylinder because it makes a difficult spatial problem explicit.

From a side view it presents long sides and shallow end curves. As it turns toward the eye:

- the circular ends project as fuller curves;
- the visible side length decreases;
- an extreme end-on view can approach a double curve with almost no side wall.

The thigh example on printed p. 106 is decisive for the project workflow:

```text
A = simple cylinder proving direction and depth
B = organic leg developed over that proof
```

The user identified A as Stage 2 and B as Stage 3. The dark construction is not decoration. It demonstrates that the simple mass correctly carries the later form.

Practical consequence:

> If Stage 3 anatomy cannot fit the Stage 2 cylinder, return and correct Stage 2 every time.

Rendering cannot repair a wrong construction.

# 4. Constant width: the source proposition and its guided boundary

Hogarth’s central proposition is that similar widths produce similar forms even when projected lengths vary.

The useful reading is not “every limb is a uniform pipe.” It is:

- compare corresponding regions;
- preserve the designed diameter at those regions;
- keep the intended taper;
- allow projected length to compress, extend, or disappear;
- use width as evidence that the member remains the same form.

The best comparison region is usually a stable mid-shank rather than a swollen knee, elbow, or knuckle.

## Stage application

The user placed this control primarily in Stage 2. It may continue into Stages 3 and 4 as a check, but the decisive work occurs while the simple masses are still exposed.

## Figure-specific, not universal

No two humans are identical. A tall ectomorphic figure and a compact muscular figure do not share one set of widths. Horses, cats, invented creatures, and machines have their own proportional systems.

Correct rule:

```text
preserve the designed widths within this figure
or among figures intentionally built to the same proportions
```

Incorrect rule:

```text
force every figure to match one canonical diameter chart
```

This correction prevents a useful depth control from becoming a body-standard law.

# 5. Construction lines are optional evidence

Hogarth uses diameter lines, ellipses, arrows, and dark cylinder contours to prove the form.

The user corrected any compulsory reading:

- use a guide when it helps;
- omit it when the eye can judge the form accurately;
- if the result looks wrong, restore the guide;
- let the guide recede when later form carries the same information.

For assistant-generated work, temporary Stage 2 guides remain a sensible default because the system does not possess human binocular depth perception. They are an accommodation, not a universal artistic rule.

The same principle applies to the numbered curves on printed p. 124. They are not width-measuring paths. They demonstrate the characteristic and more limited-than-expected range of finger movement, while the repeated finger widths preserve identity.

# 6. Rational inference: visible evidence can carry the hidden whole

Chapter 4 repeatedly solves blocked limbs by using visible comparatives:

- a visible thigh establishes the hidden thigh’s size;
- knees organize the relation between upper and lower legs;
- calves, ankles, heels, and feet support the inference;
- visible forearms and hands imply blocked upper arms;
- exposed finger fragments imply the hidden finger chain.

The user converted this into a production boundary:

> Construct the minimum hidden route required by visible dependencies.

## When the full hidden route is necessary

Draw through the complete chain when:

- the endpoint cannot be placed without it;
- the hidden bend determines where a joint or fragment emerges;
- another visible form follows the obstruction and must align;
- attachment, balance, overlap, or action depends on the concealed route;
- the evidence is ambiguous.

## When visible evidence is sufficient

Stop without inventing the rest when:

- the visible endpoint or fragment has correct size and orientation;
- it implies the missing member convincingly;
- no later visible form depends on the exact route;
- the unseen continuation would not change any visible decision.

Printed p. 114 is the clearest boundary: when only a correct visible thigh matters and no follow-up form must be explained, there is no reason to invent the rest of the leg.

The minimum may be one fragment or an entire limb. “Minimum” is determined by dependency, not by line count.

# 7. Arms follow the same size logic

Printed pp. 115–119 carry the leg solution into the arms.

Corresponding upper-arm and forearm widths remain the reliable evidence while apparent lengths change through extension, flexion, advancement, and recession.

Blocked arms can be inferred when adjacent visible forms are explicit enough:

- shoulder or upper-arm evidence;
- forearm size and direction;
- hand compatibility;
- grip relation;
- a deliberately matched partner figure.

The guided correction about body variation still applies. Cross-figure comparison is valid only when the figures are intentionally comparable.

---

# 8. Hands: graduated width and deferred depth

Hogarth applies the same method to the hand:

- palm thickness graduates from the heavier thumb/index side toward the little-finger side;
- finger widths graduate accordingly;
- the rods and knuckle capsules turn through depth;
- rounder end curves indicate deeper, more frontal foreshortening;
- flatter ellipses indicate a more extended view;
- visible fragments can imply blocked fingers.

The user treated this as a broad foundational principle but deferred full hand expertise to *Drawing Dynamic Hands*.

The Chapter 4 addition to the existing hand card is therefore narrow:

- judge stable width at the circular mid-shank rather than the knuckle;
- preserve the thickness order through obstruction;
- use end-curve roundness as depth evidence;
- infer the rest only when the visible fragments are sufficient.

Hands and feet remain practice priorities. Hiding them habitually because they are difficult does not build the skill.

# 9. Joint interlock: a visual depth decision

Hogarth’s rule is:

```text
the advancing member gets the joint
```

The user’s correction is essential:

- this is a contour and overlap decision;
- it is not an anatomical transfer;
- the knee, elbow, or knuckle remains part of one continuous chain;
- the whole body remains one connected system.

When the joint is visually attached to the wrong member, the bend can appear:

- swollen;
- cracked;
- overridden by the rear form;
- detached;
- spatially contradictory.

The diagnostic question is:

> Which member is actually advancing, and does the decisive joint contour interlock with it?

This belongs in Stage 2 because the depth relation should be decided before anatomy develops around it.

# 10. Stage 2 is the principal lock point

The guided review placed Chapter 4 inside the project’s larger workflow.

Stage 2 is where the whole composition first exists as readable three-dimensional mass. It is where major problems become visible:

- proportion;
- balance and support;
- width and taper;
- attachment;
- overlap and recession;
- hidden-path logic;
- joint interlock;
- relation of hands and feet;
- spatial coherence of the composition.

The user’s production rule is:

> Put serious effort into making Stage 2 work out of the gate.

## When one region fails

Preferred repair:

```text
erase the failed region
→ restate a quick local Stage 1 path
→ rebuild Stage 2 over that region
→ keep every unaffected object unchanged
```

This local rollback will not always integrate.

## When the composition itself fails

Return to Stage 1 and start the drawing again. That cost is preferable to carrying a bad block into anatomy and finish.

Once Stage 2 is accepted, it becomes the controlling reference for Stages 3 and 4.

# 11. Thumbnail, Stage 1, and the registration boundary

The guided test clarified that the thumbnail is an idea probe, not the first locked layer.

A thumbnail may test:

- story and action;
- dominant flow;
- focal placement;
- rough arrangement;
- positive and negative shapes;
- crop and depth hierarchy.

It need not line up exactly with the finished work. Stage 1 may reinterpret and improve it.

The continuity requirements tighten in stages:

```text
thumbnail → Stage 1:
interpretation and adjustment expected

Stage 1 → Stage 2:
limited structural correction allowed

Stage 2 → Stage 3 → Stage 4:
basic forms and composition remain registered
```

Stage 1 should remain a framework:

- action and centerline;
- simple chest and pelvis landmarks;
- limb paths;
- circles for major joints when useful;
- simple hand and foot wedges;
- no developed anatomy or mass rendering.

The image tests repeatedly over-refined the thumbnail and Stage 1. That is a stage-boundary failure, not a proof that the construction knowledge was absent.

# 12. “Ducks in a row”: same drawing at increasing resolution

The user’s staged martial artist, dragon, building, and alien examples established the intended model:

> Each stage is the same drawing at a different information level.

The protected relationships include:

- action line;
- joint centers;
- feet and hands;
- major masses;
- attachments;
- overlaps;
- perspective;
- camera;
- surrounding composition.

A Stage 1 correction may be carried into Stage 2. After Stage 2 locks, later stages should make the figure tighter, less blocky, more organic, and more finished without redesigning it.

In a larger composition, a local arm correction must not move teammates, buildings, props, spacing, or camera relationships.

The prior-stage image is the visual Wu Sao: the control reference that prevents the next pass from fighting the established structure.

# 15. The portability failure: retrieval is not registration

The decisive failure occurred when the accepted foreshortened image was supposed to be simplified into Stage 0.

Instead of editing the exact image in place, the system generated a new interpretation:

- camera and pose changed;
- proportions changed;
- crop and silhouette changed;
- the advancing fist and body relation changed;
- the result could not be overlaid on the accepted image.

This violated the onion-skin card even though the card had been retrieved.

The honest conclusion is:

```text
SkillForge controls what knowledge enters context.
It does not guarantee that the image runtime performs a layer-preserving edit.
```

Portable knowledge and portable behavior are different problems.

The training performed reliably inside a long, tightly corrected conversation because the local teaching context itself acted as a strong behavioral state. The library can preserve the rules, examples, and corrections, but the current hosted image workflow may still reinterpret an edit as a fresh generation.

This is a capability boundary, not a reason to pretend success.

# 16. New fail-closed visual continuity rule

For any future staged visual task:

1. Load the relevant SkillForge cards.
2. Identify the exact accepted image that controls the next pass.
3. Verify that the runtime can use that image as the active edit target.
4. Request one constrained stage change only.
5. Compare camera, crop, silhouette, joints, attachments, masses, and unaffected objects.
6. Reject any output that drifts; do not relabel it as the requested stage.
7. If exact image-to-image control cannot be verified, stop and disclose the limit rather than generate a fresh interpretation.

The phrase “draw over it” is not enough. The workflow needs an enforceable image target and a registration check.

A future `.skillforge/` centralization may improve adapter maintenance, but it will not by itself solve image registration. Discovery and execution are separate layers.

# 18. Failure modes to preserve

## Cylinder failures

- Drawing a nearly circular end while retaining long side walls.
- Treating the simple cylinder as finished anatomy.
- Altering the Stage 2 mass so Stage 3 can look better.
- Adding guides as compulsory decoration rather than diagnostic evidence.

## Width failures

- Using apparent length as the size norm in deep foreshortening.
- Measuring swollen joints instead of stable shanks.
- Making a tapered limb uniformly thick.
- Forcing unrelated figures or species into one width system.

## Hidden-form failures

- Drawing the entire hidden limb when no visible decision requires it.
- Refusing to draw through when an endpoint or emerging fragment depends on the hidden bend.
- Letting visible fragments imply incompatible hidden chains.
- Exposing construction after its explanatory purpose ends.

## Hand failures

- Using knuckle width as the stable finger measure.
- Flattening all fingers into equal tubes.
- Hiding difficult hands instead of practising them.
- Treating Chapter 4 as a complete hand-anatomy system.

## Joint failures

- Attaching the joint overlap to the receding member.
- Treating the joint as a detachable bead.
- Reading “gets the joint” as anatomical ownership.
- Accepting swelling or cracking as style rather than diagnosing depth.

## Stage failures

- Calling a Stage 2 mass study a thumbnail.
- Turning Stage 1 into an anatomical drawing.
- Letting Stage 3 relocate the accepted Stage 2 forms.
- Using rendering to conceal a wrong block.
- Moving unrelated objects during a local correction.
- generating several stages independently in one panel and expecting registration.

## Tooling and portability failures

- Claiming that loading a skill card guarantees that the image tool followed it.
- Starting from the verbal prompt instead of the accepted image.
- Treating a new interpretation as an overlay.
- Continuing to test an unverified layer workflow after drift is already clear.
- Blaming the library for a runtime registration failure without distinguishing the two.

# 22. Post-Chapter visual validation addendum

## Approval-gated production

Subsequent Warbot and zero-gravity astronaut tests established a more reliable default production route:

```text
Stage 0 only
→ user approval or revision
→ registered Stage 1–4 walkthrough
→ user approval or structural correction
→ standalone Stage 4 render
→ drift inspection
```

Do not invest downstream work before the upstream image is approved. A rejection at Stage 0 should produce another cheap picture idea, not a polished defense of the wrong composition.

## Precedent calibration

Before and after generating each stage, compare it with the approved same-stage process images in the repository and with the next stage as the forbidden ceiling. Compare purpose, information class, density, and degree of commitment—not subject matter. If the result resembles the next stage more closely than its own, it is over-rendered and must be simplified or rebuilt.

Stage 2 remains the most persistent weakness. The Warbot and astronaut process sheets both solved their figures well but borrowed anatomy, mechanical detail, contour polish, and environmental rendering from Stage 3. Preserve these sheets as cautionary cases, not positive Stage 2 density targets.

## Global drift and local drift

The staged workflow greatly reduced whole-picture reinterpretation but did not eliminate drift:

- **Global drift** changes camera, framing, crop, subject scale, major silhouette, action, placement, or depth order. It changes the picture and should be rejected.
- **Local drift** damages one chain or endpoint while the approved picture survives. It may be repaired locally when the correction can preserve the whole.

The astronaut final is the key example: its cinematic amplification was stronger than the process-sheet preview, but the left leg became corkscrewed across the hip → knee → ankle → foot chain. The Warbot examples exposed framing drift and a firing effect that did not follow the muzzle axis.

## Stage 4 controlled amplification

The final rule is not “change nothing.” It is:

> Preserve every approved structural relationship while allowing controlled presentational amplification.

Stage 4 may intensify lighting, color, material, texture, atmosphere, depth cues, edge control, and focal impact. It may not invent a joint rotation, alter endpoint function, change prop contacts, move the camera, reframe the subject without approval, or bend a straight beam/projectile/tether away from its emitter or attachment axis.

When asked for a final Stage 4 render, create a full standalone render from the approved Stage 3 and Stage 4 preview. Do not substitute a crop unless the request is explicitly to extract the existing panel.
