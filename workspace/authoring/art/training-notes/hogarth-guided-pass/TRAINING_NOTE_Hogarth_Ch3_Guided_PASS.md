# 3. Chapter 3’s central problem

Deep foreshortening produces a visual paradox.

To make a figure recede, one mass must pass in front of another. Yet every successful overlap interrupts a contour and can make the body look like separate pieces:

```text
thigh / knee / calf
arm / elbow / forearm
pelvis / waist / rib cage
head / neck / shoulders
```

Hogarth describes the failure as an assemblage of adjacent parts, like beads on a string.

The practical task is therefore not merely to create depth. It is to create depth **without losing the unity of the object that occupies that depth**.

The user broadened the principle correctly:

- organic objects have curves and flows that cannot be ignored;
- machines and designed objects also have interconnecting forms;
- functional structure can determine a valid connection;
- structure is the route, but believability is the actual goal.

A machine does not need anatomical tendons. It does need joints, housings, load paths, seams, overlapping shells, or functional transitions that make its parts belong to one constructed object.

---

# 4. Overlap: the partial form carries the whole

Hogarth’s opening proposition is that the visible part of an obscured form is understood as the whole form continuing behind the one in front.

The user confirmed the practical reading:

A visible fragment must preserve enough of its:

- identity;
- direction;
- volume;
- attachment;

for the viewer to complete the hidden portion mentally.

The final drawing does not need to expose the whole hidden member. It must prevent the visible remainder from looking amputated, pasted on, or unrelated.

The opposite condition is equally important. When all forms are presented completely and none interpose, they tend to sit at the same depth on the picture plane. Outline by itself does not create the same sequence of recession.

This principle belongs primarily to the Stage 2 block because the near-to-far order and major overlaps should exist before specific anatomy is added.

However, it is a guide rather than an “or else.” Other depth cues can support a relationship. The card exists because overlap is Hogarth’s governing device in this chapter, not because every deep-space image must use the maximum possible occlusion.

---

# 5. Three means of restoring unity

Hogarth proposes three means of holding overlapped forms together:

1. **Interconnection lines** at joints, skin tensions, tendon insertions, and muscle interlaces.
2. **Strong end-of-form outlines and terminal contours** that consolidate the overlap-separated figure.
3. **Continual tone gradation** that fuses the forms into a total mass.

The crucial guided correction is:

> These are controls, not three compulsory finishes.

Do not mechanically maximize all three on every figure.

Use only as much of each as the image needs. When a passage requires more development in Stage 3, render it. When it already reads, leave it alone. Experience determines the difference.

The source presents an instructional progression. The production workflow remains need-driven.

# 6. Interconnection lines: what they are and what they are not

Hogarth begins with obvious connective evidence:

- channels between knuckle mounds;
- finger insertion lines moving from outside contour into inner form;
- foot wrinkles and tendon routes;
- Achilles and hamstring connections;
- torso interlaces;
- knee, elbow, shoulder, and neck transitions.

A useful interconnection may:

```text
begin on an outside contour
→ turn into an inside passage
→ enter the next form
→ let the eye continue through the overlap
```

The user confirmed that the connection must belong to the actual body or object.

Correct priority:

```text
correct structure or anatomy
→ valid interconnection
→ depth and unity become apparent
```

Incorrect priority:

```text
invent dramatic connecting line
→ hope anatomy and depth become believable
```

No amount of depth effect rescues a line that contradicts the structure.

## Long pathways are examples, not formulas

Hogarth traces long routes through torso, leg, arm, neck, and head. These routes should not be memorized as universal maps.

Not all bodies are the same. The visible path changes with:

- build and proportion;
- pose and tension;
- anatomical variation;
- camera position;
- compression and foreshortening;
- which structures are actually exposed;
- the artist’s line economy and style.

The correct study sequence is:

```text
understand the actual structure
→ find a continuous route through the present pose and view
→ emphasize only the portions the image needs
```

Different body references will be reviewed after the book. The later study must test these routes rather than preserving Hogarth’s exact line diagrams as doctrine.

## Teaching marks versus production marks

Hogarth’s arrows and emphatic lines are teaching devices. A finished drawing does not need to display every instructional route literally.

The production image may preserve the connection through:

- a hard line;
- a light line;
- a broken line;
- a contour turn;
- a value edge;
- a plane change;
- an implied continuation.

The information must survive. The pedagogical markup does not.

# 7. Camera position changes the curves, not the body

The guided review corrected an important misunderstanding.

Overcurve and undercurve are not permanent labels attached to a body part. They describe how an unchanged three-dimensional form appears from a particular camera position.

Correct model:

```text
natural three-dimensional form remains the same
→ eye/camera moves in space
→ visible surfaces and projected curves change
```

The paper or canvas plays no causal role. It records the view.

A curve is not “upward” because it travels toward the top edge of the page. It belongs to the natural form seen through the camera.

Hogarth’s high- and low-angle torso studies demonstrate:

- which mass becomes dominant because it is nearer;
- which mass recedes;
- where compression occurs;
- how overcurve sequences can become undercurve sequences;
- how the same anatomy projects differently without becoming different anatomy.

This distinction must be protected during future study. Page direction is a graphic consequence; spatial form and camera relation are the cause.

# 8. The human figure is one connected system

The strongest user synthesis of the chapter is:

> The entire human figure is one connected system.

Hogarth’s local examples are demonstrations of that total fact.

## Knee

The knee is not a connector inserted between two limb cylinders.

It is one region of the leg where:

- the patella and condyles articulate;
- tendons latch upper and lower members;
- thigh, shin, and calf masses reorganize;
- surface flow changes direction;
- foreshortening may expose one member and hide another.

The knee receives and redirects the leg. The leg does not stop and restart there.

## Hip and pelvis

The same principle governs the leg’s attachment to the pelvis.

The thigh belongs to a continuous hip, groin, buttock, and pelvic system. A socket symbol alone cannot carry this unity.

## Deltoid and shoulder

The deltoid belongs visually to both torso and arm.

Depending on the view, it can continue into:

- trapezius and neck;
- scapular ridge and shoulder blade;
- chest and clavicle;
- triceps and elbow;
- underarm and axilla.

The shoulder should not read as a socket with an arm pasted into it.

## Elbow

The elbow is a bony mediator around which upper- and lower-arm masses interlace. It redirects the chain without becoming an isolated hinge block.

## Head, neck, shoulders, and torso

Hogarth presents several mediating complexes:

- sternomastoid from side head to chest;
- trapezius from rear torso to skull base;
- neck funnel from underjaw to neck pit;
- implied spine through the rear neck.

These are not separate attachments added around a neck cylinder. Together they make head, neck, shoulders, and torso one system. The camera decides which routes are visible, compressed, hidden, or implied.

The spine line may be absent from the finished drawing while its structural presence still authenticates the relationship.

# 9. When interconnection becomes too much

The transition on printed pages 95–100 is critical.

Hogarth warns that intensive probing of interlocking details can become a preoccupation and impair the overall concept.

The user confirmed the practical lesson:

> Once the local connections have established the flow, suppress whatever interferes with seeing the figure as one total mass.

This is not permission to add more and more connective information.

Correct progression:

```text
local overlap creates separation
→ necessary interconnection establishes flow
→ excessive probing is reduced
→ major contour coordinates the whole
```

This is one of the clearest Chapter 3 boundaries. The purpose of interconnection is unity. When the marks begin producing fragmentation, the method has defeated its own purpose.

# 10. Coordinating contour

Hogarth’s “firm, end-of-form controlling contour” is not an inventory of every anatomical bump.

It is the selected outer flow that lets the eye grasp the total figure across:

- contrary directions;
- large overlaps;
- compressed masses;
- internal complexity.

Minor forms and edges may be:

- subordinated;
- diffused;
- broken;
- absorbed into tone;
- omitted when another signal carries them.

Broken contour does not automatically mean broken form. Continuity can remain perceptual rather than literal.

The user’s broader contour teaching must be retained:

- contour control begins in Stage 3 and is finalized in Stage 4;
- line weight may communicate depth, light, shadow, tension, and rest;
- heavier and thinner passages are contextual, not fixed recipes;
- light, shadow, color, or an adjacent shape may carry an edge;
- every object deserves believable edge logic, though skin, metal, cloth, architecture, smoke, and other materials will not receive identical treatment.

This broader line-weight doctrine is not all stated by Hogarth in Chapter 3. It is first-party production teaching that grew from the contour discussion.

# 11. Tone gradation: source lesson and production correction

Hogarth’s final cohesion device allows tone to dominate line and close the figure into a mass.

The source uses tone to express:

- total mass appearance;
- atmosphere;
- texture;
- density;
- weight;
- near-to-far progression;
- form emerging from or dissolving into surrounding value.

Some plates reduce line almost completely.

## Source-specific qualification

On printed p. 103, Hogarth explicitly says the tones are conceived as sculptural “light and dark” for mass, structure, and density rather than literal illumination.

On printed p. 104, he describes one dark-light stress as compositional and arbitrary.

These statements must not be silently rewritten as physically correct lighting instruction. They are the source’s stated method for those plates.

## First-party production correction

The user’s production rule is stronger:

> Light should always govern tone.

In an actual rendered image:

```text
lighting design
→ tonal structure
→ turning form
→ volume, depth, weight, and unity
```

Do not darken arbitrarily merely to push a form back. Hogarth’s sculptural or compositional value studies can teach mass unity, but they are not universal lighting prescriptions.

Tone may:

- absorb a construction line;
- hide a true contour in light;
- let light act as the line;
- carry a plane boundary without stroke;
- make explicit line secondary.

It must not conceal an unresolved attachment or contradictory construction.

This distinction should remain visible in the Pattern and memcap. The source lesson is retained; production use is corrected by the user’s lighting doctrine.

# 12. Stage application: restraint, need, and commitment

The user’s stage correction is more nuanced than “never render in Stage 3.”

## Stage 2

- Use light construction lines.
- Establish a coherent near-to-far overlap sequence.
- Settle the major masses, attachments, balance, support, and hidden structure needed for visible forms.
- Use an explicit interconnection line only when absolutely necessary to keep the block readable.
- Too many lines here can blur the specific form that Stage 3 must develop.

## Early Stage 3

- Keep lines light while specific form is still being discovered.
- Develop anatomy and structure from the accepted block.
- Search for interconnection only where overlap has caused separation.
- Render a hidden or visible passage as fully as needed for the form to work.

## Later Stage 3

- When the form finally takes shape, begin hardening the lines known to be kept in Stage 4.
- Reduce, absorb, or reject the others.
- Develop contour and tone when the image requires them.
- Do not artificially stop short when an unresolved passage genuinely needs rendering.

## Stage 4

- Finalize the surviving line hierarchy, lighting, value, edge, texture, color, and finish.
- This is where the image receives its final magic.

The best compact statement is:

> Never over-render in the first three stages, but render what the image needs. Save final commitment and finish for Stage 4.

Experience is what teaches the artist the difference between “needed” and “merely available.”

# 13. The Stage 3 need test

The extracted cards must not turn “economy” into a ban on development.

Minimum does not mean incomplete.

A useful Stage 3 decision is:

```text
Does this passage already communicate the form?
    yes → preserve or simplify it
    no  → develop it until it does
```

Possible reasons for more Stage 3 rendering include:

- the hidden structure is required to place the visible anatomy;
- the joint still reads as severed;
- the overlap order is correct but the attachment is visually unclear;
- the contour needs more specific turning information;
- tone must replace a line that no longer belongs in the final surface;
- the intended style requires a more developed rough before final commitment.

The stage boundary is not a fixed mark count. It is a change in decision responsibility.

Stage 3 develops and proves the specific form. Stage 4 finalizes the proven image.

# 14. Teaching systems are guides, not art law

This was the decisive philosophical correction near the end of the review.

Hogarth’s lessons are not de facto rules.

Nothing in art is an “or else.”

Not every artist:

- constructs with Hogarth’s masses;
- traces Hogarth’s long interconnection routes;
- uses his contour emphasis;
- uses his tonal design;
- renders the same amount at each stage;
- aims for his anatomy, exaggeration, or finish.

The appropriate interpretation is:

> This method tends to solve this problem. Here is why it works, when it may help, and what it costs.

Future study will include:

- the rest of *Dynamic Figure Drawing*;
- *Dynamic Hands*;
- *Drawing the Human Head*;
- references across different bodies and poses;
- other artists and schools;
- practical render tests.

Those studies may produce valid methods that contradict Hogarth.

They should become variants, not failures.

The long-term purpose is not to make the assistant imitate Burne Hogarth. It is to teach the assistant to:

- recognize visual problems;
- understand why a solution works;
- compare alternative solutions;
- practice and accept correction;
- retain successful judgments;
- develop its own coherent visual style.

The user’s statement must remain central:

> We’re teaching you **your** style.

# 16. Out-of-chapter first-party teaching captured during the drift

The conversation wandered into broader production doctrine. This material should be retained, but it must not be cited as Chapter 3 source extraction.

## Global stage progression, local object priority

A scene advances globally:

```text
entire composition through Stage 1
→ entire composition through Stage 2
→ entire composition through Stage 3
→ entire composition through Stage 4
```

Within a stage, objects may be addressed in dependency and focal order. A soldier, weapon, wrecked ship, and environment can receive different local attention while remaining in the same global stage.

## Stage 2 as scene contract

Stage 2 should make the scene identifiable and structurally readable. Stage 3 is where silhouette, action, value, and lighting begin to pop together. A major late failure usually points back to a Stage 2 decision, though small local corrections remain normal.

## Rollback

Rollback begins at the smallest affected scope:

```text
failed Stage 3 passage
→ affected object’s Stage 2
→ direct dependencies
→ primary figure
→ whole composition only when necessary
```

Digital layers make rollback cheaper but do not remove the need for structural correction.

## Hidden construction

Map hidden structure in Stage 1. In Stage 2, develop as much as practiced visual judgment requires for visible forms, overlaps, attachments, and composition.

“Minimum sufficient” does not mean refusing to draw a hidden form. Sometimes the whole hidden part must be solved.

## Lighting workflow

- A broad mood may exist at Step 0.
- Stage 2 stages the actual lighting system structurally and may identify areas that will remain in shadow.
- Stage 3 builds lighting without allowing it to overpower form development; shading can absorb rejected lines.
- Stage 4 commits and finalizes.

The user commonly uses one- or two-point lighting. Hogarth’s grayscale plates should not override the chosen production setup.

## Color boundary

The user develops color in stages, but the doctrine belongs to future color-theory study. Do not infer a complete color workflow from Chapter 3.

## Atmosphere drift

Fog and smoke were discussed as volumetric obscurants whose edges and opacity can suppress visible form. That discussion was useful, but it moved beyond Chapter 3. Preserve only the limited connection:

> A line may disappear when another visual element carries the form.

Do not build a Chapter 3 atmosphere AP from this conversation.

# 18. Failure modes to preserve

## Source-boundary failures

- Treating lighting, atmosphere, rollback, or layers as if they were the chapter’s central doctrine.
- Reading a partially rendered Hogarth drawing as a complete production prescription.
- Silently correcting the source’s sculptural or arbitrary tone into physically based lighting.

## Overlap failures

- Showing every mass completely and expecting equal recession.
- Hiding so much that the visible fragment loses identity or attachment.
- Creating contradictory contour crossings.

## Interconnection failures

- Memorizing Hogarth’s routes as formulas.
- Inventing lines that do not correspond to anatomy or construction.
- Keeping teaching arrows or heavy routes in the finished image by default.
- Adding connections until the figure becomes busier and more segmented.

## Viewpoint failures

- Defining curve direction by page top and bottom.
- Reversing a curve because the canvas rotated rather than because the camera relation changed.
- Copying one view’s anatomical map into another view.

## Joint failures

- Treating the knee as a separate block between thigh and calf.
- Treating the deltoid as belonging only to the arm.
- Attaching the arm to a socket symbol without shoulder integration.
- Treating neck lines as unrelated straps around a cylinder.

## Contour failures

- Inventorying every bump.
- Assuming a connected form requires an unbroken line.
- Hardening a provisional Stage 3 line merely for visibility.
- Suppressing so much that the structure becomes unreadable.

## Tone failures

- Using value to hide unresolved construction.
- Shading every mass independently into beads.
- Darkening forms arbitrarily for depth in production.
- Treating Hogarth’s tonal plates as universal lighting law.

## Stage failures

- Over-rendering early because a tool is available.
- Under-rendering Stage 3 because “save it for Stage 4” was interpreted as a prohibition.
- Entering Stage 4 before the specific form is proven.
- Expecting final polish to create unity that construction, interconnection, contour, and value never established.

## Philosophy failures

- Turning guides into mandatory rules.
- Treating another artist’s valid solution as an error against Hogarth.
- Building the assistant into a Hogarth imitator instead of developing comparative judgment and an original style.
