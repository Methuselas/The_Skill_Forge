## Chapter 1’s role in the book

Chapter 1 supplies the basic **shape-mass vocabulary** for constructing a figure as solids in space:

- ovoid forms: balls, eggs, barrels;
- column forms: cylinders, cones, tapered limb members;
- spatulate forms: boxes, slabs, and wedges.

Its central correction is that an outline encloses a flat shape, while a drawing in depth must establish a three-dimensional mass with volume, orientation, thickness, and spatial relationship to adjacent masses.

Chapter 1 is primarily a **Stage 2 chapter**. It teaches what masses the framework can become and how those masses relate. It does not yet provide the complete ordered notation workflow; Chapter 2 does that. It also does not yet fully solve the segmented or “parts stuck together” appearance that can result from mass construction; Chapter 3 addresses that problem through overlap, interconnection, contour, and tone.

### Boundary retained from the first review

Do not extract a complete figure-drawing AP from Chapter 1 alone. Chapter 1 provides form vocabulary and local construction solutions. The ordered figure workflow begins in Chapter 2. The specialist onion-skinned drawing AP developed during this run is teacher-guided and cross-source; it is not a claim that Chapter 1 itself teaches the whole AP.

## The stage doctrine established during the Chapter 1 run

There is an internal Step 0 followed by four visible refinement stages.

### Step 0 — Visualize

Establish the intended subject, action, viewpoint, composition, silhouette, focal point, near/far order, and relevant design constraints. Step 0 may remain internal.

### Step 1 — Framework

Locate the action, axes, landmarks, joints, attachments, member paths, balance, and support. Searching lines and alternate paths may exist here. Step 1 locates the structure but does not substantially model volume.

### Step 2 — Mass construction

Convert the accepted framework into solid forms. Establish centerlines, cross-contours, turning planes, overlap, foreshortening, attachment, weight, support, and a readable near/far sequence.

**Critical exit condition:** Step 2 must settle one coherent structural solution. Competing limb positions, sockets, attachments, contact points, support relationships, and major overlaps cannot remain equally active when advancing to Step 3.

### Step 3 — Specific form

Develop the generic masses into the specific anatomy, species, character, costume, machine, vehicle, or object. Construction guides should no longer dominate. Step 3 develops the accepted Step 2 solution rather than deciding the basic pose late.

### Step 4 — Final

Select and correct lines; establish value, light, color, texture, atmosphere, and finish. Rendering clarifies the solved drawing and must not conceal unresolved construction.

### Backward test

A final image should reduce backward into a plausible Step 2 mass block, then into the same Step 1 framework, while preserving the Step 0 idea. A later stage that requires a different pose, viewpoint, attachment scheme, or silhouette fails continuity.

### Onion-skin continuity

Stages are successive states of one artifact, not separate images that merely resemble one another. For visible process work, each stage should be drawn or edited over the immediately preceding stage, as with tracing paper or a light board. Independent regeneration invites camera, pose, proportion, joint, and attachment drift.

This continuity principle came from first-party teaching and the image-generation experiments conducted during the Chapter 1 run. It must remain clearly distinguished from Hogarth’s source claims.

## Drawing is not diagramming

The most important production correction from this run was literal:

> When asked to draw, sketch, render, paint, illustrate, or design, create forms occupying pictorial space. Do not substitute an explanatory diagram for the drawing.

A working drawing must make the viewer perceive:

- depth and volume;
- orientation and turning planes;
- overlap and recession;
- weight and support;
- attachment and articulation;
- perspective and foreshortening.

Centerlines, cross-contours, visible planes, searching lines, and rough construction may all be appropriate in Stage 2 because they prove the form. Labels, arrows, panel borders, legends, flat color keys, isolated symbols, and clean iconography do not create depth by themselves.

An explanatory diagram may accompany a lesson, but it cannot replace the actual working drawing. The decisive question is:

> Could an artist continue this exact artifact into the next stage without discarding and rebuilding it?

If the answer is no, it is not a valid staged working reference.

## Chapter 1 construction knowledge to retain

## 1. Shape-mass before anatomy

The figure should first be understood as related volumes, not as a finished contour. The primitive families are flexible construction choices, not mandatory visible geometry and not a prescription for one artist’s mark-making style.

The construction must remain organic and subject-appropriate. Cylinders, wedges, scribbles, broad tonal masses, or other methods may all be valid when they solve the same spatial problem.

## 2. Head: cranial ball and facial wedge

- Build the head from a cranial ball and facial wedge.
- Their apparent dominance changes with viewpoint: the cranial mass dominates many top and rear views; the facial wedge gains prominence in lower views.
- Use centerlines and plane changes to prove turn, tilt, and elevation.
- Do not reduce the construction to a flat helmet symbol.
- The later *Drawing the Human Head* study should refine or replace details without losing this large-mass relationship.

## 3. Rib cage: barrel with oriented openings

- The rib cage is the largest primary mass.
- Treat it as a barrel or ovoid with an upper collarbone depression and lower diaphragm arch.
- The openings and centerline help establish whether the torso is front, back, up, down, or three-quarter.
- A torso turn without a readable centerline and oriented openings is not solved merely because two rounded shapes are present.

## 4. Neck seating

- The neck emerges from inside the upper chest opening.
- Its centerline must relate to the torso centerline.
- It must not perch on the surface, originate from a shoulder, or attach to an arbitrary exterior point.
- A neck may be intentionally long or short, but length variation does not excuse a broken socket relationship.

This correction came directly from reviewing failed generated head-direction trials and should remain a strong anatomy/construction gate.

## 5. Shoulder and deltoid integration

- The deltoid belongs to the arm but also changes the combined chest-and-shoulder mass.
- Arm elevation changes how the shoulder merges with or lifts away from the rib-cage barrel.
- The arm must attach through the shoulder complex rather than appearing pasted onto the outer torso.
- Detailed anatomy will later refine the transition, but the Stage 2 attachment must already be believable.

## 6. Chest landmarks

Hogarth’s 45-degree guides from the neck pit provide a useful baseline for locating nipple positions on an average rib-cage construction.

Retain the following qualifications:

- the guide is a starting control, not a universal law;
- age, body composition, muscle condition, gravity, posture, and individual variation alter the result;
- the form sits on and turns with the rib cage;
- in three-quarter and frontal views, the two breasts do not simply present as identical front-facing discs.

This card should be reconsidered after *Dynamic Anatomy* and any later material dealing with sex, age, and body variation.

## 7. Rib cage, pelvis, and flexible waist

- The rib cage is an ovoid/barrel; the pelvis is a wedge box.
- The pelvis is narrower at the top and broader at the base in Hogarth’s simplified construction.
- Rib cage and pelvis are counterpoised and joined through a flexible waist.
- The waist is not a rigid post and the torso is not two unrelated shapes stacked together.
- Each mass may turn, tip, and oppose the other while remaining one connected torso.
- Centerlines and cross-contours must make those independent directions readable.

## 8. Thigh-to-pelvis attachment

- The thighs enter the actual hip region of the pelvic wedge.
- Do not cut arbitrary sockets into the front or outer surface merely to make the legs fit.
- Construction lines that imply fractured pelvis geometry, duplicated sockets, or exterior holes are errors, not “extra detail.”

## 9. Rear pelvic “butterfly”

The rear butterfly is a conditional compound-mass reading, not a mandatory symbol that should be drawn on every pelvis.

Retain the teacher’s qualification:

- it is more visibly useful when the gluteal and hip masses are pronounced;
- it may be subdued or nearly absent in leaner frames;
- it must remain subordinate to the underlying pelvic wedge;
- do not force the butterfly when it breaks the pelvis or contradicts the individual body.

The old ectomorph/mesomorph/endomorph terminology was acknowledged as dated. Final review should decide whether the card can be expressed without relying on those categories.

## 10. Arms and legs as articulated tapered chains

- Arm and leg are each two-part articulated members with terminal hand or foot forms.
- Preserve the complete chain: parent mass → upper member → joint → lower member → terminal wedge.
- The shoulder/hip, elbow/knee, and wrist/ankle are functional attachment and direction changes, not decorative circles.
- A limb should not acquire a second origin, duplicate segment, arbitrary hole, or detached terminal form.
- Draw through hidden members when needed so that overlap does not erase the understood construction.

## 11. Underarm rhythm

- Locate the elbow and carry the major underarm rhythm from the rear armpit through the elbow toward the outer base of the palm.
- A bent arm presents two related curves meeting around the elbow.
- An extended arm can read as one controlled, tensioned curve.
- The elbow organizes the change but should not sever the arm into unrelated bulges.
- This is a large-form rhythm and later anatomy must refine rather than destroy it.

The teacher’s “Pen tool anchor” analogy is useful for remembering controlled continuity rather than a mathematically perfect curve.

## 12. S- and B-based leg rhythms

Retain the practical view families:

- **S rhythm:** commonly profile, many three-quarter, and seated orientations; the path reverses around the knee from the front thigh toward the rear calf.
- **B rhythm:** commonly front and rear orientations, including bent, crossed, hunched, and deeply projected frontal/rear views; the inner side acts as the straighter stem while the outer thigh and calf create the two lobes.

Qualifications:

- B and S are construction controls and diagnostic checks, not complete anatomy;
- the anklebones are clues to orientation, not the sole authority;
- strong foreshortening does not automatically change a front-facing leg into an S family;
- the inner B stem may curve or break with the actual shin and knee action.

These rhythms should be reassessed after later chapters address overlap, interconnection, size, and length.

## 13. Lower-leg and foot rotation

- A foot cannot swivel independently beneath an unchanged shin.
- When the foot turns, the ankle and lower-leg orientation must support that turn.
- In the front-view support pattern, the leg may thrust from the outside hip inward toward the inner ankle, then reverse outward through the foot stance.
- The foot is a support platform that must establish contact, pressure, and direction at Stage 2.

This correction arose from the failed outward-support-foot drawing and should remain a hard structural gate.

## 14. Hand wedge and digit chain

- The hand is a broad, flattened, active tool mass, thicker at the rear palm and tapering toward the fingers.
- Fingers are articulated chains emerging from the palm wedge; the thumb is an opposed spatulate member with a contrary plane.
- The hand should not be a generic mitten pasted to the wrist.
- Stage 2 should establish palm orientation, digit origin, spread, action, and foreshortening before anatomy and surface detail.
- *Drawing Dynamic Hands* will likely refine or replace many details while retaining the palm-wedge and articulated-chain logic.

## 15. Foot as a continuous support wedge

- The foot and hand are both wedge-like, but their functions and structures differ.
- The foot is a broad support mass with heel, forefoot/sole base, toes, and arch relationships.
- The arch is **not an empty mechanical cavity**. Bones, tendons, muscles, fat, and skin form one continuous volume.
- The inner side rises through the arch; the outer side gives a more continuous support path.
- The toes are compact traction and projection forms, not finger-like decorations.
- Contact with the ground, heel-to-forefoot weight path, and outward stance must be readable at Stage 2.

This soft-tissue correction must be preserved when the anatomy books add internal structure.

## Teacher-supplied diagnostics that require provenance separation

During review, the teacher introduced torso-envelope checks that were useful but were not extracted from Hogarth Chapter 1:

- an apex-down triangle from the inner shoulders through the chest landmarks toward the center-bottom pelvis;
- a more box-like envelope useful for some broader or more traditionally masculine torso designs;
- an apex-up triangle useful for some wider-hip or more traditionally feminine torso designs.

These are flexible projected diagnostics, not gender laws and not shapes that should remain visible after Stage 2. They can apply across cis, trans, stylized, and nonconforming designs according to the actual proportions being drawn.

They were deliberately held out of the Chapter 1 source extraction because the first-party teaching payload and provenance were not yet packaged correctly. During final review, either admit them with first-party grounding or leave them out. Never attribute them to Hogarth by accident.

## Failed-image corrections that must survive the final review

These corrections came from direct teacher review of failed generated construction images and may not be obvious from the final card wording:

1. **Neck/head direction tests:** the neck must remain seated in the torso opening and aligned to the torso centerline even while the head turns. A neck originating from the shoulder is a total structural failure.
2. **Outward support-foot test:** the foot turn requires compatible shin and ankle rotation; the ankle cannot swivel independently.
3. **Overlapping-limb test:** draw-through must preserve the real member count. A stray construction path that reads as a second arm is not harmless searching once the block is being accepted.
4. **Thigh/pelvis test:** thighs must enter the hip region. Arbitrary exterior sockets and fractured pelvic geometry invalidate the mass block.
5. **Torso-turn test:** separate masses require a readable centerline, oriented planes, and a continuous waist. Two forms on a post do not constitute a turning torso.
6. **Stage-reference test:** labels, flat fills, and clean primitive icons cannot compensate for missing depth.

## Workflow lessons discovered while testing the Chapter 1 cards

These were not all Chapter 1 source claims, but they shaped the drawing system built from the chapter:

- A Drill is a short repeatable exercise for training, diagnosis, exploration, correction, or warm-up before a larger piece.
- An AP is a staged production workflow that chains appropriate Patterns and may call targeted Drills when a difficult part exposes a weakness.
- The drawing AP must preserve stage refinement boundaries and onion-skin continuity without prescribing one mark-making style.
- “Construction” does not mean every artist must use clean cylinders. Scribbling, envelopes, tonal masses, angular planes, and other variants can solve the same stage requirements.
- A final-only request still invokes the hidden construction; the user need not see every stage.
- For ordinary human requests, visual design constraints such as clothing should be decided at Step 0 rather than left ambiguous.
- The Step 2 gate is especially important: Stage 3 should not be forced to decide the basic leg pose, support, or attachment while also developing anatomy.
- The same universal stage logic appeared transferable to a sea turtle and potentially to vehicles, but subject-specific APs may still be justified when the recurring structural problems differ.

## What must be reconsidered after Chapter 6

### Reconcile Chapter 1 with the later chapters

1. **Chapter 3 — unity and interconnection:** determine which Chapter 1 mass cards need overlap, interconnection-line, contour, or tone variants so they do not produce segmented “beads on a string.”
2. **Chapter 4 — controlling size:** revise limb, hand, and joint cards with constant-width reasoning, hidden-form inference, and joint interlock where supported.
3. **Chapter 5 — controlling length:** decide whether ellipse arcs, pivots, radii, isosceles triangles, and part-to-part checks become separate Patterns, variants, or AP steps.
4. **Chapter 6 — projection and phase sequence:** integrate whole-figure perspective, reversible projection, environmental grids, multiple-action exploration, and chin-led action without bloating the basic Stage 2 vocabulary.

### Reconsider object boundaries

- Should B and S rhythms remain separate reusable Patterns, become method variants, or serve mainly as diagnostics inside broader leg-construction cards?
- Should the rear butterfly remain a standalone Pattern or become a conditional variant of pelvic construction?
- Should chest-landmark placement remain a card after anatomy and body-variation study, or be narrowed substantially?
- Which hand and foot cards should be replaced, refined, or absorbed after the dedicated anatomy and hand books?
- Should neck seating and shoulder integration remain separate cards once Chapter 3 provides full interconnection logic?
- Which Chapter 1 Drills are true warm-ups, and which are better treated as validation checks inside an AP?

### Reconsider the drawing AP

- Preserve the universal stage spine while allowing variant mark-making methods.
- Confirm that Step 2 exit gates are complete for pose, support, member count, attachments, overlaps, and near/far order.
- Verify that visible staged work uses artifact continuity rather than independent generation.
- Verify that final-only work still passes the backward test.
- Decide whether one drawing AP is sufficient or whether figures, creatures, vehicles, environments, heads, and hands warrant specialist APs under a shared parent workflow.

### Audit provenance

Every retained claim must be identifiable as one of:

- Hogarth Chapter 1 source material;
- first-party teacher correction or diagnostic;
- lesson from a reviewed generation experiment;
- later-chapter refinement.

Do not let a teacher correction masquerade as a Hogarth claim, and do not let a generated experiment become anatomy authority merely because the image looked successful.

### Rebuild visual references only after the book-wide review

Chapter-specific visual references remain deferred until the foundational book is complete. Later chapters may change the correct Stage 2 or Stage 3 depiction of the same construction.

When references are rebuilt:

- make actual working drawings, not infographics;
- use one coherent subject in space;
- show the appropriate construction for the card’s stage;
- preserve depth, attachment, and continuation into the next stage;
- keep explanatory labels separate from the drawing when labels are useful;
- require human art review before `review: passed`;
- never infer acceptance from schema validation alone.

### Audit retrieval

Confirm that the drawing `SKILL.md` retrieves:

1. the universal construction foundation;
2. the specialist drawing AP;
3. the relevant Chapter 1 and later Patterns;
4. useful warm-up Drills;
5. only human-reviewed visual references.

The existence of an AP in the library does not prove that it was retrieved or executed.

## Final-review invariants

The following principles should survive unless later source study supplies strong evidence to replace them:

- Draw forms in space, not symbols for forms.
- Step 1 locates; Step 2 gives volume and decides structure; Step 3 specifies; Step 4 renders.
- Each later stage develops the accepted prior artifact.
- A final must pass the backward test.
- Every member attaches once through a believable chain.
- Centerlines, cross-contours, overlaps, and plane changes must agree with the chosen viewpoint.
- Geometry is useful when it clarifies construction and wrong when it fractures or duplicates it.
- Hands and feet are functional terminal masses, not afterthoughts.
- Rendering cannot rescue an unsolved block.
- Rejected visual references do not automatically invalidate correctly reviewed instructional logic.
- Mechanical validation never replaces expert artistic review.
