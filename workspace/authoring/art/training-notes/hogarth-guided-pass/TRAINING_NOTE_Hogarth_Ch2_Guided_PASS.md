# 4. Chapter 2 central doctrine

Chapter 2 teaches an ordered notation process for constructing the figure in deep space.

The default notational order is:

```text
torso → legs → arms → head
```

This order is not merely a hierarchy of importance. It is an order of structural dependency, comparable to the order of operations in mathematics.

The user’s rule:

> The notational order is absolute by default. Exceptions exist, but they are uncommon, case-specific, and must be justified.

Skipping ahead commits a secondary form before the governing structure exists.

The dependency logic is:

```text
torso establishes action and direction
→ pelvic wedge allows leg attachment and support
→ stabilized torso and legs give the arms a body to coordinate through
→ completed body action gives the head a valid range and narrative role
```

The recognizable image may survive an out-of-order process, but the internal relationships are much more likely to fail.

# 5. How the hierarchy behaves across the four drawing stages

## Stage 1 — Framework

Stage 1 uses the simplest possible elements to rough the skeleton of the image:

- oval for torso;
- oval for head;
- ovals for hands;
- lines for arms;
- lines for legs;
- line for neck;
- line for spine/action;
- box for hips;
- circles for shoulders;
- circles for elbows;
- circles for knees;
- wedges for feet.

Following the torso-rooted structure is useful and should be taught explicitly.

With practice, the artist may abbreviate many primitives into simple action lines because the forms are visualized internally without being drawn.

Important distinction:

> Stage 1 may abbreviate a form only when the artist can still see and control it mentally.

Abbreviation is not ignorance.

## Stage 2 — Mass construction

Stage 2 is where the hierarchy becomes imperative:

```text
torso → legs → arms → head
```

This is the first stage where the drawing truly takes form.

Stage 2 must land:

- action;
- balance;
- weight;
- support;
- attachment;
- spatial direction;
- major overlap;
- major perspective;
- terminal hand and foot structure.

Stage 2 is the decisive structural stage. Later stages should not be expected to rescue unresolved Stage 2 construction.

## Stage 3 — Specific form

The same hierarchy continues, but execution becomes looser because the major structure is already established.

Stage 3 develops:

- specific anatomy;
- specific contour;
- form transitions;
- individual character;
- surface clarification.

Stage 3 should not still be solving Stage 2 armature problems.

## Stage 4 — Final integration

Stage 4 may move around the image more freely.

The artist:

- rejects obsolete construction lines;
- integrates final contour;
- adds color when relevant;
- adds lighting and shadow;
- manages edges;
- unifies the image as a whole.

The strict construction order largely releases because the structure is already solved.

The user noted that color will later reveal similar underlying mechanics.

# 6. The action line becomes the torso centerline

The initial Stage 1 action line becomes the Stage 2 torso centerline.

Correct progression:

```text
Stage 1 action line
→ Stage 2 torso centerline
→ rib-cage barrel and pelvic wedge built around it
→ all later forms constrained by that flow
```

The torso masses are not independently invented and joined afterward.

The centerline is the figure’s **Wu Sao**.

It controls the direction of the action. Once established, later forms can only move in certain ways, directions, and curves without breaking the form.

The user’s analogy:

> The centerline is like the header for a code snippet. Without it, the code does not know where to go.

The figure is not assembled as unrelated expressive parts:

```text
torso action
+ arm action
+ leg action
+ head action
```

It is derived from one governing action:

```text
torso centerline
→ permitted torso masses
→ permitted leg bearing
→ permitted arm response
→ permitted head direction
```

Limbs may:

- continue the action;
- oppose it;
- redirect it;
- counterbalance it.

They may not contradict it without structural failure.

This becomes especially important later in comic art, where the action line must survive exaggeration.

# 7. Rare exceptions to the default hierarchy

The default order is repeated consistently because repetition prevents casual deviation.

The deeper rule is still mechanical dependency.

A rare exception may occur when the pose’s actual support mechanism changes the dependency order.

Example: a figure hanging from a pull-up bar.

Likely order:

```text
torso → arms → legs → head
```

The arms establish:

- suspension;
- gravity;
- shoulder tension;
- support;
- the torso’s relationship to the bar.

The exception is justified by actual mechanics, not convenience or stylistic preference.

Rule:

> Follow torso → legs → arms → head by default. Depart only when a specific pose has a clearly different primary support or force relationship.

Exceptions are uncommon and case-by-case.

# 8. Torso and pelvic wedge

The torso is the governing double mass:

- rib-cage barrel;
- pelvic wedge;
- flexible waist connection;
- governing centerline.

The pelvic wedge must be clarified before the legs receive their attachments.

The pelvic wedge remains structurally stable.

When the legs move away from the center pelvic mass, the visible Stage 3 hip and pelvic forms may appear to spread with them, but those forms still reside inside the Stage 2 box wedge.

What changes:

- thigh attachment separation;
- groin opening or compression;
- lower-belly relationship;
- hip contour;
- visible side planes;
- surrounding soft tissue.

What does not change arbitrarily:

- the underlying structural pelvis;
- the continuity of the Stage 2 wedge.

The Stage 3 pelvis must remain traceable back to the Stage 2 box.

The lower-belly insert begins as part of torso construction but becomes fully readable as the legs attach and compress around the pelvic basin.

# 9. Legs: B- and S-curves

Hogarth uses two main leg rhythms:

- **B-shape:** frontal and rear orientations;
- **S-line:** side-oriented legs.

The anklebones help determine which orientation governs.

These curves are:

1. Stage 2 construction controls;
2. later diagnostic tools.

Process:

```text
determine leg orientation
→ establish B- or S-rhythm
→ build thigh, knee, lower leg, ankle, and foot around it
→ verify continuity
```

With experience, the artist sees the B- or S-rhythm without drawing it.

When a leg looks:

- stiff;
- segmented;
- unnatural;
- assembled from separate cylinders;
- inconsistent;

the artist can redraw or mentally reassert the controlling B- or S-curve.

# 10. Feet and hands: Stage 2 truth test

The outward foot thrust shown in Chapter 2 is not a universal literal foot angle.

The underlying rule is force transfer.

The foot must convincingly:

- receive force from the leg;
- redirect that force;
- support the body;
- express traction, push, or stance;
- remain attached to the lower leg.

The visible foot angle may vary for:

- inward turn;
- crossed stance;
- dance;
- toe push-off;
- side view;
- other contextual poses.

The user’s practical diagnostic:

> If hands or feet do not look right in Stage 2, they are probably wrong.

Stage 2 must already establish:

- terminal wedge or major mass;
- attachment to the limb;
- direction of force;
- support or action;
- relationship to surrounding forms.

Stage 3 can specify anatomy. Stage 4 can polish it. Neither should rescue a structurally unresolved hand or foot.

This becomes a general anti-avoidance rule:

- do not hide hands behind the back to evade construction;
- do not bury feet in fog, grass, shadow, or unjustified crops;
- do not replace unresolved fingers with a mitten;
- do not let terminal forms silently disappear.

Legitimate occlusion, cropping, simplification, or concealment must have a compositional reason.

# 11. Arms and the yoke

The arms are constructed as a mechanically linked pair through the torso.

Front view:

- collarbone yoke.

Rear view:

- inverted shoulder/trapezius yoke.

The yoke is literal during Stage 2 because the arms, though independently mobile, remain restrained and powered by the shoulder girdle and connected musculature.

The deeper anatomical explanation belongs to the later book *Dynamic Anatomy*.

During learning or difficult construction:

- draw the yoke explicitly;
- connect both arms through the torso;
- solve hidden origins transparently;
- verify arm correlation and shoulder attachment.

With experience:

- the artist can visualize the yoke without drawing it.

Critical Stage boundary:

> If the artist still needs the yoke after Stage 2, Stage 2 failed.

This applies to both front and rear yokes.

The yoke should not survive into Stage 3 as an unresolved construction dependency.

Stage 3 develops specific shoulder, chest, back, and arm anatomy from an already stable Stage 2 solution.

# 12. Transparent construction and hidden arm paths

During Stage 2, overlapping or obscured arms should be understood through their complete paths and origins.

Transparent construction means:

- solving the hidden attachment;
- understanding the covered member;
- building through the obstruction;
- then deciding which lines remain visible.

It does **not** mean carrying every hidden cylinder visibly into the final drawing.

By Stage 3:

- hidden construction should yield to coherent overlap;
- the attachment should already be secure;
- visible contour should prove the unseen form continues logically.

# 13. The head and neck are always last to be resolved

A provisional head oval may appear in Stage 1 for proportion and placement.

The head and neck are still always the final forms structurally resolved.

Correct dependency:

```text
established body action
→ available neck attachment
→ physically permitted head positions
→ contextually correct final head choice
```

The head and neck have a limited range of movement and rotation.

A common failure is to choose a desired head position first, then stretch, detach, or distort the neck to force it into place.

Incorrect dependency:

```text
desired head direction
→ neck stretched to make it fit
```

The Chapter 1 crouching Codex failure demonstrated this clearly:

- the body had established a compressed, forward-driving action;
- the trial heads behaved like independently steerable forms;
- the neck was treated as extensible;
- the head choices did not resolve from the torso’s available structure.

Rule:

> Head and neck are ALWAYS last to be resolved.

## Choosing the final head direction

After physically impossible choices are rejected, the final choice is contextual.

The artist asks:

```text
What must the character look at, react to, or do?
→ Which head direction communicates that?
→ Can the established neck and torso physically achieve it?
```

Examples:

- a subject addressing the viewer should orient toward the camera;
- a three-quarter figure examining an object should look at that object;
- Superman hitting Doomsday should engage the target unless the narrative specifically requires otherwise.

Composition cannot override anatomy. Anatomy alone does not choose the composition.

# 14. Exercises in notation: experimentation, not timidity

The Chapter 2 invention exercise asks the student to work without:

- model;
- photograph;
- illustrated visual aid;
- external reference.

This is a Drill condition, not a universal production rule.

Purpose:

- test internal visualization;
- expose what has actually been retained;
- challenge passive or “art-schoolish” poses;
- encourage force, extension, thrust, and energy;
- reduce fear of invention.

“Free and open” extremities are specific to this experimentation Drill.

They are not a universal compositional law.

A compressed, quiet, frightened, exhausted, restrained, seated, or closed pose may be correct when the context requires it.

The user’s correction:

> Do not be afraid to experiment.

Proper reference balance:

- study the method and examples;
- close the references;
- perform the notation from internal visualization;
- reopen references when a weakness is exposed;
- study the correction;
- repeat without looking.

Production work may use subject and construction references whenever they improve accuracy, unless memory invention is itself the assignment.

# 15. Notation as search, editing, compression, and discovery

Notation is not only a preliminary drawing.

It is a controlled search process used to:

- discover action;
- compare alternatives;
- combine phases;
- reject weaker phases;
- compress a sequence;
- strengthen rhythm;
- test limb arrangements;
- preserve energy;
- expose a flat or stale first idea.

## Printed page 61 / scan page 62

The five-step action sequence is a mixture of Stage 2 and Stage 3, leaning toward Stage 2.

Its purpose is to show movement rhythm.

In the lower sequence:

- frames 1 and 3 are combined;
- frame 2 is removed;
- frames 4 and 5 remain;
- the action and energy survive;
- fewer drawings are required.

The hierarchy is still assumed inside the compressed figures.

## Printed page 63 / scan page 64

Hogarth uses rougher, smaller alternatives to align the desired arms and legs.

Once one succeeds:

- the others are rejected;
- the chosen notation is developed toward a nearly Stage 4 result;
- light and shadow may be present;
- color is not required.

## Printed page 64 / scan page 65

A continuous sequence of notation sketches can reveal that the primary sketch is:

- wrong;
- flat;
- stale;
- less energetic;
- structurally weaker than a later alternative.

A later sketch may become the actual solution.

Important distinction:

> The experiment may change the selected action solution, but it does not abandon the hierarchy used to construct each notation.

# 16. Animation / phase-sequence workflow

For a multi-cell sequence, the user’s preferred workflow is stage-by-stage across the whole sequence.

Process:

```text
Stage 1:
cell 1 → cell 2 → cell 3 → ...

Stage 2:
cell 1 → cell 2 → cell 3 → ...

Stage 3:
cell 1 → cell 2 → cell 3 → ...

Stage 4:
cell 1 → cell 2 → cell 3 → ...
```

Each cell is handled individually.

Inside each cell, the figure follows the proper construction hierarchy.

The artist completes all Stage 1 cells before moving to Stage 2, all Stage 2 cells before Stage 3, and so on.

Benefits:

- preserves continuity;
- protects timing and spacing;
- allows sequence-level correction before overcommitment;
- keeps every drawing at the same maturity level;
- prevents a single polished cell from dictating unresolved neighboring cells.

Constructing every torso, then every set of legs, then every arm pair across the whole sequence may be used as a specialized check, but it is not required.

# 17. Chapter 2’s boundary with Chapter 3

Printed page 64 shows the “lumpy,” segmented failure produced by strong depth recession.

The problem:

- forms bulge and compress;
- forms appear detached;
- the figure becomes an aggregate of parts;
- continuity is lost.

Chapter 2 diagnoses the problem.

Chapter 3 teaches the remedy through:

- overlapping;
- interconnection lines;
- contour control;
- tone gradation;
- form flow;
- form unity.

Correct provisional boundary:

## For a Chapter 2 Drill

A result may pass provisionally when:

- the ordered notation is correct;
- the action is clear;
- the intended depth direction is understandable;
- the hierarchy has been followed;

even when the forms still show some segmented/lumpy quality.

The Drill should:

- tag the failure;
- name it;
- defer the remedy to Chapter 3;
- not pretend Chapter 2 already solved it.

## For the complete drawing AP after Chapter 3 is integrated

A segmented or detached Stage 2 figure must not pass.

Once the full system knows the remedy, the complete AP should reject physically disconnected or discontinuous forms at the Stage 2 exit.

# 21. Failure modes to preserve

The final system must continue to recognize these likely failures:

## Order failures

- head committed before torso;
- arms committed before support;
- legs attached before pelvic wedge is clarified;
- jumping around Stage 2 without a justified mechanical exception.

## Centerline failures

- torso masses contradict the action line;
- limbs behave as if they belong to a different action;
- centerline is inserted afterward as decoration rather than governance.

## Pelvis/leg failures

- thigh floats beside the pelvis;
- pelvic wedge changes arbitrarily;
- legs become separate cylinders with no rhythm;
- B- or S-control is absent when the leg looks unnatural.

## Hand/foot failures

- terminal form looks wrong in Stage 2;
- foot does not receive leg force;
- hand floats from wrist;
- fingers fuse, multiply, or disappear;
- difficult forms are hidden without compositional reason.

## Arm/yoke failures

- arms treated as unrelated limbs;
- shoulder attachments do not answer each other;
- yoke still needed in Stage 3;
- rear yoke or hidden origins never solved.

## Head/neck failures

- neck stretched to fit a desired gaze;
- head acts independently of torso;
- head direction contradicts narrative context;
- head resolved too early.

## Notation failures

- timid, repetitive poses during invention Drill;
- treating the first sketch as sacred;
- polishing before comparison;
- changing a pose merely to escape a difficult construction;
- compressing a sequence until energy or readability disappears.

## Stage-boundary failures

- expecting Stage 3 detail to fix Stage 2 structure;
- carrying construction armatures too far;
- passing a segmented final drawing after Chapter 3 knowledge exists.
