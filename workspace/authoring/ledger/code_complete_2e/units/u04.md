## Teaching-lane scan

`teaching_candidate_count: 0`. The scan was run independently of the skill scan
and found no candidate, which is the honest result for this chapter rather than a
scan that was skipped.

One candidate was considered seriously and rejected on a specific ground, and the
reasoning is recorded because the same shape will recur. The C++-written-as-Fortran
passage (p. 63) describes negative transfer — a learner applying a prior skill's
schema and producing output that is valid in the new medium but idiomatically
wrong. Negative transfer is genuinely cross-domain; it applies to second languages,
instruments, and prose as readily as to programming, and `teaching_route` would
therefore point at `library/teaching/` rather than at this domain.

It is nonetheless not a Teaching-lane candidate, because the passage contains **no
instructional decision**. McConnell reports the phenomenon as evidence for a claim
about language and thought. He does not say how to detect it in a learner, when to
intervene, what to substitute, or how to sequence around it. A Teaching object
extracted from it would have to supply the instructional procedure from somewhere
other than the source, which is the derivation problem rather than extraction, and
its locator would point at a page that does not support it.

The operative test used here, and worth reusing: **a Teaching-lane candidate needs
an instructional decision in the source, not merely a learning phenomenon the
source observes.** A phenomenon supports a self-directed skill card — which is what
`PAT_write_the_language_you_are_in_not_the_one_you_know` is — and becomes teaching
material only when a source states what to do about it in someone else.

The remaining nine candidates were checked against the scan's list — sequencing,
scaffolding, exercise progression, variable isolation, demonstration, feedback,
correction, misconception diagnosis, assessment, scheduling, difficulty scaling,
exemplar comparison, transfer, fluency, simplification, repetition, advancement —
and none touches it. This chapter is about selecting tools and conventions before
construction. It has no pedagogical content.

## Notes

Ten candidates, four kept — three new objects and one variant. The kept set is
unusually coherent: three of the four are about the relationship between what you
intend and what the language will let you say, approached from three directions.
One says compensate when the language lacks the construct; one says check you are
not ignoring constructs it has; one says calibrate how much compensating to expect
from where the toolchain sits on its maturity curve.

Two of the six rejects are dated rather than wrong. The language descriptions and
the Table 4-1 ratios were accurate in 2004 and would now mislead if shipped as
cards — Visual Basic as the most widely used language, Web programming as the
late-wave example. Extracting the *shape* of the wave argument while rejecting its
instances is the right split, and the wave card is written so that its examples
stay in the Notes as McConnell's own dated illustrations rather than as content.

**Contract v3 note.** The Teaching-lane scan cost little on this chapter and
produced a clean zero, but the near-miss was informative and the test it produced —
instructional decision versus observed phenomenon — is recorded above rather than
left implicit. Expect the same question at ch. 21 (Collaborative Construction) and
ch. 32 (Self-Documenting Code), where the source does give instructional procedure
and the answer will likely differ.
