## Notes

Placement split three ways by what each object is for.
`PAT_diagnose_a_code_smell_by_the_cognitive_process_it_breaks` went to
`foundations/code-quality`, beside `PAT_evaluate_code_against_quality_goals`,
because it is a judgement about code structure.
`PAT_detect_linguistic_antipatterns_in_names` went to `foundations/readability`
with the u08 naming material. `DRILL_rate_code_with_the_paas_scale` went to
`foundations/code-comprehension`, since what it trains is noticing your own load
while reading.

The significant collision was `PAT_match_caller_mental_model` (gcbc u01), which
covers behaviour that steps outside what names and types advertise. That is the
same phenomenon as a linguistic antipattern seen from the other end. It was left
alone and cross-linked rather than absorbed, because the decisions differ: the
gcbc pattern is a design-time constraint on what you may make code do, while this
chapter supplies a review-time search procedure with named categories and measured
base rates. Merging them would have produced a card that is neither.

The variant onto `PAT_treat_bad_names_as_a_defect_search_heuristic` is unusual in
that it is better evidenced than the foundation it hangs from. That foundation
came from u08 at `confidence: medium` because Butler's naming-to-defect
association resists a causal reading; Khomh's smell-to-defect result is stronger
and adds change proneness. The foundation was not upgraded — the variant records
where the firmer evidence sits, and the decision they share is the same one.

Note on Fakhoury's structural result. Participants disliked badly formatted code
and said so, and the instruments found no significant load increase from it, while
the same instruments did find one for linguistic antipatterns. That gap between
complaint and measurement is the actionable content, and it is why the new naming
pattern leads with prioritisation rather than with detection.
